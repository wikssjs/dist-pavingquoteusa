Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, driveway paving is usually straightforward, but homeowners should still verify credentials and permissions before excavation begins. If the total value of the work exceeds $500, the paving contractor typically must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the PA Attorney General. You can confirm registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be especially cautious of “storm chasers”—contractors who solicit door-to-door after heavy rain or snow events—because they must have a verifiable, current HIC registration in Pennsylvania.

Permits: Most residential driveway paving typically doesn’t require a building permit in many PA municipalities, but the driveway approach (where your driveway meets a public road) may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. This is a “check before you dig” item; the wrong assumption can delay your start date and inspection.

Runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater rules (Chapter 102) require that paving projects don’t increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A professional crew will verify grading and may add drainage solutions so water flows where it should.

Workers’ compensation: confirm coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose homeowners to liability if someone is injured on-site.

Step-by-step, a Conewago homeowner should: (1) verify the HIC registration online, (2) request a certificate of insurance (general liability) and confirm it matches the company name and address, (3) confirm workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov, and (4) ask whether a driveway access permit is required for your road frontage before any base is removed.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Conewago driveway?

For Conewago homeowners, the Pennsylvania freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor. Both asphalt and concrete expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently under repeated freeze-thaw stress. Asphalt is more flexible, which helps it handle minor movement from the underlying soil and base, but it needs protective maintenance—especially sealcoating—typically every 2–3 years to defend against oxidation and water intrusion. Concrete is rigid; it can perform extremely well when properly built and drained, but it’s more prone to spalling, chipping, and cracking patterns when road salts and repeated freeze-thaw cycles stress the slab, joints, and edges.

With about 40.6% of homes in the area built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many existing driveways are already near or beyond typical service life, meaning resurfacing may not be the best value if the base is moving. In many of these cases, asphalt replacement or overlay decisions come down to whether the base can be stabilized. If the base is solid, resurfacing can keep costs lower—often in the $2,000–$7,000 band. If the base is failing, full replacement becomes the more reliable long-term fix, often starting in the $4,500–$13,000 range.

Concrete can be justified when you want low daily maintenance and plan to address drainage and edge detailing upfront. A practical example: if your driveway is already heaving and you’re staring at asphalt replacement pricing, a concrete quote that’s only slightly higher may be reasonable. But if concrete is placed over a poor base, it won’t “solve” the underlying freeze-thaw movement—both materials will struggle.

How to choose a driveway paving contractor in Conewago

Choosing the right contractor in Conewago is mostly about verifying credentials and matching the quoted scope to the real condition of your driveway. First, check IICRC certification and any Pennsylvania licensing requirements: ask for the contractor’s documentation and confirm it through the relevant online registry for the certification/license they claim. If they won’t provide details, or they can’t point you to where it’s listed online, that’s a red flag. Also confirm Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration when the job exceeds $500 through hic.attorneygeneral.gov.

Next, insist on 2–3 itemized written quotes—not a single lump sum—so you can compare labor and materials line-by-line (base excavation, stone type and depth, asphalt thickness, tack coat, compacting, sealcoating/edge details, and disposal). Read the scope for what’s excluded: for example, does it include removal of old asphalt, replacement of failing base, drainage corrections, or curbing/apron work at the street tie-in?

Warranty matters. Ask for the workmanship warranty length in writing, plus any equipment/material warranties. For payment, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back a portion until completion and final cleanup. Finally, require a timeline: an arrival estimate and a completion window in writing—so you know whether you’re booking peak-season work that can push start dates.

Does homeowner's insurance cover driveway damage in Conewago?

Sometimes, but it depends on the cause of the damage. In Conewago, homeowners often assume that “sudden damage” from a storm will be covered, while gradual deterioration from years of freeze–thaw and base wear usually isn’t. If your driveway was damaged by something like a fallen tree, a vehicle impact, or a specific insured peril, coverage may apply after you file a claim and your insurer reviews documentation and photos. However, repairs from structural settlement or base failure—common when older driveways (in the 40.6% pre-1980 housing range) have lost fines and support over time—are typically treated as maintenance or replacement. If your asphalt is failing and you’re debating resurfacing around the $2,000–$7,000 band versus full replacement near the $4,500–$13,000 range, get a written paving assessment first so you can distinguish “storm damage” from “wear and structural movement.”

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

Thickness should match the condition of the existing base and your intended design. In York County, a common approach is 3–4 inches for full-depth asphalt replacement when the base needs to be rebuilt or when movement is present, because Pennsylvania freeze–thaw can amplify weak support. For overlays, contractors typically aim for the “right thickness” over a structurally sound base (often around the 2–2.5 inch range for a basic overlay plan), but the final number depends on what’s removed, how much grading is needed, and whether there are drainage issues. The contractor should explain the asphalt lift(s) and how they’ll compact each layer. If you’re being quoted only for resurfacing pricing (often $2,000–$7,000), ask whether the base will be assessed and whether thickness changes if cracks are more than cosmetic. Proper base prep is what prevents early failures—not just more pavement on top.

How long does driveway paving take in Conewago?

Most residential asphalt projects in Conewago can be completed in a day or two, but the timeline depends on prep and access. A crack-filling and patching job can be done quickly, while a full replacement requires removal, base excavation/stone placement, grading, compaction, and then asphalt placement plus edge finishing. Weather is a key factor in Pennsylvania: rain or a thawed base can force delays to protect the new asphalt and achieve proper compaction. In busy peak season, top-tier crews may take several weeks to schedule, but that’s different from actual on-site duration. A resurfacing job over a sound base usually finishes faster because the contractor isn’t tearing everything out. To avoid surprises, ask the contractor for a written start date, a completion window, and a weather-related contingency plan—so you understand when work pauses and when asphalt can be installed.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Conewago?

Failing base support typically shows up as cracking patterns that “move” and surface depressions that don’t correct themselves after rain. Look for alligator cracking, edge breakdown near grass lines, repeating potholes in the same spots, and areas that look slightly raised or heaved—these often indicate freeze–thaw pumping and loss of fines beneath the pavement. Ponding after rainfall is another major clue: if water sits because the slope is off, freeze–thaw will keep stressing the base. You may also notice that patchwork never seems to last, or repairs reappear shortly after sealing—because the asphalt is responding to movement below it. If you see these signs, sealcoating is usually not enough. In those cases, the long-term answer is commonly resurfacing only if the base is truly sound, or full replacement when the base has failed—moving you toward the $4,500–$13,000 range rather than an overlay near $2,000–$7,000.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Conewago's climate?

In Conewago and the broader York County area, a properly constructed new asphalt driveway typically lasts about 20–30 years, provided the base is built correctly and drainage issues are addressed. The climate matters because repeated freeze–thaw cycles can accelerate failure when water is allowed to sit or when the base is underbuilt or contaminated with fines. Maintenance also plays a role: sealcoating every 2–3 years helps protect the surface and slow oxidation, often extending service life by a few years per coat. If you notice movement, heaving, or alligator cracking, waiting for sealcoating to “catch up” can be more expensive later because the asphalt is reflecting a base problem. The best way to get a realistic lifespan estimate is to have a contractor assess base condition and asphalt thickness plans, not just the driveway’s appearance.
