Where the water goes

Water is behind just about every driveway failure I see in Sewickley. It's not a finishing detail—grading is essential from the stone up. To survive Southwestern Pennsylvania's freeze-thaw cycles, a driveway needs a minimum slope of about 2% (a quarter inch per foot) to shed water, whether that's a slight crown so water runs to both sides, or a cross-slope to one edge. If your driveway slopes toward the house or garage, water will collect at the joint and work into the foundation or base, especially given the dense clay subsoil common around the Greensburg area. In those cases, a trench or channel drain at the garage entry is often the only solution if you can't regrade the slope away. That water has to go somewhere: daylighting the drain to a lawn or swale is best, but a dry well may be needed if you have nowhere to send it downhill.

Ponded water on clay soils lingers and infiltrates the stone base. When a hard freeze comes, that trapped water expands, breaking up even a heavy driveway from below. Every hump, dip, and crack starts as water in the wrong place. And in a hilly neighborhood, be mindful: you cannot legally redirect runoff onto your neighbor’s property, regardless of what the permit covers. If a contractor ignores this and causes runoff to flow onto someone else’s yard, you, not them, deal with the fallout. Good drainage and grading on day one cost less than years of patching and complaints.

What you need before paving in Sewickley

For most Sewickley homeowners, if you’re just repaving your driveway in the same spot and not expanding or changing how water runs, a full building permit often isn’t needed—this holds especially true in places like Hempfield Township. But if you’re replacing or widening a driveway in Greensburg, and any work impacts a curb, sidewalk, or drainage pattern, expect to need a zoning or building permit first. Importantly, the point where your driveway meets a state roadway (the approach) is governed by PennDOT District 12, not the local municipality—this catches many people off guard. Always check if an approach permit is required before beginning work near the road edge.

If expanding your driveway, know that adding impervious surface can trigger stormwater requirements. And no permit or inspection will protect you from neighbor disputes if your runoff creates a problem—redirecting water onto another property is a liability even if the township says yes.

Finally, Pennsylvania requires that anyone contracting for over $500 in home improvements (including paving) is registered with the Attorney General’s HIC system—check at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Every crew must carry workers' compensation insurance and you can verify it using WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. Get these in writing before any payment changes hands.

The driveway scams that work in Sewickley

Paving draws out fly-by-night crews because the job is both visible and quick—by the time real problems show up, they’ve moved on to the next county. Sewickley and the broader Westmoreland County area, with its patchwork of suburban and rural properties, provides ground for these operators. Out here, ‘leftover asphalt from a job down the road’ is the oldest pitch in the book. They’ll push ‘today only’ pricing, show up in an unmarked truck (usually with out-of-county plates), and ask for cash up front. The verbal-only quote is standard, as is the promise of a 'thick' coat of sealant—which turns out to be a thin, oily spray that lasts only months.

Don't fall for it. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract for any paving work over $500, with a mandatory three-day right to cancel (no questions asked). Contractors can’t waive your insurance deductible. If someone presses for an immediate decision or won’t put the scope in writing, they’re banking on being untraceable by the time you have a complaint—and in spread-out areas like Unity or Penn Townships, that’s often the case.

Five-minute checks defeat these scams: verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, ask for proof of workers’ comp from WCAIS, refuse all-cash deals, and never sign without a written contract stating exactly what you are getting. If you can’t verify the company and scope, keep your money in your pocket.

Reading a driveway proposal

Comparing driveway quotes in Sewickley means looking well beyond the bottom line. Demand a proposal that states the actual square footage to be paved, the depth of excavation, disposal details, what type and thickness of stone base will be installed, and how it will be compacted (in lifts, not all at once). Asphalt or concrete layers should specify compacted finished thickness, not just how thick it’s laid out—hot mix compresses. Ask about edge treatments (especially important on driveways with clay subgrade or rural edges), how grading and water flow will be handled, and what restoring the yard edge includes. Payment terms should be spelled out, with a minimal deposit and the rest due only after satisfactory completion. Warranties rarely cover cracking or settlement, so get the details in writing; most cover only severe surface raveling or potholes, if anything.

A one-line proposal—“Pave driveway $5,000”—with no details can’t be compared to anything and is the single biggest way homeowners here end up paying for less than they expect.

How to tell if the base is gone

In Sewickley, knowing whether you need a full replacement or just repairs comes down to base condition. Here’s what you can check on your own before calling contractors. Wide areas of alligator cracking—those patchwork, interconnected cracks—mean the stone base has failed, usually from water working down and then freezing. Isolated, straight cracks usually result from surface aging, not deep failure. If you see dips ("birdbaths") that hold water after rain, your subgrade has moved, often from clay swelling or freeze-thaw. Crumbling edges mean the driveway was left unsupported—common on older, narrow drives. Potholes are a sign that water has gotten into the base, eroding and softening it.

An overlay on top of these deeper failures is simply money burned—without fixing the base, new asphalt will crack or sink within a few seasons. If a contractor doesn't probe the base or only inspects from the surface, they haven’t looked at the real problem, and you won’t get what you need.

What to put in a Sewickley paving contract

The only way to protect yourself when paving in Sewickley is to require actual specification details in writing; otherwise, you’re only price-shopping, not buying what matters. A solid quote should state how deep the existing material will be dug out, what size and type of aggregate base will be installed, at what minimum depth (stone thickness is critical on clay and shale soils). Specify that compaction will happen in layers ("lifts"), not just at the top, and that asphalt depth is quoted as finished compacted thickness, not loose material, since it compresses nearly 20% after rolling. State the mix used for both base (binder) and top (surface) courses—local plants in Hempfield supply Superpave and ID-2 mixes. Clarify how edges will be built up or supported.

"We’ll put down three inches" means nothing unless you know if it’s compacted thickness, if it includes base, or just the top. In Sewickley’s climate, thin paving is a waste—it can’t handle snowplowing or heaving. Here’s what to require:

  • Excavation depth (in inches, minimum 8–12" for full replacement on clay)
  • Aggregate base type (2A modified) and compacted depth (minimum 6–8")
  • Compaction method and number of lifts (each lift ≤ 4")
  • Asphalt binder course: mix type and compacted thickness (1.5–2")
  • Asphalt surface course: mix type and compacted thickness (1–1.5")
  • Grading: finished slope and water direction stated
  • Edge treatment: supported or with curbing for rural or sloped sites
  • Cleanup: details on yard, walk, and street restoration
  • Warranty terms: what is actually covered, for how long
  • Payment schedule: modest deposit, balance upon job completion and acceptance

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Sewickley

What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating serve different roles for Sewickley driveways. Crack filling targets individual cracks—especially those under a quarter inch wide—and seals them with a flexible material to keep water from entering and causing base damage. It’s a spot repair, usually costing from $200–$800 for the average job, and focuses on structural preservation. Sealcoating, on the other hand, involves applying a thin surface layer over the entire driveway, protecting it from UV rays, weather, and minor spills. Sealcoating costs somewhat less, often $200–$700 per application. Sealcoating gives a uniform look and extends surface life, but it won’t stop structural cracks or fix base problems. In Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw climate, crack filling prevents water from getting into the base and causing real damage, while sealcoating slows oxidation and minor wear. Combining both at the right intervals is the best routine maintenance for most Sewickley driveways.
What does compaction in lifts mean? Compaction in lifts means the stone base and asphalt layers of a Sewickley driveway are installed in several thin layers (“lifts”), each compressed by heavy rollers before the next is applied. For example, rather than dumping eight inches of stone and rolling it all at once, you’d spread four inches, compact it, then add more. This prevents air pockets, ensures uniform density, and gives strength from the bottom up—vital on Sewickley’s clay and shale soils. The same logic applies to asphalt: a typical replacement here uses a two-inch compacted binder course and a 1–1.5-inch compacted surface course. If compaction is skipped or only done at the very top, your driveway will rut, settle, or crack early, especially after our winter freeze-thaw. Done right, this detail is what lets a driveway last 20–30 years versus falling apart in half the time—even though it won’t show the day of the work, it’s included in every solid proposal, even if the price is at the higher end of $3,400–$8,700.
Does Sewickley have local asphalt plants? Yes, Sewickley is served by local asphalt plants, particularly from the Hempfield Township area in Westmoreland County. This matters because having plants nearby limits haul distance, ensuring that hot mix asphalt arrives at your driveway at the right temperature for proper compaction and finish. In more rural parts of Westmoreland, like Unity Township, longer distances from the Greensburg-area quarries and plants can factor into price—delivery times mean the material can cool, impacting the resulting pavement durability. So for Sewickley and most surrounding towns, access to close-by plants is a real advantage and helps keep service prices in the typical $3,400–$8,700 range for a full replacement. Always ask your contractor which plant they use and be wary if it sounds unusually far away.
Why are my driveway edges crumbling? Crumbling driveway edges are a frequent problem in Sewickley due to inadequate support at the perimeter during the original installation. In this part of Pennsylvania, driveways are often built with exposed stone base but little or no built-up edge, especially on rural or older homes. The edge takes the brunt of weight from tires and suffers the most runoff erosion—if the soil underneath is clay, it can also soften and shift after snowmelt or rain. Once the edge breaks, water and freeze-thaw cycles widen the damage. Fixes start around $400–$1,500 for proper edge repair, or you might consider installing curb or block edging for a longer-term solution. If allowed to grow, edge crumbling invites deeper base failures requiring a full replacement in the $3,400–$8,700 range, so it pays to address it early.
Asphalt or concrete for a Sewickley driveway? Both asphalt and concrete are common choices in Sewickley. Asphalt is less expensive up front—$3,400–$8,700 for full replacement—cures faster, is flexible enough to handle freeze-thaw, and repairs are cheaper. Concrete costs more initially—typically $4,800–$11,500—but can last 30–50 years with minimal maintenance if poured thick enough on a solid base. On clay subgrades here, both require proper thickness and sub-base prep or you’ll see heaving and cracks. Asphalt is more forgiving of minor settlement and easier to patch, but needs routine sealcoating every 2–4 years. Concrete can hold up to plows and heavy loads, but repairs, if needed, stand out. If you want minimum fuss over decades, concrete is the pick—but only if you’re willing to pay more up front and accept the look. If you plan to move soon or need to control costs, properly built asphalt lasts decades.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in Sewickley usually costs less per square foot than a full start-over, but not by much. Contractors still need to excavate, add base, and join new paving to the old edge to prevent separation. Most projects land between $1,450–$4,400 for asphalt overlay (if your base and existing drive are sound) or $3,400–$8,700 if new full-depth work is needed, especially to tie into the old driveway properly. Costs run higher if you also need to change grading or add drainage features to prevent water from reaching the house. In Greensburg and other parts of Westmoreland County, permitting may be required if you’re altering a curb, sidewalk, or drainage, which can add to the cost and timeline.
What does sealcoating cost in Sewickley? Sealcoating a residential asphalt driveway in Sewickley typically runs $200–$700 per single application, depending on size and any necessary prep work like cleaning or minor crack sealing. This helps slow oxidation and water damage from freeze-thaw cycles, especially in our climate where uncoated surfaces break down faster. While it doesn’t fix cracks or base problems, regular sealcoating keeps your driveway looking fresh and provides an important layer of UV protection. Unlike repaving, sealcoating doesn’t require a municipal permit in Hempfield Township, letting you maintain your drive without extra paperwork. For a typical tract home, expect to pay around $375 for a good job—including one that’s done by hand for the edges and apron, not just sprayed on thinly. Don’t skip it if your surface is still structurally sound.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes, in Sewickley and throughout Westmoreland County, you’ll almost always need a separate approach permit if your driveway ties into a state route—that means the very end where the pavement meets the public road. This falls under PennDOT District 12 via the Westmoreland County maintenance office, not your local borough or township. Even if the rest of your project doesn’t require a permit, you can’t alter the curb, drainage, or apron near the street without PennDOT approval. Failing to get one can lead to fines and mandatory ripping and replacement at your cost. Approach permits help ensure the slope, drainage, and sightlines are safe, and you can confirm what’s needed before hiring a contractor—sometimes this is handled within the overall paving contract cost, which usually falls between $3,400–$8,700 for a full job including the approach.
When is the best time of year to pave in Sewickley? The best season to pave a driveway in Sewickley is late spring through early fall, generally from May through September, when daily highs consistently stay above 50°F and there’s less rain. Warm, dry weather lets asphalt and concrete cure properly, ensures good compaction, and keeps surface finish smooth. Early spring and late fall bring more freeze-thaw cycles, making base prep and asphalt cooling tricky—projects started during these times are more likely to crack or settle. Scheduling in high season may mean a little longer wait and sometimes higher prices at the peak, but you’re paying for optimal conditions and lasting results. For a standard single-family home, expect to pay between $3,400–$8,700 for asphalt or $4,800–$11,500 for concrete, regardless of season, but you’ll get the most for your money when the weather is on your side.
