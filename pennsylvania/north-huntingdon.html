Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors must be properly registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before you sign anything for a North Huntingdon driveway, verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Also be cautious of door-to-door sales after storms (“storm chasers”)—a credible contractor should have a verifiable PA HIC registration you can confirm online.

Permits vary by scope. Residential driveway paving often does not require a building permit in many PA municipalities, but driveway approach work—where your driveway meets a public road—may require an access permit from PennDOT or the local municipality, especially if you’re changing the apron, widening the entrance, or altering drainage. Confirm before excavation so you don’t get forced to adjust later.

Runoff matters under Pennsylvania stormwater rules (commonly referenced as Chapter 102). Your contractor should ensure the project doesn’t increase runoff onto adjacent properties or the public right-of-way. That typically means correct grading and, when needed, drainage solutions.

Workers’ compensation is also essential: verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose homeowners to liability if something goes wrong on-site.

Step-by-step for North Huntingdon homeowners: (1) check HIC registration online; (2) request a certificate of insurance showing general liability and auto (if applicable); (3) confirm workers’ compensation coverage on the WC registry; (4) ask whether any PennDOT/local driveway access permit is required based on your driveway approach and grading plan.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your North Huntingdon driveway?

In North Huntingdon, the freeze–thaw cycle is the defining factor in choosing asphalt versus concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail in different ways. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically performs better against freeze–thaw cracking when it has a properly built base and adequate thickness. However, asphalt needs routine preventive maintenance—commonly sealcoating every 2–3 years—to slow oxidation and protect the surface from salt and moisture.

Concrete is rigid and can last decades, but it’s more prone to specific failures when conditions aren’t ideal—think spalling, small cracks that widen, and rough patches near edges where salt and trapped water can attack. In Westmoreland County, winters frequently bring multiple freeze–thaw cycles per season, and road salt use makes edge protection and jointing/finishing important. With about 70% of North Huntingdon homes built before 1980, many driveways are at or beyond expected life, so a full replacement is often the most reliable path—whether you choose asphalt or concrete—rather than trying to “stretch” an already-failed base with an overlay.

Here’s how to think about the money: if your base is failing, an overlay won’t stop the movement underneath. A full asphalt replacement in the $7,000–$14,000 range may be justified because it includes base rebuilding and thicker asphalt to control rutting and edge failure. If your base is sound and you only have surface cracking, resurfacing around $3,500–$8,000 can make sense—without paying for excavation that isn’t necessary.

Ultimately, the right choice depends on what’s happening below the surface. In a region with older housing stock and freeze–thaw, we recommend confirming base condition first, then selecting the material that matches your long-term maintenance comfort and performance expectations.

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in North Huntingdon?

What causes driveway cracks in North Huntingdon?

Driveway cracking in North Huntingdon is usually caused by a combination of subgrade/base issues, water intrusion, and freeze–thaw cycling. Common triggers include inadequate base depth, contaminated or poorly compacted stone, and insufficient asphalt thickness for the way vehicles load the driveway. When water gets into the base—often from poor slope, lack of crown, or edge drainage issues—it freezes and expands during winter, stressing the pavement and leading to heaving and cracking. In older homes—where a large share of housing stock is pre-1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—driveways may have been built with thinner bases and weaker drainage performance than modern standards. Salt use can also worsen surface deterioration, especially if sealcoating is delayed. Minor narrow cracks may respond to crack filling, but if cracks are spreading, accompanied by rutting or potholes, the driveway typically needs base and thickness corrections rather than surface-only patching.
