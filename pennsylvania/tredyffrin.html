Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, contractor qualification and proper drainage are just as important as the paving itself. If the work exceeds $500, Pennsylvania generally requires contractors to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. Before you hire, verify the registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be especially cautious of “storm chasers” who solicit driveway work after heavy weather—if a contractor can’t provide verifiable HIC registration, it’s a significant red flag.

Permits can vary by municipality. In many PA cases, residential driveway paving does not require a building permit, but the driveway approach—where your driveway meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. If you’re widening the entrance, changing the curb cut, or doing work near the road right-of-way, check before any excavation begins.

Runoff and stormwater matter in Chester County. Pennsylvania’s stormwater requirements (often referenced under Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A professional crew will grade the surface correctly and may incorporate drainage improvements.

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose you to liability.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Tredyffrin driveway?

For Tredyffrin homeowners, the defining factor in choosing asphalt versus concrete is Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle. Both materials move with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible and tends to handle freeze-thaw cracking better, especially when the base is properly prepared and drainage is corrected. Concrete is rigid; when water gets trapped under slabs or around edges, the freeze-thaw can lead to cracking and spalling—issues that can show up sooner in areas exposed to road salt or repeated temperature cycling.

Material upkeep also matters. Asphalt commonly needs sealcoating every 2–3 years to slow oxidation, which helps keep the surface from drying and cracking prematurely. Concrete typically requires less routine maintenance, but once it cracks in a freeze-thaw environment, repairs may be limited to targeted fixes rather than “resetting” the whole driveway.

Housing age plays a major role in which choice fits best. With 63.4% of Tredyffrin homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways are at or beyond their expected structural life. In those cases, resurfacing or sealcoating alone is often not enough because the failure is rooted in base conditions. That’s why we frequently recommend full replacement when we see widespread base weakness, alligator cracking, or edge breakup.

A dollar example: if your existing asphalt base is failing, an asphalt full replacement often aligns with the $5,000–$12,000 range. If the base is sound and only the surface is worn, resurfacing in the $2,500–$7,000 band can make sense—because you’re paying for the right layer, not covering up deeper problems. The correct answer depends on your structure, drainage, and how much of the driveway needs true reconstruction.

What causes driveway cracks in Tredyffrin?

Driveway cracks in Tredyffrin usually result from a combination of shrinkage and movement, water intrusion, and base issues—then accelerated by Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle. One common scenario is edge breakup: water seeps at the sides, freezes, expands, and loosens the asphalt from the base. Another is alligator cracking from repeated cycles over weakened base or insufficient compaction. Also, improper drainage grading can create low spots that pond; when that water freezes and thaws, it can lead to heaving, then potholes. In neighborhoods with older homes, many driveways were built before modern base standards, and we often find base depth and condition that can’t resist winter forces indefinitely (63.4% pre-1980 homes, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). Heavier vehicle loads—SUVs, delivery vehicles, and frequent trips over the same wheel paths—can also contribute to rutting and fatigue cracking. The “right” fix depends on cause: sealcoating and crack filling help with maintenance, but structural cracking and recurring soft spots generally require overlay or replacement to address base and drainage.
