Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors must be properly registered with the Pennsylvania Attorney General as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) when the job exceeds $500. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Also be cautious of any contractor that solicits driveway work door-to-door after storms (“storm chasers”) without a verifiable PA HIC registration—this is a significant red flag.

Permits vary by municipality, but residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit by default. However, driveway approach work—where the driveway meets a public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. If you’re widening, changing the driveway profile, modifying a curb cut, or adjusting anything near the roadway, check before excavation.

On the stormwater side, Pennsylvania’s Chapter 102 regulations generally require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. That means proper grading and, when needed, drainage solutions (like swales, curb adjustments, or approved routing) should be part of the plan—not an afterthought.

Finally, confirm workers’ compensation coverage. Verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. On an East Whiteland property, an uninsured crew can expose you to liability if someone is hurt.

Homeowner checklist (step-by-step): (1) verify HIC registration online; (2) request a certificate of insurance (general liability) matching your project address; (3) request proof of workers’ compensation coverage; (4) ask what permits apply to your driveway approach and who will handle them; (5) keep copies of everything with your signed contract.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your East Whiteland driveway?

For East Whiteland homeowners, Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle is the deciding factor. Both asphalt and concrete move with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze-thaw cracking better when it’s built correctly with adequate base and proper drainage. Concrete is rigid; it can be long-lasting, but in Chester County winters it may be more prone to spalling or cracking—especially where road salt and deicing chemicals are used, or where minor base movement is present.

That older housing stock matters too. With 41.2% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways have already aged past their original lifespan expectations. When the base is failing, resurfacing won’t “fix” the movement—full replacement is often the smarter path regardless of surface material. In those cases, asphalt often remains the cost-effective choice because it allows smooth restoration with a flexible system and maintenance planning.

Sealcoating is key for asphalt: it’s a protective maintenance step, generally done every 2–3 years when the base is sound. Concrete typically needs less routine maintenance like sealcoating, but when concrete does crack, repairs can be more variable in appearance and cost depending on extent and placement.

Concrete can be a great long-term investment when you want low maintenance and you’re addressing drainage and base properly. But the price difference needs to be justified: for a typical East Whiteland driveway, asphalt replacement often falls within $5,000–$12,000, while new concrete commonly lands in the $7,000–$16,000 range. If your driveway is already moving from freeze-thaw water infiltration, the extra upfront concrete cost may not pay off unless the drainage and base are corrected thoroughly.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in East Whiteland?

In East Whiteland and across Chester County, base failure often shows up as “it’s more than the surface.” Look for areas that repeatedly break up after patching, sunken spots that don’t stay firm, and cracks that look like they’re spreading from beneath the asphalt (not just surface splitting). Alligator cracking combined with soft or spongy feel is a common signal that the stone base is no longer providing stable support. Edge breakup—where the driveway starts raveling along the sides—can also point to inadequate side support and water infiltration. Another clue is chronic ponding or slow drainage after rain; with Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle, standing water can freeze, expand, and heave the pavement. If you’re seeing rutting under vehicle weight or new potholes that seem to recur in the same spots, the base is likely compromised. In those cases, sealcoating or crack filling is usually not a long-term fix; you’re often looking at overlay only if the base is sound, or full replacement when the foundation needs rebuilding.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in East Whiteland's climate?

A properly installed asphalt driveway in East Whiteland generally lasts about 20–30 years, but the “how” matters as much as the “what.” With Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle, longevity depends on adequate base thickness and compaction, correct asphalt thickness, and proper drainage so water doesn’t sit under the pavement. When the stone base is rebuilt to specification and the asphalt is placed in proper lifts, asphalt handles seasonal movement well and tends to resist cracking better than rigid surfaces. Maintenance also plays a role: sealcoating is usually the practical preventive step every 2–3 years when the driveway is still structurally sound, helping slow oxidation and extend service life. If homeowners delay maintenance until the asphalt is badly raveled, the pavement can fail sooner—even if it was originally built correctly. Also, driveways near curb cuts or with runoff patterns that send water across the surface will wear faster unless grading and edging are addressed. The biggest takeaway: in East Whiteland, base and water management are what protect that 20–30 year lifespan.

Should I repair or replace my driveway in East Whiteland?

Repair can make sense when the asphalt surface damage is limited and the base is stable. If you have minor cracking under about 1/4 inch, limited localized issues, and no signs of ponding or repeated failures after patching, crack filling and patching may be appropriate—often a short-to-mid-term solution. However, in East Whiteland and the surrounding Chester County townships, many older driveways show base failure patterns where repairs don’t “stick.” If you see widespread alligator cracking, rutting, heaving, or soft spots—especially where water tends to collect—the cost-effective long-term move is usually mill-and-replace or full replacement with new base. That’s because Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle can turn trapped water and weak support into potholes and rapid surface breakdown. As a general decision guide: if you’re mainly treating the surface, options can align with resurfacing around $2,500–$7,000; if you’re rebuilding the foundation, full replacement more often lands around $5,000–$12,000. A site evaluation of base condition is what ultimately determines the best call.

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in East Whiteland?

Sealcoating is most effective as a preventive maintenance step, not as a rescue treatment for failed base. In East Whiteland and across Chester County, a sound asphalt driveway is typically sealcoated every 2–3 years when the surface is still intact and cracking is manageable. If your driveway has stable support—no widespread alligator cracking, no major rutting, and no areas that feel soft when walked on—sealcoating can slow oxidation and help the asphalt stay flexible through winter. Expect the typical sealcoating application cost to fall around $200–$800 for a standard driveway (depending on size and prep). If you’re dealing with chronic ponding, heaving, or repeated patch failures, sealcoating won’t correct the underlying water and base problems; that’s when full replacement or a properly designed resurfacing plan may be needed. Also, time matters: sealcoat should be applied when temperatures are suitable and the driveway is dry, so the sealer bonds properly. In Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw season, you want a coating that cures fully before winter stress hits.
