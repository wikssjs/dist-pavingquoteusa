What affects the price of driveway paving in Lower Merion

A “resurface” sounds simple, but if we find a compromised base, contaminated stone, or water pooling, the job becomes a full rebuild. Driveway size and current condition are usually the biggest drivers. Finally, the decision to sealcoat versus replace depends on integrity. Sealcoating is a protective, cosmetic step that makes sense when the asphalt is still structurally sound. If you’re dealing with alligator cracking or heaving, sealcoating won’t fix the underlying issue, and a full replacement in the $4,500–$16,000 range becomes the realistic path. In Lower Merion, where many homes were built before 1980, it’s common to see that shift from “maintenance” to “rebuild.”

Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors that do work exceeding $500 generally must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If you see door-to-door solicitation after storms (often called “storm chasers”) offering driveway repairs, treat it as a serious red flag if they can’t show a verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits vary by municipality, but driveway paving typically does not require a building permit for the driveway surface itself in many PA towns. The driveway approach—where your driveway meets the public road—can require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality, especially if curb, sidewalk, or right-of-way areas are impacted. Before excavation, your contractor should confirm what applies in Lower Merion to avoid stopping work midstream.

Water runoff matters, too. Pennsylvania stormwater requirements (commonly referenced under Chapter 102) mean paving projects should not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Expect the contractor to confirm proper grading and may include drainage solutions if ponding is currently an issue.

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage by checking wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews on your property can create liability exposure for the homeowner.

Step-by-step for Lower Merion homeowners: confirm PA HIC registration online; request a certificate of insurance (general liability and, if applicable, auto) that names you as applicable additional insured; ask for workers’ compensation proof and verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov; and make sure any driveway access or right-of-way permits are handled before excavation begins.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Lower Merion driveway?

In Lower Merion, the defining factor between asphalt and concrete is Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is more flexible, which helps it tolerate the repeated movement that comes with cold nights and thawing periods. Concrete is rigid; when water gets into weak points—plus road salt and freeze–thaw forces—concrete can spall or crack. Winters in Montgomery County often bring multiple freeze–thaw cycles per season, which is why we see more surface-related deterioration on rigid slabs over time, especially at edges and joints.

Housing age plays right into this decision. With about 81.7% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways are already near or past their original performance window. When you’re replacing an older driveway, full replacement is more common than overlay, because the base often can’t support a new surface without reconstruction. That’s why asphalt may still be the most cost-effective choice for many Lower Merion homeowners: it provides a flexible surface plus routine preventive maintenance.

Here’s a practical dollar example. If your driveway needs resurfacing, you might consider the $2,400–$9,000 asphalt overlay range. But if inspections show base failures, switching to full replacement (often $4,500–$16,000) is more justified than spending on an overlay that will fail early. Concrete can be a strong long-term option for homeowners prioritizing longevity and willing to accept higher upfront cost—typically $6,000–$18,000—while planning for occasional repairs. When you start from an older driveway profile in Lower Merion, the “right” choice often comes down to base condition and drainage as much as the surface material.

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

For Lower Merion and most of Pennsylvania, thickness depends on whether you’re resurfacing or replacing the driveway, plus how the base was built. A typical long-lasting approach for a full-depth asphalt replacement is about 2.5–3 inches of new asphalt placed in two lifts, after rebuilding compacted base where needed. That rebuild is important because in Montgomery County’s freeze–thaw winters, weak or undersized stone base often turns into cracking and alligatoring. If your existing asphalt is structurally sound and the damage is limited to surface cracking, an overlay may use a thinner asphalt approach, which is why overlay pricing is usually in the $2,400–$9,000 band rather than full replacement. The correct thickness should be tied to an on-site inspection: we check base condition, drainage, and the extent of cracking/heaving before choosing thickness and whether you should sealcoat afterward.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Lower Merion?

Signs of base failure are common in older Lower Merion driveways—especially those built before 1980—because water and freeze–thaw cycles magnify base weaknesses over time. Look for driveway sections that look “wavy,” settled, or uneven; areas that crack repeatedly in the same spots; and alligator cracking (the interconnected “map” pattern) that often expands instead of staying stable. Another red flag is ponding or slow drainage: if water sits on the driveway after a rain, it can infiltrate the base and lead to heaving. Edge failure is also a common indicator—if the apron area near the street or sidewalk breaks up faster than the field of the driveway, base loss is likely near the edges. While a surface-level repair might be suggested initially, true base failure usually requires excavation and rebuild before resurfacing. That’s what moves a project out of “sealcoat or overlay” territory and toward replacement budgets like the $4,500–$16,000 range.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Lower Merion's climate?

A properly built new asphalt driveway in Lower Merion—meaning the base is rebuilt where needed, asphalt thickness is appropriate, and the surface is sealed at the right time—often lasts about 20–30 years. Pennsylvania’s climate is tough on driveways: freeze–thaw movement and moisture intrusion can accelerate deterioration if drainage is off or if the base is undersized. That’s why we don’t treat driveway paving as “just laying asphalt.” Preventive maintenance also matters. Sealcoating is usually done every 2–3 years when the asphalt is still structurally sound, and it can extend service life by roughly 3–5 years per coat. If you skip maintenance and let the surface oxidize badly, cracks can worsen and what started as minor issues can become base problems. In other words, longevity comes from correct construction plus ongoing protection—not just the day the paver roller leaves the site.

Should I repair or replace my driveway in Lower Merion?

Repair is often appropriate when damage is limited—think minor cracks under about 1/4 inch, no significant settlement, and no evidence of base failure. In those cases, crack filling and patching can be a practical short-term option (commonly $200–$800) while you plan for bigger work. Replacement is usually the better move when you see alligator cracking, heaving, widespread cracking, or uneven sections that suggest the base is failing. In older Lower Merion driveways, those base issues are frequently tied to moisture and freeze–thaw cycles, so resurfacing alone can fail early. A meaningful rule of thumb: sealcoating ($250–$900 per application) is protective and cosmetic; it doesn’t correct base failure. If you’re deciding between overlay and full replacement, compare the scope. Overlay pricing (often $2,400–$9,000) can make sense only if inspection confirms the base is structurally sound, while full replacement (commonly $4,500–$16,000) is the realistic solution when the underlying system is compromised.
