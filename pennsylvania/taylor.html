Most driveways in the area are old enough that edge failure and widening come up regularly, especially with houses built before 1980. What homeowners rarely realize is that the biggest driver of price isn’t the blacktop or the concrete you see, but the base below it. It’s easy to compare square-foot prices. It’s harder to know who’s quoting the full excavation, base stabilization, and drainage correction your driveway might need – and who’s just giving you a surface refresh that won’t last.

Almost all the price difference between two quotes comes down to what happens underground and which shortcuts are avoided or not. Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In Taylor and across Lackawanna County, most homeowners don’t need a building permit to simply repave a driveway in its current size and location. That said, the moment you expand, change the curb cut, or do anything where your drive meets a public road, a different set of rules kicks in. Scranton, for example, requires permits for new driveways, curb cuts, or any work in the public right-of-way. If your driveway meets a state route, you’ll need an approach permit from PennDOT District 4-0. Townships and cities manage approach permits for local streets, so always check with the local municipality before starting any curb or edge work.

Stormwater is a growing concern. If you expand your driveway and increase the impervious surface, you may trigger stormwater runoff requirements; in any case, Pennsylvania law won’t let you direct stormwater from your drive onto a neighbor’s property. That liability sits with you, not the contractor or municipality.

As for hiring a contractor, Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Office of Attorney General for any contract over $500. You can verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation coverage is mandatory for all employers—check compliance through WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. Don’t assume coverage just because a certificate was shown. A reputable contractor will already have these, but it’s your right to verify before you sign.

Diagnosing a failing Taylor driveway

If you’re trying to decide whether to repair or replace your driveway in Taylor, there are a few telltale signs to check before calling anyone out. These clues come from what’s happening beneath the surface. If you see alligator cracking—a dense, scaly pattern across a wide area—it almost always means the base underneath has failed. Even the thickest overlay will fail soon if you don’t rebuild the base. Isolated, straight cracks are less worrisome and usually just surface aging. Depressions or birdbaths that hold water indicate subgrade movement—often from freeze-thaw cycles and poor drainage. Crumbling along the edges means there’s no proper edge support, a common problem in local mid-century drives. Potholes mean water has already worked its way through.

Don’t let anyone sell an overlay or quick fix for a driveway that’s showing true base failure. If a contractor doesn’t dig a test hole or probe several inches down, they’re guessing and so are you. A true evaluation looks under the asphalt, not just at the surface. Here’s what typical issues look like and what actually fixes them:

Why Taylor driveways fail from the bottom up

Every durable driveway in Taylor starts below ground level. What you see is only the final layer. The assembly is always subgrade (the native glacial till soil here), compacted aggregate base, then the asphalt or concrete layer. Each layer exists to distribute loads to the one below. It doesn’t matter how good the asphalt mix is if the base is too thin, badly compacted, or sitting on wet, unstable soil. Our Taylor subgrade—often a stony, silty glacial till—will shift and heave if not stabilized and drained properly.

The real enemy in this climate is water and freezing. When the water table rises under your drive and freezes, it expands, lifting the entire assembly. This “frost heave” cracks the surface from below, creates depressions, and opens the door to more water intrusion. That’s why the specs for base depth and drainage are higher here than in milder regions—frost penetrates to 36 inches, and the freeze-thaw swings are harsh. If a paving crew only scrapes off the surface and doesn’t rebuild the base, you won’t see problems right away, but year three will show soft spots, cracks, and dips as the seasons work on what’s beneath. This is exactly where a cheap quote shaves money: by skipping excavation, using too little or untested aggregate, poor drainage, and no staged compaction.

A written quote should specifically state: at least 6–8 inches of compacted 2A modified stone (though 10"+ is better for driveways carrying heavy vehicles); aggregate type (local quarry, not recycled demolition debris); and that compaction is done in lifts, not all at once. Anything less is someone saving on your long-term expense, not theirs.

Maintenance that actually works

The cheapest and most effective way to extend the life of your driveway in Taylor is proactive crack filling — don’t wait until water gets to the base. Seal cracks as soon as you see them forming, which keeps freeze-thaw cycles from splitting them wider. Keeping the edges supported with good base and not letting cars run off the sides goes further than most people think, especially on our typical suburban lots where edge crumbling is common. Always keep water draining away: clean gutters, make sure the surrounding grade slopes off, and don’t let snow piles sit for weeks on the edges.

New asphalt needs proper curing. Don’t park, especially heavy vehicles, or turn wheels on the spot for at least a week, preferably longer if it’s cool out, or you’ll risk early scarring. Avoid loading up the same exact tire tracks or leaving dumpsters sitting for days — even after a year, asphalt is softer in a Taylor spring than at any time in Arizona.

Salt is rough on concrete, so use treated sand or calcium magnesium acetate for de-icing. On asphalt, the real problem with salt is re-freezing water that gets into cracks, not the salt itself. Alternatives like CMA cost more up front, but save money on repairs down the line for high-value drives.

Finding a legitimate paver in Lackawanna County

Paving brings in contractors from all over, especially in a market like Lackawanna County. Protect yourself: make sure your contractor holds a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (search at hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and verify active workers’ compensation coverage through WCAIS. Do not take a generic paper certificate as proof. A real local business has a physical address you can verify, not just a cell phone and trailer. They own (not just rent) equipment, can give references from jobs at least three years old, and know where their material is coming from.

With local aggregate and asphalt coming from our Northeastern Pennsylvania quarries, any paver worth hiring can name their supplier. If they can’t, they aren’t the ones actually doing the work. Passing these basic checks is the minimum — not a guarantee, but a filter for the biggest risks. Here’s what to check:

  • Valid PA HIC registration (not expired; verify online yourself)
  • Active workers’ compensation coverage, not just liability
  • Local business address — no PO boxes or just a cell number
  • Ownership of paving equipment (look for their logo and PA registration on trucks)
  • References from at least three local jobs, 3 or more years old
  • Ability to name their stone/asphalt supplier
  • Written estimates with full specs (see below)
  • Compliance with Taylor or local approach permits for curb work
  • No demand for large cash deposits up front
  • Avoids high-pressure, limited-time “today only” pricing
  • Answers basic code questions without guessing
  • Offers a written contract compliant with Pennsylvania law

If they avoid questions, won’t show you their registration, or use only rented equipment, walk away. Most drive-by “we have extra asphalt” offers are a scam in this area.

Numbers, not adjectives

Here’s how you avoid regret: demand a contract that puts all the numbers in writing. Don’t settle for “a good base” or “thick asphalt.” Ask for specifics: how deep will they excavate? How many inches of what type of stone, and is it compacted in lifts, not just at the very end? What is the binder (base) course as compacted, not as placed? How thick is the wearing (surface) course? Local mix numbers (e.g., PennDOT 25mm base, 9.5mm surface) should be named. Edge treatments (cobble, concrete curbing, bevel) must be spelled out.

The phrase “three inches” means nothing until you ask: is that when it’s hot from the truck, or after the roller has finished? Asphalt compacts by at least 20–25%, so 3" loose is just over 2" once finished — not adequate for our climate and soil. Our Northeast Pennsylvania winters mean frost can reach 36", and the glacial till underneath doesn’t tolerate shortcuts. Specs that would work in Maryland or Ohio fail quickly here.

  • Excavation depth (inches below final surface)
  • Aggregate base type (specify 2A modified stone, not generic “crushed stone”)
  • Aggregate base depth (6–10 inches, compacted in 2–3-inch lifts)
  • Compaction method (plate or roller, lifts, passes per lift)
  • Binder course thickness (as compacted, e.g., 2.5–3 inches)
  • Surface course thickness (as compacted, e.g., 1.5–2 inches)
  • Specific asphalt mix designation (local PennDOT spec, e.g., 25mm, 9.5mm)
  • Edge treatment (concrete, bevel, “keyed” into lawn, etc.)
  • Drainage plan (pitch or swale)
  • Supplier (quarry/plant location, haul distance)

If a quote leaves these details out, you’re price shopping apples and oranges. Only numbers keep a contractor honest a few years later.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Taylor

Are door-to-door pavers a problem in Taylor? Door-to-door paving offers are a real concern in Taylor and across Lackawanna County. With so many older homes and a high ownership rate, it’s an easy target for out-of-town crews looking to unload leftover asphalt at “discount” prices. Some of these operations offer driveways for a few thousand dollars in cash, often well below the normal market range of $3,200–$8,200 for a full replacement, but they rarely do proper excavation or base preparation. The results might look fine for a few months, but lack of compaction and poor drainage leads to cracks and potholes by the second or third freeze-thaw season. If someone knocks offering a same-day deal, especially if they push for cash or offer a price that feels “too good,” you’re likely dealing with unregistered or outright fraudulent contractors. Always verify HIC registration through hic.attorneygeneral.gov before hiring anyone for driveway work.
How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Taylor? In Taylor’s climate, sealcoating every two to four years is usually about right. Our region’s freeze-thaw swings, coupled with a fair amount of snow and road cinder, mean asphalt oxidizes and weather-checks faster than in milder Pennsylvania areas. Most homeowners have found that staying on the two-year interval prevents surface cracks from opening up during our harsh winters, but if your driveway is newer or gets little sun, you might stretch to four years. Each application costs about $175–$650 for a standard single-family driveway. Avoid sealcoating too often; more is not better, and over-application can cause its own issues. The best strategy is to watch for fading or hairline cracks and treat them, rather than just working by the calendar.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, all contractors working on jobs over $500 need to be registered with the Office of Attorney General as Home Improvement Contractors. The only official way to check is hic.attorneygeneral.gov — you can search the number on their business card or contract. Always confirm that the registration is in the business’s real name and hasn’t lapsed or been revoked recently. If the job involves a crew, verify current workers’ compensation insurance through WCAIS. For a full driveway replacement, which runs $3,200–$8,200 in Taylor, never proceed with just a handshake or a business card. Ask for local references on jobs at least three years old and make sure their address matches what’s on the state registry. Pennsylvania law also requires a written contract and gives you a three-business-day right to cancel; a legitimate contractor will volunteer both.
Should I repair or replace my Taylor driveway? The deciding factor is usually not what’s at the surface, but what’s happening in the base. In Taylor, driveways often date back decades and have faced repeated freeze-thaw damage. If you see widespread alligator cracking or persistent dips and potholes, the base is almost certainly compromised, meaning a surface repair or overlay simply won’t last. For isolated cracks and small holes, repairs can run in the $200–$800 range and can buy a few more years. But if the issues are spreading, it’s time to consider a full replacement — which costs $3,200–$8,200 — to avoid wasting money. A good contractor will probe the base and explain the cause, not just offer the highest- or lowest-priced fix.
How deep should the aggregate base be here? In Taylor, a proper driveway needs an aggregate base of at least 6–8 inches of compacted 2A modified stone for average cars, and 10 inches or more if you expect heavier vehicles. That’s a step up from many milder parts of Pennsylvania, due to our glacial till soils and deep frost (36" per code). Anything less risks frost heave, dips, and early cracking. Cutting corners on base depth might save a few thousand up front, as seen with some quotes under $3,200–$8,200, but you’ll pay triple for repairs by the fifth year. Always require “compacted in lifts” in the quote, not dumped all at once.
How much does driveway paving cost in Taylor? For a standard single-family driveway in Taylor, a full asphalt replacement with new base typically runs $3,200–$8,200, with most landing around $5,000. An asphalt overlay where the underlying base is still solid is less — between $1,350–$4,100. Concrete runs higher, from $4,600–$11,000. Small jobs like crack filling or patching might be as low as $200–$800. The biggest variable is what’s under the old driveway: failed base, wet clay, or improper slopes can all increase the cost, while simple resurfacing on a sound base can save thousands. Always get a written quote that spells out exactly what’s included below the surface.
When is the best time of year to pave in Taylor? The best time to pave a driveway in Taylor is between late May and early October, when daily highs reliably reach 60°F or better. Our region’s cool nights and unpredictable spring and fall weather make it tough to achieve the compaction and cure you need for a durable job outside that window. Paving too early or late risks weak bonds and premature failure, even if you got a good price. Contractors often offer discounts to fill their schedule in the shoulder season, but unless the surface temperature is right, a job in April or November can cost you more in patches and repairs. Plan your paving early in the season if you want it ready for family events, but remember — even with a slot opened up, the work only counts if done in the right weather. A rushed job at $3,200–$8,200 will not outlast one installed at the proper temperature.
Why are paving quotes in Taylor so different from each other? Quotes for driveway work in Taylor can swing wildly, sometimes by thousands of dollars, even for the same looking job. The reason is that most of the cost isn’t the asphalt itself, but what’s happening below ground: how much base is excavated and replaced, where the material comes from (haul costs are real here), and how well the base is compacted. Skipping base prep can drop a price below $3,200, but it’s a shortcut that will show up within a couple of seasons. Some contractors own their equipment and source locally, others act as brokers and sub everything out, adding a markup. Any quote that is vague about base depth, compaction, or drainage is probably bidding low up front but leaving you with the risk down the line.
What causes driveway cracks in Taylor? Cracks in Taylor driveways usually start from freeze-thaw action. Water seeps into small surface imperfections; when it freezes, it expands, widening and deepening the cracks. Our glacial till subgrade is especially unforgiving, and the 36" frost depth means nearly every driveway is exposed to this stress every winter. Aging asphalt and simple oxidation will cause surface checks over time, but most structural cracks are due to base movement and poor drainage, not just normal wear. Edge cracking is common with our mid-century houses, as many original drives were poured or paved without proper edge support. Once edges start to go, repair costs sit in the $400–$1,500 range, but if the base has shifted, replacement is the smarter move. Routine sealing and watching for drainage issues beats waiting for the first big crack.
