Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In most of Logan Township, you will not need a building or paving permit just to patch or resurface an existing driveway—especially for routine repair where the footprint stays the same. This saves you time and paperwork compared to projects in nearby Altoona, where even modest changes can require municipal oversight. However, the point where your driveway meets the public road, known as the approach, is a different matter. For driveways connecting to U.S. 220 near Hollidaysburg, the required Highway Occupancy Permit comes from PennDOT District 9, not your local township office. If your driveway opens onto a local street, the approach is regulated by your municipality or borough, each with its own process.

Expanding your driveway or adding new impervious area can trigger stormwater management rules—even when no building permit is needed. Channeling runoff onto a neighbor’s yard brings real liability in Pennsylvania, regardless of permits or code approvals. Logan's permitting requirements aside, it’s always your responsibility to handle stormwater so it doesn’t cause downstream problems.

If hiring a contractor for more than $500, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration is required—verify this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All employers must carry workers' compensation insurance, which you can check at the state’s WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Getting these checks in writing protects your job and your home.

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is sold as a must-do, but in Logan’s climate and on driveways with decades of exposure, the real impact is smaller and more nuanced than most homeowners are told. What sealcoating does well is slow the oxidation of the asphalt binder caused by sunlight and exposure, blocking out UV and resisting intrusion from fuel and oil. This helps the driveway keep its dark, freshly-paved look for a time, and it’s a fast way to improve curb appeal.

What sealcoating cannot do: add structural strength, repair cracks, or fix a base that is already compromised. A failing or alligatored driveway will not be helped by sealant, and repeated applications will not prolong the life meaningfully. In fact, overselling the service leads to another common problem: too-frequent sealcoating builds up layers that dry unevenly, crack, and peel. New asphalt needs to cure for several months before any sealer is applied, or trapped solvents will bubble the surface.

The right interval in Logan is every three or four years—not annually—and only when the old layer has sufficiently faded and the surface is dry and clean. Between sealcoats, crack filling is actually more important; wide, unfilled cracks let water into the base, which is the primary cause of early driveway failure here. A proper sealcoat will be squeegeed on in a thick, even layer—not misted on thin by a passing crew. A suspiciously cheap job, under $200, usually means watered-down materials, overspray, and little real protection. Quality crack sealing and occasional sealcoating, done right, will preserve your driveway far more than yearly cosmetic touch-ups.

Drainage: the failure nobody plans for

More than wind, sun, or even heavy traffic, water is what destroys driveways in Logan and across Blair County. Proper drainage isn’t just about where the surface water goes on the day your new drive is installed—it’s about years of freeze-thaw cycles that work their way up through the layers. The driveway must be built with a minimum slope of at least 2% (about a quarter inch per foot) to shed water; most often this means crowning the center or giving the slab a cross-slope so water drains away to the sides and not toward your garage or basement.

Steep hillside lots, common in this region since the post-1945 buildout, sometimes make perfect grading impossible. Here, trench or channel drains installed at the garage, or at the lowest point, collect water before it can enter your home. Discharging the collected water to daylight, rather than to a neighbor’s property or back under the slab, is critical. Dry wells can work if nowhere else is available, but they must be sized for our heavy winter runoff.

The local limestone and shale soils in Blair County drain slowly and retain moisture against the driveway base—especially if clay is present. This raises the risk that any ponded water will infiltrate, freeze, expand, and heave the driveway. Once water finds a way under, every freeze-thaw cycle makes the damage worse. It’s worth remembering: sending any extra runoff onto a neighbor’s land creates a liability headache, even if the township signed off on the plan.

Comparing materials in north-central Pennsylvania

Choosing a driveway surface in Logan is a trade-off shaped by our particular climate and winter maintenance habits. Asphalt’s flexibility is its main strength in north-central Pennsylvania. In a region where ground snow loads top 40 psf and frost reaches down 36 inches, freeze-thaw cycles are inevitable. Asphalt deals with this by flexing and moving with the ground, so it resists cracking during the winter. Its downside: shorter life and a need for periodic sealcoating, particularly if edges are left unsupported or drainage is poor.

Concrete enjoys far greater lifespan—often 30 to 50 years—but requires you to avoid salt-based de-icers. Salt is a concrete killer in this region; spalling and scaling, not cracking, cause most concrete failures locally. If you don’t spread salt but rely on sand or other treatments, you might get full service life. But snowbelt winters and strong home habits make that a tough promise to keep for many in Logan.

Pavers offer long life and easy repairs, but installed cost is higher and requires a firm, well-draining base to avoid settling. Gravel remains a practical solution for rural lots and long approaches, especially outside the denser Altoona/Logan corridor. It handles drainage well and is budget-friendly, at the cost of regular upkeep.

What's under the asphalt matters more than the asphalt

Whether your driveway lasts three years or three decades in Logan comes down to what’s under the blacktop. The construction is layered: first, the subgrade (native soil); second, a compacted aggregate base; then, and only then, the asphalt or concrete cap. Each layer spreads the weight to the one below. If the aggregate base isn’t deep enough—or isn’t compacted in proper lifts—the surface flexes or cracks the first time a truck parks there or the ground freezes hard.

Blair County’s common subgrade, a mix of limestone and shale, tends to hold water and can settle unpredictably, especially on hillside lots carved out after 1945. The region’s 36-inch frost depth and 40 psf ground snow means water trapped in the base will expand as it freezes and physically lift the slab—this is frost heave. When that happens, cracks form from the bottom up, and no amount of crack filling or surface patching can fix it.

Here’s where cheaper bids cut corners: skipping excavation, laying asphalt over a soft or wet base, using less aggregate (or the wrong kind), or failing to compact each four-inch lift until it passes a simple density test. None of these show the first week, but all will telegraph through in winter number three when the frost heaves up the places where water has pooled. Get it in writing: minimum base depth (for Logan, 6–8 inches is standard on residential driveways, more on poor soils), aggregate type (crushed stone, not recycled), and that every lift was machine-compacted and tested. Everything else is window dressing if the base isn’t right.

Choosing a paving company

Hiring for driveway paving in Logan is more than checking websites and looking at before-and-after photos. In Blair County, the legitimate paving outfits have a verifiable local business address (not just a mobile or out-of-county number), own their paving and compaction equipment, and will give references that go back at least three full seasons. Ask where their blacktop comes from—the Altoona and Duncansville plants are the direct source for almost all reputable jobs in this area. If they can’t answer, you’re likely dealing with a broker or seasonal crew passing through. Verifying workers’ compensation insurance is also essential—Pennsylvania’s WCAIS system makes it possible to check everyone. Registration as a Home Improvement Contractor ($500+ jobs) is required by state law, and you can look up any firm at hic.attorneygeneral.gov.

  • Verify active Pennsylvania workers’ compensation (wcais.pa.gov)
  • Require a written, signed contract—no exceptions
  • Ask if the address is a shop or yard, not just a PO box or house
  • Get references from jobs at least three years old in Logan or Blair County
  • Ask who supplies their asphalt and aggregate—must be local plants
  • Look for owned equipment, not rented or borrowed
  • Review the specs: base depth, aggregate, compaction method, and finish
  • See their insurance certificates, not just promises
  • Avoid contractors who demand large deposits up front
  • No high-pressure or “today only” discounts
  • Do not accept verbal change orders—get everything in writing

Red flags: anyone working only off a cell phone, can’t name their supplier, offers a dramatically low price, or pressures you to skip workers’ comp or contract terms. In this trade, if it sounds too good to be true, it’s worth walking away.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Logan

How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Logan? Sealcoating every three to four years is ideal for most asphalt driveways in Logan. Our freeze-thaw cycles and long winters mean surfaces oxidize faster than in milder climates, but sealing annually creates buildup that can peel and crack. Wait until the old coating has visibly faded and the surface is clean and dry before sealing again. Applying sealcoat too often or too soon after a new install (before the asphalt has cured) can do more harm than good. Budget about $200–$650 per application. In between, focus on proper crack filling; unsealed cracks let water reach the base, which causes far more damage than waiting an extra season for a new coat.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? Logan driveways should be built with a minimum slope of 2% (about one-quarter inch per foot) to ensure water runs off the surface quickly. This is particularly important here because our winters bring heavy snow and frequent freeze-thaw cycles. Without proper grading, water pools, seeps through the asphalt, and freezes—eventually breaking up even a well-built driveway. In situations where the grade cannot be corrected, such as steep hillside lots common around Logan, channel or trench drains may be necessary to move water away from the house or garage. Never direct runoff onto neighboring property—Pennsylvania law holds you liable for water you send downhill.
What kind of soil is under Logan driveways? In most of Logan and surrounding Blair County, the subgrade is a mix of limestone and shale. This material offers decent support when properly compacted, but it drains slowly and tends to hold moisture under the surface. On hillside lots, fill soils brought in after 1945 can introduce even more variability. Poorly drained soils mean driveways need a thicker aggregate base and sometimes underdrain systems to prevent settlement and frost heave. Driveway failures here almost always trace back to water trapped in the base, not the asphalt itself.
What does sealcoating cost in Logan? In 2026, a single sealcoating application on a typical Logan driveway runs $200–$650, with most homeowners paying around $375. This range covers most single-family drives in the area; unusually large or long drives on rural lots might run higher, but suspiciously low prices usually mean watered-down product or skipped prep work. Quality sealcoating should be thick, evenly applied, and include proper crack filling, not just a fast spray-on.
Are there stormwater rules on paving in Logan? Yes. Expanding or repaving your driveway in Logan—especially if it changes the footprint or adds new impervious area—can trigger stormwater management requirements. Even when a permit isn’t required for minor patching, you remain responsible for ensuring that runoff doesn’t drain onto your neighbor’s property. Municipalities or, for US 220 approaches, PennDOT District 9 may require stormwater plans when driveways are widened. Any new paving work should plan for drainage; inadequately managed runoff is a common source of neighbor disputes and liability in Pennsylvania.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? In Logan’s climate, it's best to wait at least 3 to 5 days before driving on fresh asphalt. Cooler weather can slow the cure, so err on the high side if temperatures drop. Early traffic risks scarring or rutting the surface, especially from heavy vehicles. For a typical replacement, costing $3,400–$8,600, holding off those first few days protects your investment for years. Avoid parking in the same place or turning tires sharply for several weeks; full curing takes months, particularly in the cooler spring or fall months common in north-central Pennsylvania.
Asphalt or concrete for a Logan driveway? Both can work in Logan, but each has a trade-off shaped by our harsh winters and soil. Asphalt, with an install cost of $3,400–$8,600, does better with freeze-thaw cycles because it flexes with the ground. It needs resealing but rarely cracks from winter movement if built right. Concrete lasts much longer if kept salt-free ($4,800–$11,500), but spalls and scales when exposed to de-icing salts. Most driveways in Logan are asphalt, as the extra upfront cost and strict maintenance on concrete don’t fit every homeowner’s needs.
Why are my driveway edges crumbling? Crumbling driveway edges in Logan nearly always point to lack of base support or water infiltration. The borders are the most vulnerable part—they get less support from the ground and often no extra aggregate. Over time, vehicular traffic near the edge breaks down the asphalt, especially if the base was thinned to save money. Freeze-thaw cycles and slow-draining local soils make this worse, as water sneaks in, freezes, and separates the layers. If caught early, isolated edge failures can sometimes be patched ($200–$800), but widespread crumbling usually means the base and drainage need redesign, not just a quick fix.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in Logan? For a standard single-family home in Logan, expect to pay $4,800–$11,500 for a completely new concrete driveway in 2026. The typical job—two cars wide, up to 50–60 feet long—lands around $7,700. This price covers proper excavation, a new aggregate base, and quality mix. If your site needs retaining, extra grading, or underdrain (common on hillside lots), costs rise accordingly. Concrete is more expensive to install than asphalt but can last twice as long, provided you avoid salt and address drainage at the start.
