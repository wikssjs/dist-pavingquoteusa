What thickness should an asphalt driveway be in Pennsylvania?

Driveways used by heavy vehicles (trucks, RVs) should have a thicker asphalt layer (3-4 inches). Our Yeadon contractors provide material specs with every estimate.

Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, driveway work is regulated through a mix of contractor licensing rules and, depending on where and how you’re paving, local or state access requirements. First, Pennsylvania requires paving contractors to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If someone is soliciting driveway work door-to-door after a storm (often called “storm chasing”) and they can’t provide a verifiable HIC registration, treat that as a serious red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in many Pennsylvania municipalities, but the driveway approach where your driveway meets the public road can require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. If your project involves curb-cut/apron modifications, excavation near the right-of-way, or drainage changes at the street connection, check before any work begins.

Runoff and grading: PA stormwater rules (commonly referenced under Chapter 102) generally require paving not to increase runoff onto adjacent properties or the public right-of-way. That means proper grading and, when needed, drainage solutions are part of compliance—not optional.

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can leave you exposed to liability if something happens on your property.

Step-by-step for Yeadon: (1) confirm HIC registration at the AG site, (2) request a current certificate of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation, (3) compare the certificate dates and names to the contractor on your proposal, (4) confirm whether the scope includes any right-of-way work that triggers a PennDOT/local permit, and (5) keep copies with your contract and payment receipts.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Yeadon driveway?

In Yeadon, Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle is the deciding factor for asphalt versus concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze-thaw cracking better when the base is properly prepared and the driveway has correct drainage. That flexibility is also why asphalt needs maintenance: with local freeze-thaw cycles, sealcoating every 2–3 years helps protect the binder from water intrusion. Concrete is rigid and can spall or crack when freeze-thaw cycling and road salt expose weak points—particularly if the subbase isn’t built correctly or if the surface joints aren’t managed.

Because Yeadon has a large share of older housing stock—about 94.1% of homes were built before 1980—many driveways are at or beyond their expected lifespan. In those cases, full replacement is commonly the right call. If your existing asphalt is cracked from age and moisture and the base is failing, overlay/resurfacing won’t correct the underlying issue; it just delays symptoms.

Where the decision becomes practical is cost justification. If your driveway can be resurfaced, asphalt overlay often fits the $2,800–$7,000 band, and that can be a smart value when the base is sound and the grading drains properly. But if you’re looking at ruts, heaving, or wide alligator cracking that suggests base failure, concrete at $7,000–$18,000 or full asphalt replacement may cost more up front than overlay, yet it’s what you pay to stop the cycle of repeated repairs during Pennsylvanian winters.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Yeadon?

Base failure is the big reason homeowners end up paying for more repairs than they expected. In Yeadon’s climate, look for evidence that problems are going beyond the surface: (1) ruts or depressions that remain after minor patching—pavement can “look repaired” while the base continues to shift; (2) cracking that seems to widen rapidly or becomes alligator cracking in multiple areas; (3) areas that feel hollow when you walk on them or look “pumped” after rain; and (4) heaving/spalling near the driveway edges or at transitions to curb/sidewalk aprons where drainage and support change.

Water behavior is another clue. If you notice ponding after rainfall or during thaw periods, water is likely infiltrating cracks and stressing a weak or poorly draining base. In Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle, that repeated expansion and contraction can break pavement from underneath, not just on top. That’s why sealcoating and crack filling are maintenance steps—they can slow deterioration on serviceable pavement, but they don’t rebuild base strength.

If you suspect base failure, ask your contractor to explain whether full replacement is needed versus overlay. A resurfacing/overlay quote can make sense only when the base is structurally sound. If base failure signs are present, full replacement is typically the better long-term fix.
