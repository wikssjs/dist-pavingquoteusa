Derry’s driveways are mostly a single-family concern, and with a homeownership rate above 80% in the area, most residents bear this expense directly. The majority of homes here are detached, with driveways that reflect a generation of steady use and Derry Township's rural patterns. Unlike some metro suburbs, driveways around Mifflin County must make it through long, icy winters and freeze-thaw cycles, putting real demands on both materials and installation practices. The subgrade around Lewistown and Derry comes with its own twist—shale and limestone beneath the topsoil—increasing the need for a thick, well-compacted aggregate base to resist frost heave, rutting, and settling.

Most of the cost difference between two Derry paving quotes is not what you see on the surface. Materials are only half the story; what’s underneath—excavation, base prep, compaction, materials haul—is where shortcuts happen and where price differences add up. An “expensive” quote with full excavation, proper base depth, and compaction in lifts will save you years of headaches compared with the smooth-talking crew willing to pave over soft spots or fail to address drainage. Driveways here are an investment, and with local contractors often tacking on travel surcharges, it pays to understand what you’re really getting for your dollar.

Sealcoating: what it does and what it doesn't

Most driveways in Derry will hear about sealcoating sooner or later, especially with older asphalt that’s losing its dark finish. Here’s what it really does: sealcoating applies a protective layer to the top of your asphalt, acting as sunscreen for the pavement. It slows down oxidation, shields the asphalt binder from Pennsylvania sun, fuel, and oil spills, and freshens up the look. Over time, that slows brittleness and helps keep the drivewater-tight for the first several years.

But despite what some salesmen claim, sealcoating does not add structural strength or fix underlying problems. If your base is failing or the pavement is cracking deeply, a sealcoat—no matter how thick—won’t buy you more than a better-looking patch. Repeated sealing, especially annually, is actually harmful: layers build up, peel, and create spots water can seep underneath. Putting sealcoat on fresh asphalt before it’s properly cured traps volatiles, which ruins the new surface. The right interval is every 3–4 years for most Derry driveways, and wait at least six months—preferably a full year—after new paving before thinking about sealant.

Crack filling is the real maintenance step that extends life. Clean, routed cracks filled with a hot-applied rubberized material last years longer than a spray-on seal. Thin, spray-and-go applications—often the cheapest sealcoating jobs—usually last a single winter, deliver minimal protection, and leave you paying again next year. When you see a price for sealcoating under $175 for a whole Derry driveway, it almost always means watered-down product and no real prep. Don’t pay twice for a job that should last several seasons.

The paving season in north-central Pennsylvania

Paving in Derry is a strictly seasonal business, thanks to Mifflin County’s long, cold winters and regional weather that’s among the harshest in Pennsylvania. The winter design temperature here drops to 2 °F, and for most of the year you simply can’t lay hot-mix asphalt because it cools too quickly to compact properly. Once the frost starts going into the ground—well before snow sticks—you’ll find every reputable crew is shutting down their pavers for the season. Most asphalt plants serving Derry shut down from late November until late March or April, because the mix will clump before it can be laid and finished.

Concrete isn’t much different; even with insulated blankets, you need above-freezing weather for concrete to hydrate and cure. The local limestone aggregate used in the base performs well, but nobody gets gravel or asphalt in place once the ground hardens up. Add in travel surcharges from contractors coming from State College or Harrisburg, and you’ll see why the short season compresses demand and raises late-spring and early-summer paving costs.

If a crew says they can pave your drive in late November or well before April, ask hard questions about how they’ll keep mix quality up and what happens if the weather turns overnight. The sweet spot for price and scheduling here is after spring thaw and before July heat, or in early fall. A little patience can save you real money and a lot of future repair headaches.

What's under the asphalt matters more than the asphalt

What you see on top—a fresh strip of smooth, black asphalt—means nothing if the layers beneath are wrong. In Derry, where most driveways are put through repeated freeze-thaw cycles, the real work happens underground. Your typical drive is built in three layers: subgrade (native soil), a compacted aggregate base (almost always local limestone), and then the asphalt layer on top. Each one passes the weight downwards, and every failure underneath multiplies up—especially in a region plagued by hard winter frost.

Here’s why your neighbor’s bargain paving job failed in three years: shortcutting the base. The shale and limestone subgrade found around Lewistown and Derry Township means you need a thicker, well-draining base than you would on sandier soils elsewhere in Pennsylvania. Why? Frost heave. When water in the subgrade freezes, it expands, lifting spots of your drive unevenly. That cracks asphalt from below—nothing you do up top fixes that. Proper base prep means excavating soft spots until you hit undisturbed soil, laying 6–8 inches of compacted aggregate, and placing it in 2–4 inch layers—or "lifts"—that are compacted before the next goes on.

Cutting corners here means leaving old, soft, or organic material under your drive, using too little base, skipping compaction, or slapping asphalt over a driveway that’s already shifting. The scary part: you won’t see these failures when the crew rolls out, and you can’t tell by eye. Three years later, cracks and potholes show up right where all the money was "saved." If you’re comparing quotes, demand these specs in writing—base depth in inches, limestone aggregate, placed and compacted in lifts—and don’t give that final payment until you see each layer done right.

Getting thirty years out of a driveway

A Derry driveway can last up to three decades if treated right, but it’s rarely the weather up top that kills it first. Keeping cracks sealed before water gets down to the base is the best investment—crack filling should be done at least every other year or as soon as you spot a problem. Next in importance: support the edges. The sides of a driveway in single-family neighborhoods see the most crumbling, since wheel loads close to the edge easily break unsupported asphalt. Top-dress those shoulders with gravel or soil as needed.

Directing water away matters most in a climate with 6,300 heating degree days; water pooling on or near your drive gets inside every crack, leading to freeze-thaw expansion and quick failure. Never let dumpsters, jack stands, or heavy trucks sit in the same spot for days—point loads create indentations that never come out.

Right after new asphalt is installed, stay off it for as long as your contractor recommends—usually up to a week in Derry’s cooler spring or fall temperatures. For concrete, avoid parking or turning wheels too soon; early use makes it prone to spalling.

When it comes to de-icing, never use salt on concrete—it accelerates scaling and ruins the surface. For asphalt, salt is less damaging, but the real danger is freeze-thaw from repeated melt and refreeze. Sand and calcium magnesium acetate (CMA) are alternatives, but both cost more than rock salt and can be harder to find. Consider non-chemical traction when you can.

Comparing materials in north-central Pennsylvania

The freeze-thaw rollercoaster in Derry and across Mifflin County shapes every driveway. Asphalt’s big advantage is flexibility: it bumps, settles, and springs back as the base shifts with freezing and thawing. For the area’s 69% of homes built before 1980, that’s key, since few old driveways were built to today’s base depth standards. Asphalt rarely cracks straight through from base movement; when it fails, it does so gradually, and patching or overlay buys you useful years.

Concrete, on the other hand, is rigid and less forgiving. It can last fifty years—if the owner avoids the single most common mistake: using salt to de-ice. Here in the Pennsylvania snow belt, salt is everywhere. On concrete, it accelerates surface spalling, which is why so many older slabs in Derry look pitted and rough even when they haven’t cracked. You get longer life with concrete, but only if you commit to gentle de-icing and immediate cleaning after winter road treatments. Most people don’t.

Permeable pavers handle freeze-thaw fairly well and allow repairs by swapping out individual units if a spot heaves. Their cost is higher than asphalt and close to concrete. Gravel is the true budget choice outside boroughs like Lewistown—low install cost and indefinite lifespan with regular grading and top-dressing. On longer rural runs, it’s still the standard.

Permits and approvals for paving in Derry

In Derry Township and most surrounding parts of Mifflin County, you don’t typically need a building permit to repave or resurface your driveway when you keep it in the same spot and footprint. That saves you a step compared to more urban Pennsylvania locations, and it means you can upgrade worn-out asphalt or even switch to concrete with less paperwork. But where the driveway meets the public road—the approach—things change: driveway approaches connecting to state highways in Derry need a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 2-0, while approaches off township roads are handled by the local township office. Change the curb or sidewalk in Lewistown Borough, and you trigger a right-of-way permit there as well.

If you’re planning on widening your driveway or adding impermeable surface, remember that you could affect stormwater runoff. Even without a permit, you’re liable if added water ends up on a neighbor’s property or causes flooding. Always check with your township before regrading or expanding, especially in older neighborhoods with limited stormwater infrastructure.

When hiring any paving, verify the contractor holds a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General—required for jobs over $500. For employers, up-to-date workers’ compensation is also mandatory; both can be checked online at hic.attorneygeneral.gov and wcais.pa.gov. Never sign a contract in Derry without seeing proof, and know that you’re entitled to a written contract and a three-day right to cancel under state law.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Derry

Does Derry have local asphalt plants? The Lewistown and Derry area do not have full-scale asphalt plants directly in the township, so mix often comes from plants outside Mifflin County. This matters because longer haul distances can raise costs and shorten the window crews have to work the material, affecting the quality if mixed isn’t delivered hot. Most contractors in Derry will factor extra delivery charges into their quote, increasing the installed price of asphalt driveways to somewhere between $3,300–$8,400 depending on driveway size and exact mix sourcing.
Is an overlay worth it or should I start over? An asphalt overlay or resurfacing is only worth considering if your existing driveway base is still solid and free of deep structural cracks. If the cracks are wide, the base is soft, or you are dealing with rutting—common in older Derry driveways on shale and limestone subgrades—starting over with full base replacement is the smarter use of your money. Overlays usually cost $1,400–$4,200 but last only 8–15 years; a full replacement will address the root problems and can give you up to 30 years of life. Have a reputable local contractor core or probe your base before deciding.
Is gravel a cheaper option in Derry? Yes, gravel is often the most budget-friendly and practical option for longer or rural driveways in Derry Township, especially where plowing and road salt aren’t a constant concern. A new gravel drive usually runs $900–$3,500, well below concrete or asphalt. With limestone aggregate available from local quarries near Lewistown and Burnham, material supply is steady, keeping costs reasonable. Just be ready for periodic grading and top-dressing—gravel is low on up-front cost but trades that for regular upkeep.
How much does driveway paving cost in Derry? Paving costs in Derry are set by both square footage and base condition, along with material choice and hauls from regional asphalt plants. A full replacement asphalt driveway lands in the $3,300–$8,400 range for residential projects in Mifflin County, with overlays typically running lower at $1,400–$4,200. Concrete driveways cost more upfront—$4,700–$11,000—but last longer if maintained. Always get written detail on base depth and compaction, as unseen shortcuts are where many cheap jobs show up just a few winters later.
How deep should the aggregate base be here? In Derry and the rest of central Mifflin County, the local shale and limestone subgrade calls for a thicker aggregate base than in other parts of Pennsylvania. Six to eight inches of well-compacted limestone base is standard over undisturbed soil or prepared subgrade. This base thickness is crucial in a climate with heavy frost—our 36-inch frost depth means that a shallow base will almost always heave and crack the asphalt, no matter how thick the top layer is. More is always better than less when it comes to base prep for driveways costing $3,300–$8,400 to replace.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes—almost every Derry homeowner needs some type of permit where the driveway connects to a public road. For driveways accessing a state highway in Mifflin County, you’re required to get a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 2-0. On township roads, the local township issues approach permits. In Lewistown Borough, disturbing the sidewalk, curb, or apron always triggers a separate permit. Approach permits aren’t strictly about paperwork; they control how water drains and ensure sight lines remain safe, even on paving jobs under $4,200.
Does sealcoating fix cracks? No, sealcoating alone does not repair cracks in your Derry driveway. Sealcoating is meant to shield the surface from the sun and minor spills; cracks wider than a hairline must be filled with a separate crack-filling product before any sealer is applied. Skipping this step leads to water infiltration, freeze-thaw damage, and shortened lifespan. A proper crack-filling job runs $200–$800 and will do far more to protect your drive long-term than a surface-only sealcoat costing $175–$650.
How far ahead should I book a paver in Derry? Because Mifflin County sees a compressed paving season, most reputable contractors in Derry and nearby will be booked weeks or even months ahead from April through October. Waiting until you see damage is almost sure to push your project into the busiest part of the year, when prices can be in the upper part of the $3,300–$8,400 band due to demand and scheduling constraints. For the best shot at both fair pricing and a quality job, reach out as soon as spring weather arrives and be ready to sign before summer or right after Labor Day for fall work.
What causes concrete to spall or flake in Derry? Concrete in Derry is especially vulnerable to spalling—surface flaking—because of our routine use of de-icing salts in winter and prolonged freeze-thaw cycles. When salt is applied, it reacts with the concrete’s pore structure, speeding up water ingress. After a melt and refreeze cycle (which happens frequently here), the ice inside those pores expands and pops the surface off. The damage compounds over years, making it the single most common source of failure in this climate. Concrete driveways costing $4,700–$11,000 can last decades if you avoid salt entirely, but that’s a tough habit to break for many homeowners here.
