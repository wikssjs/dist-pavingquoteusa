How to tell if the base is gone

The most important sign of driveway trouble in West Whiteland isn’t faded color or little cracks — it’s what’s happening beneath the pavement. Because almost a third of homes here were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways are facing base or subgrade issues that simple resurfacing will not solve.

A quick self-test: stand back and scan the drive. If you see dense “alligator” cracking that forms a rough sort of map or patchwork pattern, the base under the asphalt has failed and an overlay won’t last — you’d be throwing money at a lost cause. Isolated, linear cracks usually mean surface shrinkage, which is normal over time and responds well to crack filling and sealcoating. Birdbaths or low spots that hold water point to subgrade movement, often from water infiltration or poor drainage. Crumbly edges mean the drive wasn’t built with enough side support. Potholes mean water has breached the base and the spot needs excavation, not a surface patch.

Be careful: a contractor who quotes you for an overlay without probing or coring into the existing driveway to check the base is skipping the most vital part. If the foundation is gone, anything on top is temporary.

How often should a West Whiteland driveway be sealed?

Sealcoating has become a ritual in Chester County, but more is not always better. Its real benefit is to form a protective film that slows the sun and air from oxidizing the asphalt and shields against oil, salt, and minor fuel spills. It also freshens appearance. But it’s not a cure-all — sealcoating adds no strength, won’t fix cracks or alligator areas, and can’t substitute for solid base work.

Once every 2 to 4 years is a good rhythm in West Whiteland’s climate, depending on shade, traffic, and how often debris is cleared. Sealing more often than that will actually hurt. Layer on too soon, especially over a still-gray new drive, and you’re just trapping uncured oils, leading to peeling. Lay fresh sealer every summer and it’ll flake off in sheets. Your first sealcoat should wait until the surface has fully cured — usually 6–12 months after new asphalt is laid.

What matters more is addressing cracks as soon as they appear, because water that gets to the base is what ruins driveways here. If a contractor’s sealcoat bid is barely above material cost, that almost always means a thin, sprayed-on coat that washes off with the first winter salt, not a coat mopped on thick and into the pores, which is what you want. A proper single sealcoat runs $225–$800 for a typical single-family drive.

The paving season in southeastern Pennsylvania

The paving calendar in West Whiteland is dictated by temperature, not by contractor preference. Hot-mix asphalt has to be compacted quickly before it cools, and below about 50 °F, it stops bonding well and the end product is weak and porous — not worth paying for. Local asphalt plants close for the winter, usually from late November or December through March, so there’s simply no material available or warranty for work done out of season. For concrete, the challenge is similar: it can’t cure right if it freezes before it sets, and low overnight temps risk surface scaling or pop-outs.

Winter design temps sit around 14 °F and we see plenty of freeze-thaw days and occasional storm leftovers in Chester County, so late fall and winter paving just doesn’t happen. If you’re looking for the best timing, aim for spring through early fall. Shoulder months like April, early May, September, and early October are ideal: conditions are right, and crews have more availability than the peak of summer. If someone offers to pave for you in December, especially after the plants shut down, you’re looking at a rush job with cold material — not worth the savings.

The short haul from local quarries and plants near Downingtown and Coatesville helps on scheduling, but temperature always calls the shots here.

Maintenance that actually works

Saving your driveway in West Whiteland is less about surface cosmetics and more about keeping water out and support in place.

  • Crack filling: The single most cost-effective step. Any crack wider than a quarter inch is a water route to the base. Fill these as soon as you see them — annual checks each spring go further than frequent sealcoating.
  • Edge support: Driveway edges that butt up to nothing will crumble and spread. Maintaining turf, stone, or curbing support prevents this common failure.
  • Drainage: Keep gutters and downspouts from dumping onto the drive, and ensure proper grading so that water rolls off and away.
  • Avoid point loads: Don’t park dumpsters, trailers, or let jack stands sit on the hot blacktop after a new install — it takes a good 1–2 months for asphalt to cure hard enough, especially during humid summers.

On de-icing: salt is tough on concrete — it eats at the surface and leads to pop-outs. On asphalt, the bigger worry is that melt-and-refreeze cycle, which shoves water deep. Sand or calcium chloride are alternatives, but cost more per bag. If you use salt, sweep what’s left once the ice is gone and don’t overdo it.

Good basic maintenance in this climate — moderate freeze-thaw, humid summers, and hard winter freezes — is less about fancy coatings, more about quick action and steady support.

What you need before paving in West Whiteland

For most driveways in West Whiteland, especially if you’re just resurfacing or replacing within the same footprint set back from the street, a building permit usually isn’t required. This saves time and paperwork. However, if your project includes any work at the approach where the driveway meets a public road, or if new paving encroaches on the right-of-way, that’s a different story — driveway approach permits are handled not by the township but, for state routes, by PennDOT Engineering District 6-0 (see the FACTS). North Coventry Township also requires a permit for driveways in the right-of-way.

Keep in mind, if you expand a driveway and create more impervious surface, you might trigger local stormwater requirements or grading reviews. No matter what approval you have, directing runoff to your neighbor’s yard can leave you on the hook for damage. Drainage matters as much as compliance.

Contractor verification is straightforward: anyone bidding for work over $500 must have a Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration — you can check at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation is required for all employers in Pennsylvania; status can be confirmed via WCAIS. If your home was built before 1978, federal EPA Lead RRP rules may apply to disturbance work — verify at the EPA’s site if there’s any demolition involved.

Drainage: the failure nobody plans for

Most driveways in West Whiteland fail from underneath, and most of those failures are water-related. Good grading isn’t a finishing touch — it’s the make-or-break detail. Your driveway surface should have a minimum slope of at least 2% (a quarter-inch per foot) away from the house or garage so rain rolls off fast. Crowning the middle or building in a cross-slope can direct water to the lawn, but if your drive slopes toward the house or sits in a low spot, surface water will pool, seep into the base, and — in this region’s frequent freeze-thaw cycles — expand and destroy the base structure as it heaves and contracts.

Where the grade can’t be changed, trench or channel drains at the garage threshold, or where water otherwise pools, redirect water before it becomes a problem. But proper outlets matter — daylight discharge is simplest, but in denser tracts, dry wells may be necessary to avoid sending water onto the neighbor’s property and creating a liability risk. Chester County’s abundance of limestone and dolomite means the subgrade can be susceptible to settlement if not properly compacted and drained.

Poor drainage is no small detail; if you see regular puddling anywhere on your current drive — especially after moderate rain — the base is being compromised year after year. Always plan for where the water goes, not just what’s on the surface.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in West Whiteland

How much does driveway paving cost in West Whiteland? In West Whiteland, the cost of paving a driveway depends on both the size and the condition of what’s already in place. For a full asphalt replacement, most single-family homes will pay from $4,100–$10,500, with the typical job falling closer to the middle. If your existing driveway base is still solid, resurfacing with an asphalt overlay runs less, about $1,750–$5,300. Concrete driveways cost more upfront — generally $5,900–$14,000 — but offer greater longevity. Small jobs like sealcoating or crack filling will be much less, sometimes as little as $225–$800. Your best value is always in a properly prepared base, even if you don’t see where the money went — that’s where the difference in lasting value lies in southeastern Pennsylvania.
How many freeze-thaw cycles does West Whiteland get? West Whiteland, like the rest of Chester County, sees a moderate number of freeze-thaw cycles each winter. Exact counts can swing each year, but our region typically gets dozens of days where temperatures fluctuate above and below freezing throughout late fall, winter, and early spring. This is a key reason why driveway bases here need to be carefully built and why drainage is so important. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles cause water in and under the base to expand and contract, cracking and pushing up the pavement from below. That process, if unchecked by good crack filling ($225–$800 for a typical seal and fill job), will destroy driveways that would otherwise last far longer.
How deep should the aggregate base be here? In West Whiteland, a good aggregate base for a residential asphalt driveway is typically at least 6–8 inches after final compaction. Some properties over limestone or dolomite bedrock may do better with even thicker bases to avoid future settlement, especially if there’s any chance of soil movement or drainage issues. Less than this, especially on older drives built before standards tightened, is a shortcut that will show up as cracking or potholes after a few hard winters. While thinner bases might save on upfront costs (bringing bids closer to $4,100–$5,000), a properly thickened base is what gives you the expected 20–30 years of life from a new install in this climate.
Can asphalt be laid in cold weather? Asphalt paving in West Whiteland shuts down in cold weather for good reason. Hot-mix asphalt must be compacted before it cools below workable temperature, generally around 50 °F, or else it won’t bond or seal properly. Local asphalt plants close for the winter, usually late November until spring, so if anyone offers to work out of season, it’s either with leftover material or cold mix, which won’t deliver a lasting surface. Wait until the season opens back up; repairs and full installs done during spring through early fall, when plants are open and temps are up, provide reliable results. You can expect all those seasonal quotes for paving — like $1,750–$10,500 — to be valid only when plants are actually running.
Should I repair or replace my West Whiteland driveway? The answer depends on what kind of cracking, settlement, or fading you’re seeing in West Whiteland. If there’s alligator cracking, widespread depressions, or recurring potholes, it usually means the base has failed and a full replacement (typically $4,100–$10,500) is warranted. Surface cracks under a quarter inch, or faded color, can nearly always be managed with crack filling or sealcoating, which range from $225–$800. Your best bet is to ask your contractor to physically probe the base. Overlays save money only when the foundation is still intact; otherwise, you’re delaying an inevitable replacement and paying twice.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? In West Whiteland, sealcoating is used to protect the asphalt surface from oxidation and minor stains, giving it a renewed black finish, but it does not fill actual cracks or add structural strength. Crack filling, by contrast, involves applying a hot rubberized compound designed to expand and contract, sealing up gaps wider than a quarter inch so water can’t reach the base. If you only sealcoat, cracks remain open under the shiny surface and let water in, which is what leads to freeze-thaw damage. For a well-maintained drive, plan on crack fill and seal every few years — budget $225–$800 per application, depending on length and width of cracks.
Do I need a permit to repave a driveway in West Whiteland? In most of West Whiteland, if you’re simply resurfacing or repaving a driveway within the existing limits set back from the street, you often don’t need a building permit. However, changes at the driveway approach — where you meet a public road — or any expansion into the right-of-way will require a permit, and if your street is a state route, PennDOT Engineering District 6-0 is the permit authority. North Coventry Township is an exception, always requiring permits for work in the right-of-way. Even without a permit, be mindful of drainage — changes that direct water toward your neighbor can result in legal issues. This step costs you nothing, but do check before hiring; the work itself will run from $1,750–$10,500 depending on scope.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in West Whiteland typically falls under full-depth replacement pricing, since expanding means new excavation, new base, and repaving to match grade and drainage. For asphalt, expect about the same per-square-foot rates as new driveway work, so a minor widening could cost as little as $2,000–$4,000 if you’re just adding a few feet, but more significant expansions land in the $4,100–$10,500 band. Concrete is higher. Budget extra for any needed drainage improvements or curbing — those add both value and cost. Permitting may also be required if the apron or street connection changes.
What causes driveway cracks in West Whiteland? Driveway cracks here usually come from one (or more) of three sources. First, freeze-thaw cycles: water gets into surface cracks, freezes, expands, and breaks up more of the pavement from below. Second, subgrade instability, especially where limestone or dolomite underlayment wasn’t well compacted or has shifted — common with Chester County’s varied soils. Third, a lack of edge support lets the drive spread and crumble at the margins. Surface aging and oxidation also play a role in shallower cracks. Small cracks, less than a quarter inch wide, can be filled and sealed for $225–$800; larger or networked “alligator” cracks signal a bigger base problem and will push you toward full replacement or extensive patching.
