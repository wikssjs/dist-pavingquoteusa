Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors are generally required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office when the job exceeds $500. You can verify a contractor’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious if someone solicits driveway work door-to-door after storms (“storm chasers”) and can’t provide verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits: residential driveway paving usually does not require a building permit in most Pennsylvania municipalities. However, the driveway approach where your driveway meets a public road may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or a local municipal approval—especially near borough streets or state routes. Because the right-of-way boundary is where projects get delayed, always check before excavation begins.

Stormwater: Pennsylvania’s stormwater requirements (Chapter 102) require that paving doesn’t increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public right-of-way. That means correct grading and sometimes drainage solutions should be part of the scope—particularly on older Franklin lots where water historically sheeted across lawns.

Workers’ compensation: verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. An uninsured crew can leave homeowners responsible for injuries on the property.

How to verify credentials step-by-step in Franklin: (1) check HIC registration online; (2) request a certificate of insurance for general liability and confirm it matches the job address; (3) confirm workers’ comp coverage via wcais.pa.gov; and (4) confirm whether your driveway approach needs a municipal/PennDOT access permit before work starts.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Franklin driveway?

In Franklin, Pennsylvania, the freeze–thaw cycle is the defining factor in choosing asphalt vs. concrete. Both materials move with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better when it’s installed over a properly built base and maintained with sealcoating every 2–3 years. Concrete is rigid; it can be an excellent long-term surface, but in Greene County conditions it’s more vulnerable to spalling or cracking when water and road salt exposure combine with repeated winter cycling. With about 56.4% of Franklin homes built before 1980, it’s common to find driveways that are already near the end of their service life, making full replacement—often with asphalt—one of the more frequent solutions homeowners consider.

Where the decision becomes practical is cost-to-performance. If your base is compromised, resurfacing usually isn’t appropriate; you’d be paying to cover structural problems that winter will reopen. A driveway in Franklin might be a good example of that: a full asphalt replacement (often in the $4,200–$10,800 range) is justified when the old base is failing and the driveway needs regrading and compacted stone. On the other hand, if the base is sound and the damage is mostly surface-level, an asphalt overlay (often $1,800–$6,000) can extend life without the cost of removal and rebuild.

Concrete can make sense for homeowners who want long-term, low-maintenance use and are planning for the right base preparation and jointing. Still, you’ll generally want to avoid deciding based on curb appeal alone—ask your contractor how they’re addressing drainage and base support first, because that’s what controls performance through Pennsylvania winters.
