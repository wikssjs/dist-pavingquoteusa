Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, reputable driveway paving companies treat paperwork as part of the job—not an afterthought. If your project exceeds $500, the paving contractor must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Also be cautious with any contractor soliciting driveway work door-to-door after a storm (“storm chasers”) who can’t provide verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits: many residential driveway paving jobs typically do not require a building permit in most PA municipalities, but the driveway approach where your drive meets a public road can require approval or a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. That’s especially true if you’re changing the curb cut, shifting the driveway alignment, or adding parking closer to the road. If excavation starts before approvals, you may be stuck redoing work or delaying the schedule.

Water runoff matters. Pennsylvania stormwater rules (Chapter 102) require that paving projects don’t increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A good contractor will plan proper grading and may recommend drainage solutions to prevent ponding that worsens freeze–thaw damage.

Finally, confirm Workers’ Compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose you to liability if someone is hurt on your property.

Step-by-step for Butler homeowners: (1) check HIC registration online; (2) request a Certificate of Insurance (general liability) showing your job address or general service territory; (3) confirm Workers’ Compensation coverage through the WC registry; (4) keep copies of the insurance documents and your signed scope so disputes don’t become a “he said/she said.”

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Butler driveway?

In Butler, Pennsylvania, the freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor in the asphalt-versus-concrete choice. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently when water gets involved. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better and can “give” slightly as the base moves. The tradeoff is that asphalt needs maintenance—sealcoating every 2–3 years is a common schedule to protect the surface from oxidation and water intrusion. Concrete is rigid; it can last a long time, but repeated freeze–thaw cycles and road salt can lead to spalling or cracking, particularly if the sub-base isn’t well-drained.

Because about 37.7% of homes in Butler were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways are approaching or exceeding their expected service life. In those situations, the more cost-effective path is often full replacement with the right system rather than resurfacing over a compromised base. Asphalt replacement commonly lands in the $4,800–$11,000 band, while concrete installation commonly runs $6,500–$15,000. The higher upfront cost of concrete can be justified when you want low day-to-day maintenance and you’re installing on a well-prepared, well-drained base—especially when the driveway will see heavy parking loads.

A concrete example from typical Butler projects: if your asphalt is alligator-cracked and edges are crumbling, resurfacing might quote in the $2,100–$6,300 range, but it often won’t solve the root issue (base water infiltration). If you’re already paying for tear-out and grading, spending closer to the full replacement range for asphalt can be a better value than paying again later. If your existing base is stable and you’re choosing between materials for new install, asphalt often makes sense for flexibility, while concrete makes sense for long-horizon durability—provided the drainage plan is solid.

If you notice ponding after rain or melting snow, that water usually finds its way to the base, and freeze–thaw then accelerates heaving and structural breakdown.

Another red flag is alligator cracking with a network pattern that spreads or where small repairs don’t hold. If you’ve had multiple patch jobs over a few seasons, it often points to compromised support beneath the pavement.
