Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors generally must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the job exceeds $500. Before you hire, verify the contractor’s registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be especially cautious if someone solicits driveway work door-to-door after a storm; “storm chasers” without verifiable PA HIC registration are a major red flag.

Permits: In most PA municipalities, residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit, but the driveway approach—where the driveway meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. That can affect how contractors stage equipment, cut curb/edge, or tie into the existing roadway. Always confirm with your municipality before excavation.

Water runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A credible contractor will plan grading so water sheds properly and won’t redirect flow toward neighboring lots.

Workers’ compensation: verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews on your property can expose you to liability.

Step-by-step for South Fayette homeowners: (1) Confirm HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. (2) Ask for a current Certificate of Insurance (general liability) and verify it matches the service dates. (3) Confirm workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. (4) Request written scope notes showing how grading/drainage will be handled, especially at the driveway apron. (5) If your driveway alters the approach to a street or sidewalk, ask whether a PennDOT/local access permit is needed before work begins.

Be concrete about it: driveway approach modifications to the public road often trigger permit requirements; resurfacing that doesn’t change slope or access typically does not, but you should still check—especially in older neighborhoods with curb ties and stormwater controls.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your South Fayette driveway?

For South Fayette homeowners, Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle is the defining factor in choosing asphalt vs. concrete. Asphalt is flexible and typically handles repeated temperature swings better, which helps resist the kind of freeze-driven cracking that can spread across rigid surfaces. That said, asphalt needs preventive maintenance: sealcoating is usually appropriate every 2–3 years when the pavement is structurally sound. If your asphalt has widespread fatigue or deep base movement, resurfacing won’t solve the root cause and full replacement may be the better long-term value.

Concrete is rigid and can be excellent where you want low day-to-day upkeep, but in Allegheny County winters it can spall or crack—particularly where road salt exposure, surface scaling, or poor subgrade conditions are present. With 38.9% of South Fayette homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many drives are at or past expected service life, which often shifts decisions toward replacement rather than resurfacing.

A practical decision approach: if your driveway has tight, stable asphalt with surface cracking only, asphalt resurfacing in the $2,500–$6,500 range can be justified. But if you’re already seeing ponding, alligator cracking, and loose areas, full replacement in the $4,500–$11,000 range is often the smarter investment. For example, if a 900 sq ft driveway shows base failure, spending the “overlay budget” may just delay the inevitable—whereas full-depth rebuild removes the freeze–thaw weak link. Concrete might be justified when you want long-term durability and can afford the higher upfront cost (often $7,000–$16,000), especially when your contractor will properly prepare subbase and drainage.

How to choose a driveway paving contractor in South Fayette

Choosing a driveway contractor in South Fayette should start with verification. First, check IICRC certification if the contractor references it for material or surface conditions—use the official registries to confirm current status and scope. Also verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov so you’re not stuck with liability if a worker is injured on your property.

Next, request 2–3 itemized written quotes, not just one lump sum. You want a breakdown that separates demo/removal, haul-off, base stone type and depth, compaction, asphalt or concrete quantities, and any drainage/grading work. Make sure the scope includes what’s excluded (for example, “no repairs to failing subgrade” is a common loophole). Ask whether insurance claim documentation, if needed, is handled through their process.

Pay attention to warranty terms: workmanship warranty length matters more than vague promises. Ask for equipment and material warranty details, and confirm how repairs are handled if early failure occurs. Finally, structure payment properly—never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back funds until the job is complete and cleaned up as specified. Get the schedule in writing: an arrival estimate and a completion window based on weather and material lead times.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in South Fayette

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

In South Fayette and the broader Allegheny County area, asphalt thickness depends on whether you’re resurfacing an existing pavement or replacing the full section. For many full-depth asphalt driveway replacements, crews commonly plan about 2.5–3 inches of asphalt installed in two lifts over a properly compacted stone base (often 6–8 inches of base in this region when the driveway needs to perform through freeze–thaw). If you’re doing an overlay, the thickness and lift design still matter, but the key is confirming that the base is stable—overlays can fail if the underlying drainage or soil has soft spots.

Because South Fayette driveways frequently experience Pennsylvania freeze–thaw movement and older subgrade conditions (38.9% of homes pre-1980, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), an experienced contractor should inspect for ponding, pumping, and alligator cracking before deciding thickness. If your quote is only based on square footage and not base evaluation, ask detailed questions. As a reference point, resurfacing in the $2,500–$6,500 band is typically appropriate when the base is structurally sound; full replacement around $4,500–$11,000 is more common when the base must be rebuilt to achieve the correct section.

How long does driveway paving take in South Fayette?

Most driveway projects in South Fayette are measured in days on-site, though you should plan for scheduling time and weather impacts. For a typical asphalt replacement, you can often expect demo/removal and base work on one day, paving on one day, and finishing/curing and cleanup across the following day depending on temperature and traffic control. A resurfacing/overlay is usually faster because less excavation is required—provided the base is sound—so the paving operation can be condensed into a shorter work window.

Concrete generally takes longer in terms of curing and controlled access. Even when crews can place and finish the concrete quickly, homeowners should follow the contractor’s guidance for when to open the driveway safely to cars, especially in the early curing period. South Fayette’s spring and fall can bring temperature swings, and Pennsylvania freeze–thaw can also affect how quickly surfaces can be compacted and how materials behave.

In practice, reputable contractors often book 4–8 weeks out in peak season in the Pittsburgh area, so you’ll want to schedule early. Ask for a written arrival estimate and completion window, and confirm whether your proposal includes regrading and drainage adjustments (a common scope item). If your driveway is moving toward the higher end of replacement pricing like $4,500–$11,000, the added time is usually tied to excavation, hauling, and base compaction.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in South Fayette?

Signs of a failing base in South Fayette often show up as pavement behavior, not just cracks. Look for areas where the asphalt is “alligator” cracked and also appears to be moving—especially when you see separation at joints or edges. Ponding or depressions that hold water after rain is a major indicator that drainage and grading aren’t right, and trapped water can accelerate freeze–thaw damage. Another common sign is rutting or soft spots that feel spongy underfoot near the garage approach or where vehicles turn.

You may also notice that patchwork doesn’t last. For example, if utility-cut repairs or small patches keep opening back up, that often points to an underlying base issue rather than a surface-only problem. In older South Fayette driveways (38.9% built before 1980, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), original base materials may have compacted or shifted over time, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles can worsen that.

When base failure is present, overlay work usually isn’t the right fix. If you’re being offered resurfacing in the $2,500–$6,500 range without discussing base condition and drainage, ask whether they’ll test for stability and whether excavation and stone base rebuild will be included where needed. Proper full replacement around $4,500–$11,000 is often the only way to restore long-term performance.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in South Fayette's climate?

In South Fayette’s Pennsylvania climate, a properly installed new asphalt driveway typically lasts about 20–30 years. That range depends on correct base prep, adequate thickness, good compaction, and drainage control—because freeze–thaw cycles can turn water issues into structural movement. Asphalt itself is flexible, which helps it resist many forms of freeze-driven cracking, but it doesn’t eliminate the need for preventive maintenance.

To maximize service life, plan for sealcoating every 2–3 years when the pavement is still structurally sound. That kind of maintenance helps protect the surface and slows deterioration from oxidation and moisture infiltration. In practical terms, maintenance plans can add roughly $300–$600 over a typical year when averaged over the schedule, depending on driveway size and how many coats are applied.

Home age matters too. With 38.9% of South Fayette homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways start with older subgrades and drainage patterns. If those underlying issues aren’t corrected, even a new asphalt top layer may fail earlier than expected, making full replacement necessary sooner than planned. When homeowners keep drainage properly sloped away from garages and don’t allow ponding, asphalt typically performs closer to the upper end of the lifespan range.

Should I repair or replace my driveway in South Fayette?

The repair vs. replace decision in South Fayette should be based on base condition, not just the visible cracks. Crack filling and patching-only work can be a good choice when the damage is minor—for example, small cracks under about 1/4 inch on recently paved or otherwise stable pavement. In that scenario, you might be looking at something like $200–$800 for localized patching and sealing of joints. But if cracks are widespread, you see alligator cracking, or there are signs of settlement or ponding, those symptoms often indicate base failure or drainage problems that repairs alone can’t fix.

Asphalt resurfacing/overlay is often the middle option when the base is structurally sound. If that’s the case, resurfacing typically falls in the $2,500–$6,500 range and can extend service life, especially with planned sealcoating afterward. However, when the base is soft or the driveway has a history of water holding and repeated freeze–thaw heaving, full-depth replacement is usually more cost-effective over time—often in the $4,500–$11,000 range for this region’s driveway tier.

A quick rule: if water can’t get away from the driveway and the base has begun to move, replacement is often the smarter long-term choice in Allegheny County. A reputable contractor can confirm this with site evaluation and a clear scope, so you’re not paying twice for the same problem.
