Sealcoating: what it does and what it doesn't

Sealcoating is one of the most misunderstood services in Washington and throughout Pennsylvania. When done right, a single application protects the asphalt from oxidation—sunlight turning the binder brittle over time—and from household spills like oil or gasoline. It also renews the look of aged pavement, making your drive look newer and cleaner.

But, and this is important: sealcoating does not add any structural strength to your driveway. You cannot seal your way out of cracks, rutting, or a failed base. If you've got major cracking or sunken areas, sealcoat is no cure. It will not extend the life of a driveway where water is already undermining the subgrade. The main maintenance value of sealcoating is slowing down the slow surface weathering, and that's it.

Few mention the downside: sealcoating yearly actually creates problems here. Layers build up, then crack and peel, sometimes flaking off in sheets. Sealing new asphalt before it's truly cured (typically a season in southwestern Pennsylvania) can trap petroleum volatiles, weakening the surface. In this climate, every 3–4 years is a reasonable interval for established driveways.

Crack filling is even more critical. Filling active cracks before they widen is what keeps water out of the base and prevents potholes—do this before sealing. And not all sealcoats are equal: a proper job, squeegeed into the pores of the asphalt, protects. Most "deals" sold door-to-door are a thin spray, often watered down, and last a year at best. At $175–$650 per service, beware of anyone offering a bargain rate far below this range—they're cutting material or labor, or both.

What you need before paving in Washington

If you're resurfacing an existing, previously permitted driveway in Washington County, you typically do not need a new building permit—especially if you aren't altering the layout. Where the rules get strict is at the driveway approach: the section meeting the public road. For state routes, a Highway Occupancy Permit is handled not by the county, but by PennDOT's district office. For local streets inside the City of Washington, the Code Enforcement office requires an access or paving permit for new or expanded driveways, and the same holds for most nearby townships.

Adding width or changing grade can trigger local stormwater management rules. Runoff is a big concern in this hilly, clay-rich county. If paving changes water flow onto a neighbor’s property, you're potentially liable, permits or not, so plan for responsible drainage.

Before you hire anyone, Pennsylvania requires paving contractors working over $500 to have a current Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration. Confirm this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Employers must also carry workers’ compensation insurance; check via WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. Hiring unregistered or uninsured crews risks penalty for both contractor and homeowner, and you have little legal recourse if things go wrong.

How paving fraud actually operates

Paving frauds pass through Washington County every year, usually in spring and fall. The easy marks are homes with visible cracking or old, neglected driveways—common here given the widespread single-family housing. Here’s how the scams work:

  • Someone knocks, claiming to have “leftover asphalt” from a nearby job, offering to pave or seal at a deal rate if you “act now.”
  • No local signage, out-of-area plates, or no business name on trucks.
  • A price if you pay cash right then—no written estimate, no contract, just a handshake or verbal promise.
  • Classic “spray-and-go” sealcoat—thin, oil-based material, often sprayed from tanks that once held motor oil, offering a perfect-black look that peels up in months. “This will last three years”—it lasts one Pennsylvania winter, at best.

Rural and moderate-density suburban neighborhoods typical of the Washington-Peters-North Strabane corridor are targets because most people are present during the work day, neighbors are less likely to flag out-of-place crews, and verifying a business takes a little more homework.

Pennsylvania law protects you: any paving contract over $500 requires a written contract, and you have three business days to cancel for any reason under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. You cannot be pressured into waiving your rights, and any contractor offering a deal for skipping your deductible is breaking the law.

It takes five minutes to: (1) verify HIC status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, (2) ask for workers’ comp coverage and check wcais.pa.gov, and (3) insist on a detailed, signed contract—never cash up front, no matter how good the discount sounds.

Diagnosing a failing Washington driveway

Most driveway failures in Washington County are visible to the eye, but you have to know what matters. Don’t just look at the surface color — check for these signs:

  • Alligator cracking: Spiderweb cracking over wide areas is almost always base failure. An overlay won’t fix it—it’ll look good for a year then break up again. You’re due for a full replacement.
  • Isolated straight cracks: These are mostly surface aging, which can be sealed or filled, buying you years at low cost.
  • Depressions or “birdbaths”: Standing water after rain points to movement in the subgrade or base, especially in the region's clay soil. Fixing just the surface is a waste.
  • Crumbling edges: Usually means no edge support was installed. On steep or sloped drives, this accelerates rapid breakdown.
  • Potholes: Water has gotten into the base. Patching helps, but only if you stop the water first, otherwise they come right back.

If a contractor quotes an overlay or patch without probing the base or subgrade, find another. Washington’s climate and subsoil mean proper prep is non-negotiable, even if it costs a bit more up front.

Comparing materials in southwestern Pennsylvania

Southwestern Pennsylvania's freeze-thaw cycles and clay soils put specific demands on driveways. Asphalt is more forgiving here: when soil expands or contracts, asphalt flexes. This means a typical asphalt drive takes the yearly punishment from frost and stays serviceable for decades—especially important considering the county's 30 psf ground snow load and 36-inch frost depth. Concrete, though more durable in theory, doesn’t respond well to movement; it cracks and, more importantly here, spalls if you use de-icing salt, which is common during our cold, snowy winters.

For most Washington homeowners, asphalt offers a safe middle ground: not the longest-lived, but easier to repair and less likely to fail catastrophically. Concrete needs careful drainage, regular sealing (even if “maintenance-free” is advertised), and strict avoidance of salt to reach its big lifespan. Most people do end up using salt on icy days, so concrete rarely outlasts its reputation here.

Pavers are legitimate on a stable base and give a premium look, plus you can replace single units after heavy freeze-thaw cycles. Gravel is still common on long, rural or sloped approaches—our local quarries mean supply is never a cost driver, and drainage is excellent.

Getting thirty years out of a driveway

The best return on a driveway in Washington comes from early and regular maintenance. Filling cracks—especially those that grow wider than 1/4 inch—should be top priority, since water in our heavy freeze-thaw cycle is what destroys driveways from beneath. Most base failures here can be traced to neglected sealant or poor edge support. If you have a rural or wide suburban drive, keep those edges backed with soil or stone to prevent crumbling.

Drainage matters next: southwestern Pennsylvania's hilly terrain and clay subgrade means any standing water will quickly undermine your base. Gutters should discharge away from the drive, and driveways should slope away from structures at a minimum of 1/4 inch per foot for the first several feet. Don’t park heavy dumpsters or jack stands on your asphalt, especially in the first year; point loads sink right through a soft spring base.

If you're installing new asphalt, be patient about curing. Avoid parking (especially trucks) and hard wheel turns for a few weeks. For winter, use sand or calcium chloride on concrete—never salt. On asphalt, salt is less harmful, but avoid excessive melting and freeze cycles; sand is safest if traction is your main concern. The cold, wet winters here demand sensible maintenance, not quick fixes.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Washington

Can I pave over my existing driveway?

In Washington, Pennsylvania, it's often possible to install an asphalt overlay if your current driveway base is sound, even if the surface is cracked or faded. An overlay works best if you don't have alligator cracking, depressions, or active potholes—issues which signal base or subgrade failure. If your driveway shows signs of major base movement, as is common on our local clay soils, overlaying is just covering up a problem that will reappear quickly. Budget-wise, an asphalt overlay in Washington costs between $1,350–$4,100, which is substantially less than a full replacement. Always have a reputable contractor probe the old base to confirm it's stable; if they don't, you're risking money for a temporary Band-Aid.

Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door?

Yes—a homeowner in Pennsylvania, including Washington County, can cancel any paving contract signed at their residence within three business days, no questions asked. This right is guaranteed under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act for all contracts over $500, and contractors must provide you with a written notice explaining this right. If someone pressures you after the fact or doesn't provide a written contract, that's a red flag. Don't let anyone start work or take payment until you're certain you're ready; if you have doubts about a deal, use that cooling-off period to verify their Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration and compare estimates. Given that paving jobs here can run from $1,350–$8,200, protecting yourself with the law is smart practice.

What does an asphalt overlay cost in Washington?

For Washington homeowners, an asphalt overlay (sometimes called resurfacing) costs in the range of $1,350–$4,100 as of 2026. That price reflects the moderate-to-high demand in our Pittsburgh-adjacent market, efficient local asphalt supply, and the typical widths found on post-1950 suburban homes. The actual cost depends on your driveway size and—most importantly—the condition of the existing base. Overlays should only be considered if there’s no base movement or extensive cracking; otherwise, the money is better spent on a full-depth replacement, which falls between $3,200–$8,200.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base?

In Washington, the real warning signs of base failure are wide alligator (spiderweb) cracks, sunken areas that hold water after rain, ongoing edge crumbling, and repeated potholes in the same spots—all common issues when driveways are built on shale and clay without enough aggregate base. Surface-only cracks (straight, less than 1/4 inch wide) are usually just cosmetic and can be handled with crack filling, a service costing $200–$800. But if you see the structural symptoms above, basic patching or overlays are no match for the local climate, and it’s time to consider a full rebuild.

What slope does a driveway need for drainage?

Here in southwestern Pennsylvania, Washington driveways need to be built with a minimum slope of 1/4 inch per foot (about 2%) for at least the first 5-10 feet away from the foundation to avoid standing water and surface freeze-thaw damage. Hilly terrain and clay soils mean poor drainage can ruin even new pavement. For driveways parallel to the house, build in a slight crown or cross slope so water drains off both sides, not just toward the low point. Failing to do this leads to puddling, which multiplies base failures and maintenance costs. Bringing in a reputable contractor who understands local drainage is worth the money, considering that correcting drainage problems later can cost from $3,200–$5,000 if excavation and rebuilding are necessary.

Is gravel a cheaper option in Washington?

Yes, gravel remains the lowest up-front cost for driveways in Washington County, thanks to abundant supply from local quarries in the Pittsburgh-Washington corridor. For a typical two-car rural drive or a long approach, expect to pay $900–$3,500 installed, depending on length and base prep. Gravel costs less per square foot, but keep in mind that you'll need to budget for periodic grading and re-dressing, especially after our wet springs and freeze-thaw cycles. It's not maintenance-free, but for long rural drives or budget-conscious homeowners, it's the local practical choice.

Does sealcoating fix cracks?

No, sealcoating does not fix existing cracks in your driveway. In Washington, many homeowners think a new coat of sealer will somehow bond things together, but sealcoating only acts as a surface protectant—it keeps UV light, water, and chemicals from aging the asphalt too quickly. To actually fix cracks, the gaps must be cleaned and filled with a specialized crack sealant prior to sealing. Ignoring cracks allows water into the base, particularly harmful on our local clay soils and during the heavy freeze-thaw cycles here. Crack filling typically costs $200–$800 for most driveways and should always be done before any sealcoating if you want the longest possible life out of your surface.

How far ahead should I book a paver in Washington?

If you're planning to pave or resurface a driveway in Washington, give yourself at least four to six weeks lead time in spring and summer, when demand spikes thanks to better working weather and everyone trying to address winter damage. Some established contractors get booked out even longer during dry stretches, especially if you want a reputable firm with solid references. If you want the best results at a fair price (from $3,200–$8,200 for full replacement), book as soon as you decide—don't wait until your driveway is truly failing. Reliable contractors in our area rarely offer “today only” openings unless there's a last-minute weather gap, so plan ahead.

Are door-to-door pavers a problem in Washington?

Yes, door-to-door “pavers” selling leftover material or urgent “discounts” remain a problem in Washington and throughout southwestern Pennsylvania, largely because our neighborhoods combine long driveways with moderate population density—making it harder for neighbors to compare notes before a job is done. These operators often offer very cheap prices (well below the typical $1,350–$4,100 for overlays or $175–$650 for sealcoating) and may skip base prep or use thin materials. Pennsylvania law requires a written contract and a three-day cancellation right for any job over $500, protections designed for exactly this scenario. Always check a contractor's HIC registration and workers’ comp insurance before signing or paying anything, especially when the offer sounds too good to be true.
