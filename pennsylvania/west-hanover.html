Getting thirty years out of a driveway

Your driveway’s lifespan depends far more on maintenance and water control than on the surface material. In West Hanover’s freeze-thaw climate, the cheapest and most effective thing you can do is fill cracks before water can reach the base. Unfilled cracks let water get into the subgrade, freeze, and expand—setting off a chain that accelerates every problem you see later. Keeping the driveway edges supported, whether by a proper aggregate shoulder or curbing, prevents edge breakdown that can spread inward. Make sure water flows off, not under, your driveway by cleaning edges and downspouts. Avoid heavy point loads on the surface: car jacks, dumpsters, or storage containers can permanently dent both asphalt and concrete. After a new install, do not park cars or turn wheels sharply for at least seven days on asphalt.

De-icers are a hot topic. Common roadway salt is rough on concrete and can pit and flake the surface, especially during West Hanover’s late-winter freeze-thaw cycles. Alternatives like calcium chloride or magnesium chloride are less harsh but cost more. For asphalt, the bigger problem isn’t the salt itself but the water: melt-and-freeze cycles accelerate base degradation. Sand or grit can be safer for both materials and offers traction without chemical reaction. Bottom line: salt with restraint, especially near concrete, sweep up excess in spring, and always watch for water sitting on or next to your drive.

Overlay, patch or full replacement?

In West Hanover, the key question is what condition your driveway’s base is in, because that determines your repair choices—and your long-term outcome. Here’s how to tell. If you see spiderweb “alligator cracking” across wide areas, your base has likely failed and any overlay will crack again within a few cycles. Don’t burn good money: an overlay on a failed base won’t last. If your driveway shows just a few isolated, mostly linear cracks and the surface hasn’t sagged anywhere, that’s usually surface aging—crack filling and sealcoating can go a long way. Birdbaths or “birdbath” depressions—shallow dips that hold water—mean your subgrade has shifted, and you’ll need excavation and a rebuild in that zone. Crumbling or breaking away at the edges usually points to the lack of side support and requires reinforcement or curbing. Potholes are always water infiltration, signaling the base is already compromised. Ask any contractor quoting resurfacing or overlays for a base inspection. If they don’t check or can’t explain what’s underneath, move on—because the visible asphalt is only half the story.

Grading and water management on a West Hanover driveway

Grading on a driveway in West Hanover is not a finishing touch—it is the most important part of the build. Water that doesn’t drain off the surface ends up inside the base, especially in soils shaped by limestone and shale like ours, where pockets and uneven settlement are already a challenge. The minimum surface slope you want for reliable runoff is 2% away from any structure, whether garage or house. Most driveways here use a gentle crown (higher in the center) or a cross-slope to one side. If your drive slopes toward your garage—and plenty in West Hanover do—that’s a setup for water migration under the slab, which leads to frost heave and slab cracks. When grade correction isn’t realistic with the space or you have a flat tie-in, a channel drain or trench drain at the threshold can intercept and redirect water. Water should exit—or “daylight”—as far from any footing or wall as possible. When that’s not available, a dry well is the backup, provided it’s sized for our rainfall and not just a shallow pit.

Redirecting drainage onto a neighbor’s land, even if not expressly prohibited by a permit, is a liability for you and can get ugly fast. Proper grading, well-placed swales, and controlling the exit point on your own property are always best. In our winters, ponded water infiltrates, freezes, and expands—heaving the pavement and breaking it from below. Good grading and surface drainage are your best insurance against both visible and hidden damage.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

For most repaving jobs in West Hanover—where you’re resurfacing an existing, properly permitted driveway on a local non-state road—you typically do not need a new building permit. That can save you time and paperwork. However, once you reach the driveway approach—the area where your driveway meets the public road—that’s a different story. Driveways connecting to state roads in Dauphin County require a permit from the PennDOT Engineering District 8‑0 Highway Occupancy Permits office, not just your township. That catches a lot of homeowners off guard. If you’re widening or constructing a new apron in the Harrisburg city limits, you’d go through the city’s street opening/access permit process, but for most township roads in West Hanover, simple resurfacing stays out of the permit system as long as you’re on the original footprint.

Any time you increase your driveway’s square footage or overall impervious area, be aware—Dauphin County stormwater requirements can kick in, and you cannot legally direct runoff onto your neighbor’s property. Your project is still subject to civil liability if water is pushed onto adjacent land.

When hiring a contractor, always verify their Home Improvement Contractor registration with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and check that they have mandatory workers’ compensation coverage through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov) if they have employees on site.

The specifications to demand in writing

If you compare only price, you’re gambling with your driveway lifespan. In West Hanover, our local limestone and shale base, plus aggressive winter freeze/thaw, mean national “ballpark” specs for asphalt or concrete simply aren’t enough. Every quote should specify, in clear numbers, exactly what you’re paying for: how deep the excavation will be, what material makes up the aggregate base, how thick each layer of asphalt or concrete will be after compaction (not just as poured or laid), what type of asphalt or concrete mix, and how the edges will be supported. The difference between “three inches of asphalt” on a quote and “three inches compacted” in the field can mean the actual layer thickness is closer to two—and that will fail early.

In this climate, a deeper base and proper compaction are not luxury add-ons—they’re the bare minimum. Demand proof that compaction was done in lifts (usually 2 inches at a time for asphalt), which lasts longer than laying down all at once. Here’s what to require, in addition to a clear project price:

  • Excavation depth below finished grade (in inches)
  • Aggregate base depth (minimum 6–8 inches for our soils, 10+ inches in soft spots)
  • Aggregate base material type (crushed limestone or equivalent)
  • Base compaction method and proof of density
  • Asphalt binder course depth (compacted)
  • Asphalt surface course depth (compacted)
  • Mix type/designation for each asphalt or concrete layer
  • Edge treatment—shoulder, curb, or edge-tamping method
  • Drainage plan or slope statement with percentage grade
  • Warranty—what is covered and for how long

Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

The performance of every driveway in West Hanover—especially on our local limestone and shale—is built from the ground up, but the part that controls its lifespan is the base, which you’ll never see again once it’s done. Here’s how it works: First, the topsoil and organic layer are fully removed down to undisturbed subgrade. Next comes the compacted aggregate base—typically crushed limestone from Dauphin County quarries, installed in at least two lifts, each compacted fully before the next goes down. The reason for base thickness (at least 6–8 inches, more if the subgrade is soft or holds water) is to distribute vehicle loads evenly. On top of that goes the asphalt or concrete. If the base flexes, any asphalt or concrete above it will crack or sink, and neither can fix what’s wrong underneath.

In south-central Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw environment, you can’t skimp on this step: water in the subgrade expands when it freezes, so if the base is thin, poorly compacted, or made of the wrong stone, you get frost heave—lifting the surface, popping sections loose, and cracking from below. This is the invisible part of a “cheap” quote: shortcuts like skipping full-depth excavation, laying new asphalt directly over a failed base, using less or lower-quality aggregate, or rushing compaction all save the paver time and money, not you. These problems might not show until year three, but when they do, the only fix is a rebuild.

Always require the following figures in your contract: total base depth (in inches, after compaction), stone type (“2A modified” or local crushed limestone), and that compaction is done in lifts. No hard number, no deal.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in West Hanover

When is the best time of year to pave in West Hanover? For West Hanover homeowners, the best window for paving is late spring through early fall—typically May through mid-October. That’s when daytime highs are consistently above 50 °F and overnight lows rarely dip below 40 °F, allowing proper curing of both asphalt and concrete. Our region’s freeze-thaw conditions mean paving in late fall risks improper curing and may lock in moisture that leads to early cracks, especially if there’s a sudden cold snap. Asphalt plants around Harrisburg and Middletown generally operate April through November, so material supply isn’t an issue, but you want enough dry, warm weather that you’re not rushing the process. Most reputable West Hanover paving crews book up fast for summer, so get estimates in the spring. Expect typical full replacement pricing in the $3,800–$9,700 range, depending on your chosen window and scope.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, any contractor doing more than $500 of work on your home, including driveway paving, must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) with the Office of Attorney General. You can check their status instantly at hic.attorneygeneral.gov by entering their registration number, and you should see their company and insurance listed there. If the contractor has a crew or brings any employees to your West Hanover home, workers’ compensation insurance is required; you can search the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) by business name for current coverage. Do not rely solely on business cards, vehicles, or word-of-mouth—the online registries are the official sources. For any job over $500, you must get a written contract, and you have the right to cancel within three business days under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act.
How often should I sealcoat my driveway in West Hanover? For West Hanover homes, sealcoating is best applied every two to four years on a sound asphalt driveway. Our region’s freeze-thaw cycles and the occasional use of salt or de-icers can wear away the protective surface more quickly than in milder climates. If you have lots of sunlight exposure or frequent snow plowing, you might lean toward the two-year mark. Sealcoating isn’t a cure for deep cracks or base failure, but it will help prevent water and oxidation damage to an intact surface. Don’t overdo it—overapplication can lead to peeling. Expect a typical single application to fall in the $225–$750 range, depending on driveway size and any minor crack repairs included.
Asphalt or concrete for a West Hanover driveway? In West Hanover, both asphalt and concrete have their place. Asphalt is preferred for most replacement jobs thanks to its flexibility and faster installation, especially where frost heave and settlement are common due to our local limestone and shale soils. Asphalt repairs are also cheaper and less disruptive. Concrete, on the other hand, brings top longevity—often 30–50 years—and much lower maintenance, but is more sensitive to road salt in winter. Concrete cracks are less forgiving and costlier to fix. If you want very low maintenance and looks matter more than up-front cost, concrete, at $5,400–$13,000, might be worth the investment. For most West Hanover homes, asphalt provides a good balance of lifespan and price, typically $3,800–$9,700 for a full replacement.
Are door-to-door pavers a problem in West Hanover? Yes, door-to-door paver crews still show up in West Hanover, pitching leftover asphalt or offering steep “one-day only” discounts. While some may be legitimate, these operations often cut corners on base prep and compaction, which shortens the lifespan you get for your money. They may be unregistered or uninsured, making your property a liability risk. A job that appears cheap up front can end up costing the full replacement price—$3,800–$9,700—again in just a few years. West Hanover’s high homeownership rates mean neighborhood reputation still matters, so ask for local references and check every contractor against the Attorney General’s HIC registry before you sign a contract or hand over a deposit.
What does sealcoating cost in West Hanover? For a typical West Hanover driveway, sealcoating costs usually fall between $225–$750 for a single application. That price covers surface cleaning, crack filling for minor separations, and one fresh coat. The total varies with driveway size—bigger surfaces mean more material—and with how much prep is needed if there’s moss, weeds, or lots of edge handwork. If your asphalt is still structurally sound and you’re simply looking to extend its life, periodic sealcoating is the best value maintenance there is. Bigger repairs, like full-depth patches or overlays, will run much higher, so get any serious cracks or depressions evaluated before you spend on sealcoating alone.
How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in West Hanover? Given West Hanover’s pronounced freeze-thaw cycles and the region’s limestone and shale soil base, you should require at least 3 inches of compacted asphalt—meaning after the roller’s done—not just “3 inches as laid.” This is usually structured as 2 inches of binder course plus 1 to 1.5 inches of surface course. For heavier vehicles or softer spots in the subgrade, 4 inches or more is warranted. Don’t overlook the base: a quality install means 6–8 inches of compacted crushed stone underneath, more in poor soils. Cutting corners on thickness is the leading cause of premature failures, even on jobs that look good in year one. Installation in this market averages $3,800–$9,700 for a full replacement, but on price alone, always clarify compacted depth in your contract.
What causes driveway cracks in West Hanover? Most driveway cracks in West Hanover are caused by a mix of freeze-thaw cycling, age, water infiltration, and the occasional heavy vehicle. Our shale and limestone-dominated soils can shift slightly, making edge support and a solid, compacted base essential. Once water finds its way through surface cracks—even hairline ones—it gets into the base, freezes in winter, and expands, pushing the asphalt or concrete up and then leaving gaps as it thaws. Tiny cracks become big ones after a few cold seasons. Maintenance like regular crack filling and proper drainage can prevent problems before they require big repairs. Most minor repair jobs—crack filling and sealcoating—cost $225–$750, while letting problems go too long can lead to a full replacement at much higher cost.

These shortcuts will show as cracks and depressions by year three—even on a job that looks perfect the day it’s finished. In this region, a quote that’s much lower than others usually means a thinner base, less excavation, or less material, not a bargain on identical work.
