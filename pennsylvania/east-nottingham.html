Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors who do home improvement work exceeding $500 generally must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify a contractor at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious if you’re approached by door-to-door crews after storms (“storm chasers”) who can’t provide a verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits vary by municipality, but driveway work often hinges on where the driveway meets the public road. While residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most PA municipalities, the driveway approach/curb-cut area may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality—especially when widening, reconfiguring drainage, or working near sidewalks/curb lines. Confirm before excavation.

Runoff matters. Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (commonly referred to as Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A reputable contractor will address grading so water flows as intended, and they may include drainage solutions depending on site conditions.

Finally, verify workers’ compensation coverage by checking wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose homeowners to liability if someone is hurt on-site.

East Nottingham homeowner checklist (step-by-step): (1) verify PA HIC registration online; (2) request a current certificate of insurance (general liability) showing your address as an additional insured when possible; (3) request proof of workers’ compensation coverage; and (4) ask which permits apply to your specific driveway approach and whether any PennDOT/local approvals are needed before work starts.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your East Nottingham driveway?

In East Nottingham, Pennsylvania, the freeze-thaw cycle is the defining factor in asphalt versus concrete performance. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze-thaw cracking better—provided the driveway has a properly compacted stone base and adequate asphalt thickness in proper lifts. Asphalt also needs routine maintenance: sealcoating every 2–3 years helps slow oxidation and keeps the surface from turning brittle.

Concrete is rigid and can be a great long-term option, but it can spall or crack when repeated freeze-thaw cycling and road salt attack the surface—especially if joints and drainage aren’t managed correctly. With East Nottingham having a meaningful amount of older housing stock (about 30.2% built before 1980), many existing driveways are at or beyond their expected lifespan. That’s why, locally, we see full replacement decisions more often than “patch-and-hope” when base failure is present.

Here’s a practical dollar example: if your driveway can be resurfaced because the base is sound, you may fit in the overlay/resurfacing band of $2,500–$7,000. If the contractor finds base instability, the investment often shifts toward full asphalt replacement (often $5,000–$12,000), which is usually the more cost-effective route in Chester County winters because it rebuilds what actually moves under freeze-thaw.

Bottom line: when your base is solid and the issue is mostly surface cracking, asphalt resurfacing plus maintenance can be a smart value. When the base and drainage are failing, full replacement is usually the best way to protect your investment.
