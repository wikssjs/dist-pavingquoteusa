Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors who will perform Home Improvement work exceeding $500 generally must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. After storms, be cautious of “storm chasers” who solicit driveway work door-to-door; if a contractor cannot provide a verifiable PA HIC registration, treat it as a serious red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most PA municipalities, but the driveway approach—where the driveway meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. This is especially relevant if you’re doing excavation near the right-of-way, adjusting the apron, or changing drainage patterns. It’s also important to check before work starts; a reputable local contractor will ask about the approach location early.

Water runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (commonly referenced as Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Your contractor should plan grading and, when needed, incorporate drainage solutions so water flows as intended.

Workers’ compensation: confirm coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose you to liability.

Camp Hill homeowner verification steps: (1) confirm HIC status online; (2) request a current Certificate of Insurance (general liability and, when applicable, umbrella/excess) naming you as additional insured if your township requires it; (3) provide workers’ compensation proof; and (4) ask whether a driveway access permit or street-opening permit applies before excavation.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Camp Hill driveway?

Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle is the defining factor for Camp Hill homeowners deciding between asphalt and concrete. Both materials handle temperature movement, but they fail differently. Asphalt is more flexible, so it typically resists freeze-thaw cracking better, especially when it’s properly built and sealed on schedule. Concrete is rigid; it can perform extremely well for decades, but when soils move or road salt exposure is heavy, concrete may spall, crack, or require patching in cold climates.

Local housing age matters here too. With about 93.1% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many existing driveways show early-stage base issues like settlement or poor drainage. In those scenarios, a well-built asphalt system—often with 6–12 inches of compacted stone base and 2.5–3 inches of hot-mix asphalt in two lifts—can be more forgiving while still delivering excellent results when maintained. Asphalt also pairs naturally with a maintenance plan: sealcoating is commonly done every 2–3 years when the asphalt is structurally sound.

Concrete can be a great long-term investment when you expect low maintenance and your driveway site has stable drainage and subgrade. But if you’re paying to address drainage and base anyway, sometimes the “cheaper” asphalt path becomes smarter. For example, if a full replacement is in the $4,500–$12,000 asphalt range, moving to concrete at $7,000–$16,000 is justified when you want a longer maintenance interval and you’re confident the base and drainage are being handled properly.

In Camp Hill, if your goal is to match your driveway’s expected service life to an older property with frequent winter cycles, asphalt replacement plus routine sealcoating often provides the best value—while concrete makes sense when the site is stable and you want a tougher, longer-wearing surface with targeted crack repair.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Camp Hill?

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in Camp Hill?

Sealcoating timing in Camp Hill depends on the pavement’s condition and age, but the usual preventive maintenance approach is every 2–3 years for existing asphalt that’s still structurally sound. If your asphalt has minor surface cracking only and no signs of active base failure (no heaving, no pumping, no sections that are sinking or crumbling at the edges), sealcoating can help protect against moisture intrusion, oxidation, and the effects of salt and winter cycling.
