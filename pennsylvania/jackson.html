The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is the most widely promoted driveway maintenance in Jackson, but most pitches exaggerate its impact. Here’s what a sealcoat does: it shields the asphalt binder from sun and oxidation, helps keep out leaked oil and gas, and restores a uniformly black appearance. What it does not do: it provides zero structural support to the driveway, won’t bridge serious cracks, and will not reverse base or deep asphalt failure. If a driveway’s base or lower layers are compromised, sealcoating is lipstick on a pig and won’t buy you real time.

An overlooked fact: there’s such a thing as too much sealcoating. Repeated annual coats can actually build up brittle layers that crack and peel, trapping moisture rather than shedding it. Likewise, sealing new asphalt too soon locks in volatiles, weakening the surface. For Jackson’s climate, a first sealcoat should wait 6–12 months after new paving, and reapplication should be every 2–4 years, not yearly.

Crack filling matters much more than sealing alone. Properly cleaning and filling cracks keeps water from penetrating to the base—the source of almost all serious driveway problems in Lebanon County. A real sealcoat is applied by squeegee or spray in two cross coats, never thinned to stretch material. A suspiciously cheap job—say, under $200–$400 for an average Jackson driveway—almost always means diluted product or skip-work that lasts a single winter, if that.

Used right and at the right interval, sealcoating is low-cost insurance ($200–$700 per application) for a driveway in genuinely good shape. Used as a panacea or cover-up, it’s money wasted.

How to tell if the base is gone

When deciding whether to resurface or replace a Jackson driveway, the base condition is the key. Here’s what you can check yourself before you call anyone:

Alligator (network) cracking over a wide area signals base failure—a direct overlay will bubble and crack again within a couple of seasons. Isolated, straight cracks (less than 1/4 inch wide) usually mean surface aging and can be managed with crack fill and sealcoat. Depressions and “birdbaths” indicate that the subgrade has moved, likely from poor drainage or weak base—patching rarely holds. Crumbling edges mean there was never enough stone foundation or edge support; these fail fastest where trucks or plows stray off the edge. Potholes mean water has gotten into the base—patching them is a short-term fix, and more will follow if the root problems aren’t addressed.

If you’re quoted for resurfacing (an overlay) without the contractor probing or coring down through the old asphalt, you’re being sold a short-life overlay. A reputable contractor will dig—literally—before pricing. Here’s what common symptoms mean and what you’re looking at for repairs:

Asphalt or concrete for a Jackson driveway?

For Jackson homeowners, the choice between asphalt and concrete matters more than it might elsewhere. Lebanon County’s pronounced freeze-thaw cycling and winter lows (12°F design temp, 30-inch frost depth) favor resilient materials. Asphalt flexes with subgrade movement, tolerating heaving and settling without cracking—a clear strength for south-central Pennsylvania’s climate. Properly built, asphalt typically delivers 20–30 years of life before replacement, at a lower upfront cost.

Concrete, though costlier, can last 30–50 years—but only when treated right. In Jackson, winter road salting is common. Concrete suffers from de-icing salt, which causes spalling and surface scaling. If you know you (and anyone who clears your walk) can consistently avoid salt-based products, concrete may work. Most people don’t, so early wear is common here.

Concrete costs more to install, but needs less maintenance (just joint sealing and cleaning) if you avoid salt. Asphalt needs sealcoating every 3–4 years to slow aging. For charm and drainage, pavers or gravel are real choices: pavers cost as much or more than concrete but allow easy repair of isolated spots; gravel is best for long rural drives, offering indefinite life with regular grading and top-dressing, and handles Jackson’s drainage well.

What you need before paving in Jackson

If you’re repaving an existing residential asphalt driveway in Jackson Township without changing its footprint, you generally don’t need a building permit—the county’s approach frees you from unnecessary paperwork in these cases. But if you’re putting in a new driveway or expanding your asphalt or concrete’s area, you will need zoning and building permits through the Lebanon County Planning Department. Even for minor widening, expect to navigate permitting if you increase impervious area, as this can affect local stormwater management and runoff requirements.

The spot that trips up many: the driveway approach where yours meets a public road. For driveways onto state highways in Jackson, you need a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 8. For all other roads, your township issues the approach permit—this is separate from building permits and absolutely not optional.

Stormwater should never run onto neighboring properties—permit or not, doing so creates liability in Lebanon County. Good contractors will always address drainage in their scope, and townships may want to see your runoff plan for new driveways or expansions.

By law, any contract over $500 triggers Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration; verify status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All contractors employing workers must have workers’ compensation—check this at wcais.pa.gov for peace of mind.

Local soil, drainage and driveway lifespan

Jackson Township sits over Lebanon County’s limestone karst, a unique subgrade that shapes every driveway’s future. Limestone karst is notorious for cavities and pockets—settlement and small sinkholes are a real risk unless driveways are built with a deep, well-compacted stone base. Lebanon County’s 30-inch frost depth compounds the threat: freeze-thaw cycles drive surface heaving and shifting, making any shortcut on base construction a gamble.

Older driveways—especially those placed during the 1950–1980 suburban building boom—were often built to minimal standards. Many of these driveways are now at the end of their lifespan, showing failure not just at the surface but well below. Simply sealcoating does little; most need at least partial base reconstruction or full-depth replacement to correct decades of slow movement. Where limestone is shallow, expect more edge and surface settlement than you’d see a county or two away, and demand a thicker base layer when bidding asphalt or concrete work.

Gravel driveways in Jackson need especially careful grading to manage drainage—limestone pockets don’t forgive standing water. Reliable driveways in Jackson demand attention to subsurface drainage as much as what’s visible. Local contractors who know this soil build every job accordingly, aiming for longevity in a place where shortcuts show up early and stay visible for decades.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Jackson

How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in Jackson? In Jackson, due to the limestone karst subgrade and freeze-thaw cycles, it’s smart to insist on a total asphalt thickness of at least 3 inches for residential driveways—typically 2 inches of binder course and 1 inch of top course. Beneath that, the stone base is even more critical; 6–8 inches of compacted crushed stone is common in Lebanon County to avoid future settlement or sinkholes caused by limestone cavities. Some older driveways skimp with thinner sections, but that’s where you’ll see early cracks and dips. Always get the spec in writing—good contractors include a detailed layer breakdown in bids. Full replacement (including new base) usually lands in the $3,500–$9,100 price range for a standard single-family drive.
How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Jackson? Jackson’s climate doesn’t require annual sealcoating, despite what some flyers say. For most driveways, a good interval is every 2–4 years, based on sun exposure and vehicle use. The first sealcoat on new asphalt should be held off until the surface has cured—generally 6 to 12 months after initial paving. Over-sealing, especially annually, can actually build up brittle layers that crack and peel off during our freeze-thaw cycles. When done properly, sealcoating costs $200–$700 per application and helps prevent premature oxidation, but it’s just one part of long-term maintenance. Crack filling and drainage matter even more here than anywhere.
Does Jackson have local asphalt plants? Yes, Jackson is close to several Lebanon County asphalt plants, especially in Lebanon and North Lebanon townships. This has a real impact on your paving project—short-haul aggregate from local quarries means faster installation, better material quality, and more competitive bids. In the rural edges of Jackson, haul costs can add a bit, but nothing like in more remote counties. When contractors name a specific Lebanon supplier, it’s proof they’re not brokering the job out. Good local sourcing keeps prices reasonable—like the typical $3,000 for an asphalt overlay—and ensures you get properly graded aggregate, not trucked-in leftovers.
What does an asphalt overlay cost in Jackson? For Jackson driveways with a sound stone base but a tired, cracked surface, asphalt overlays (resurfacing) run $1,500–$4,600 in 2026. The typical price for a modest single-family driveway sits around $3,000. This covers cleaning, minor patching, and adding 1.5–2 inches of new hot-mix asphalt. It does not solve base or drainage problems—a reputable quote will include probing the old surface first. Resurfacing is only worth it when the foundation is solid, because overlays laid over failing subgrade won’t last more than a few winters in south-central Pennsylvania’s climate.
What are the signs of a failing driveway base? In Jackson, the main visual signs are alligator (network) cracking over wide areas, deep depressions (“birdbaths”) holding water, repeated edge crumbling, and the appearance of new potholes after every freeze-thaw cycle. These symptoms signal that water or poor drainage has compromised the stone base—common in driveways built before base thickness standards improved in Lebanon County. Occasional linear cracks aren't a crisis; wide-pattern cracking and settlement almost always require full replacement, not just patching. If a contractor suggests an overlay for these symptoms, get a second opinion. Expect a cost in the $3,500–$9,100 range if the whole base is compromised.
What does sealcoating cost in Jackson? In Jackson Township, a single correct sealcoat application on a typical residential driveway costs $200–$700. The job should include cleaning, edge work, and two cross coats of sealer—either squeegeed on or sprayed thick. Cheaper “spray specials” under $200 may be thinned, streaky, or leave bare patches that don’t last through a winter. If your driveway is much larger or you have extensive cracks to be filled, costs rise accordingly. Done at sensible intervals—every 2 to 4 years—it’s a small investment for extending the life of sound asphalt, not a cure-all for bigger structural issues.
Should I repair or replace my Jackson driveway? The right answer depends almost entirely on what’s underneath. If you see small, isolated cracks or minor edge crumble on an otherwise solid surface, crack filling or patching ($200–$800) makes sense. But if alligator cracks cover large areas, edges keep breaking, or you have repeated depressions after rain, the base is likely gone. In Jackson, overlays over a bad base rarely survive our freeze-thaw winters. If you’re quoted for a quick overlay but the contractor hasn’t cored through the old asphalt to check the base, be wary. Full replacement may cost more up front ($3,500–$9,100), but it’s the only answer for widespread failure—and lasts decades if done right.
When is the best time of year to pave in Jackson? The best window for driveway paving in Jackson runs from late April through October, when temperatures allow for proper compaction and curing. Hot-mix asphalt needs consistent temps above 50°F to be workable; paving too early or late risks poor bonding, early cracking, or surface raveling during our freeze-thaw season. Concrete work is also best done when overnight lows stay above 40°F, avoiding early freeze damage. Booking early helps, since reputable contractors fill up by midsummer. You’ll get the best value and longest life—like the typical $5,600 full replacement—by working in this sweet spot.
Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door? Yes, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act gives you a three business-day right to cancel any home improvement contract you sign at your Jackson home. This applies even if the contractor is pushy or the signature was collected quickly. If you’re feeling pressured—whether for a quick sealcoat at $400 or a major job—submit your cancellation notice in writing before the three-day window ends. Legitimate Lebanon County pavers will respect the law; those who won’t are best avoided entirely. Never let anyone rush you into a decision, especially for work that costs thousands.
