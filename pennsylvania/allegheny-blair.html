Allegheny paving prices by scope — 2026

In Allegheny, driveways are a real investment for most homeowners — over 72.5% of homes in this area are owner-occupied and more than half are single-family, where each driveway is a separate structure and responsibility. Most paved driveways here have to stand up to the realities of north-central Pennsylvania’s climate: long winters, frequent freeze-thaw cycles, and the kind of subgrade soils found throughout Blair County. The ground here, especially around Hollidaysburg, is mostly limestone and shale, which means you need a solid aggregate base and, quite often, underdrain to prevent frost heave and settlement.

What does that all add up to? Most of the difference you’ll see between two paving quotes is hidden below the surface. Good base preparation, proper thickness, and grading all drive up short-term cost but are what keep a driveway from failing within a few years. Skimping on the invisible work is how jobs fail — and a neat appearance on day one tells you very little.

Why you can't pave in February

Paving in Allegheny and greater Blair County comes with hard seasonal limits. The winter design temp here is just 2 °F, and hot-mix asphalt needs far warmer conditions. Asphalt must be placed and compacted while it’s still hot — if surface temps drop below 45–50 °F, the mix cools too quickly to bond and compact properly, leading to early cracking and a porous, short-lived surface. It simply won’t last through a Blair County freeze cycle.

This isn’t just a risk. The local asphalt plants near Altoona and Duncansville completely shut down for the winter; material supply isn’t available no matter what a contractor claims. Concrete has its own limitations — it can’t cure correctly below freezing, and sudden temperature drops can cause it to crack even before you park on it.

Crews tend to be busiest in the late spring and summer peak, especially in Altoona-area neighborhoods where demand is steady. Shoulder seasons — late April through May, then September through October — are when quality crews can usually fit you in. If a paving outfit offers to repave your driveway in late November or claims to pour new concrete in February, take it as a clear warning: they’re either making up the specs or don’t care if your project fails.

In north-central Pennsylvania, proper scheduling is non-negotiable. Rushed cold-weather paving is the number one way to waste your money.

Diagnosing a failing Allegheny driveway

Your driveway’s problems are almost always symptoms of what’s happening beneath the surface. Most contractors know this, but not every one will be straight with you because the right diagnosis can halve or double the price. Here’s what you can look for yourself in Allegheny:

Alligator cracking — interconnected, blocky cracks — over a large area means the base is failing. An overlay or sealcoat here is throwing good money after bad; those cracks will reappear by next spring. Isolated linear cracks, especially under a quarter inch wide, are surface aging and can be sealed up for a few years more service. Birdbaths and low spots collecting water show that the subgrade or base has settled or heaved — this is common in steep, fill-heavy properties around Altoona and Hollidaysburg. Edge crumbling means your driveway wasn’t built wide enough or doesn’t have any edge support for vehicles. Potholes form where water reaches and weakens the base — they never get smaller on their own.

The rule: overlays and sealcoats can cure surface issues only. If the base or subgrade is failing, skip the quick fixes and invest in a proper rebuild. Any contractor proposing an overlay without checking below the surface hasn’t done their job.

What to put in an Allegheny paving contract

If your goal is to get a driveway that lasts through Blair County winters — and not just looks good for six months — you need to know exactly what you are paying for. A contract that just says “three inches of asphalt” is basically meaningless. Why? “Three inches” as laid is not “three inches” after compaction, and you need to know which you’re getting. The right thicknesses here are not Florida specs — freeze-thaw, steep slopes, and Blair County’s limestone and shale soils mean deeper base and more careful grading.

Always demand a quote that lists these details, in writing, not just a lump-sum price. This is what keeps a new driveway from settling, alligator cracking, or washing out in three winters. The contractor’s spec sheet should include:

  • Excavation depth (inches) down to undisturbed subgrade
  • Aggregate base thickness and gradation (e.g., 8+ inches of 2A stone)
  • Aggregate compaction method and confirmation — compaction in lifts
  • Final compacted asphalt binder course thickness (inches)
  • Final compacted asphalt wearing/surface course thickness (usually 1.5–2 inches here)
  • Exact asphalt mix designations (e.g., PennDOT ID-2 wearing)
  • Edge treatment plan — will asphalt be backed by stone shoulders or curbing?
  • Surface grading and slope for drainage (percent slope specified)
  • Underdrain plan (if needed for poor-drainage soils)
  • Cleanup, debris removal, and warranty terms spelled out in writing

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is the most hyped driveway service, so let’s lay out what it actually does (and does not) do for an Allegheny driveway. The pros: a quality sealcoat protects the surface from sunlight, slows down the oxidation of asphalt binder that leads to brittleness, and helps shed oil and fuel spills before they soak in. It makes a faded drive look fresh. If your base and surface are sound, sealcoating on a 2- to 4-year interval is realistic for this climate.

The cons: sealcoating adds no structural strength at all. It cannot and will not fix cracks, low spots, or base trouble — it is only for surface preservation. Layering on new coat after new coat, especially more than every other year, is actually harmful: it creates a brittle, crack-prone skin that peels off in sheets after one of our freeze-thaw winters. New asphalt needs at least a whole warm season to cure; sealcoating too early can trap solvents and speed up failure.

The real must-do maintenance around here is crack filling — stopping water from going through the surface and working into the base. A proper sealcoat uses a squeegee or brush for coverage; a dirt-cheap spray deal (usually under $175) is nearly always just a thin coat that does nothing. For the typical Allegheny homeowner, budget $175–$650 for a full driveway and don’t do it more than every couple of years.

Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

Every driveway in Allegheny is built from the ground up — and it’s the ground, and what gets put on top of it, that decides how long your driveway will last. Here’s how it works: start with undisturbed subgrade (limestone or shale, often damp in this part of Blair County), then a thickly placed, well-compacted aggregate base, and only then the asphalt or concrete driveway itself. Each of those layers needs to be thick enough and compacted enough to spread out the weight of cars and resist the heaving that freeze-thaw cycles produce in this part of the state.

Frost heave is the silent killer of driveways here. Water trapped in the subgrade freezes, expands, and lifts the pavement — only for it to drop back down, cracked and sunken, when things thaw. For hillside lots and clay-leaning soils (common near Hollidaysburg and the surrounding area), proper drainage and plenty of aggregate are non-negotiable. This is where the job gets cheap in all the wrong ways: shallow excavation, scraping off just the topsoil, skipping underdrain, using too little or poorly graded stone, or failing to compact the base in several thin lifts. These shortcuts cost a little less up front and add a lot of trouble by year three.

Don’t just take a “4-inch” claim at face value. Ask for, in writing: at least 8 inches of compacted 2A modified stone base for asphalt, placed and compacted in lifts, and base compaction verified before paving. Drainage must be called out — look for mention of underdrain, especially on slopes or poor soils. If it’s not in the specs, you’re not getting it.

Permits and approvals for paving in Allegheny

In Allegheny, most routine asphalt repairs and resurfacing that don’t change your driveway’s size or access to the street don’t need a separate building permit — this saves you work if you’re only patching or sealing. But if you’re widening or reconstructing your driveway so it connects differently to a public road or a new spot, expect to need a paving or occupancy permit from your local municipality. For driveways that tie into U.S. 220 near Hollidaysburg, the Highway Occupancy Permit comes not from your borough, but from PennDOT District 9. Local approaches are always handled by the local office, not anyone else.

One thing that often gets missed: making your driveway bigger means more runoff, and Blair County’s long winters and steep grades make drainage a big deal. Improperly directing water onto a neighbor’s property can create headaches no permit will fix, so get runoff handled in the plan and maintain positive drainage in your finished grade.

Any work over $500 must be in a written contract, with a registered Home Improvement Contractor number from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Double-check that anyone putting a crew on your property also carries workers’ compensation, which you can verify through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Blair County is not an area where skipping these steps is worth the risk.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Allegheny

Is gravel a cheaper option in Allegheny? Gravel is almost always cheaper up front for an Allegheny driveway, with installation running $900–$3,500 for most residential jobs. This is especially attractive in the rural sections of Blair County, where long driveways are common and keeping costs down is a real concern. But there’s a tradeoff: gravel surfaces demand regular raking, topping up, and pothole patching, and they can often wash out or rut on the steeper hillside lots that turn up around Hollidaysburg and Altoona. For someone who’s comfortable doing ongoing maintenance, it’s a solid, flexible choice. If you don’t want dust, regular upkeep, or tracking material indoors, investing in a long-lasting asphalt or concrete surface may make more sense, even at a higher up-front price.
What kind of soil is under Allegheny driveways? The subgrade beneath most driveways in Allegheny, especially close to Hollidaysburg, is a mix of limestone and shale. These soils can drain reasonably well but are vulnerable to frost heave if not handled right, thanks to Blair County’s high snow loads and deep freezes. This geology means base preparation is more demanding than it might be west of the Alleghenies. The need for a thicker, well-compacted aggregate base — usually at least eight inches — is tied directly to these soil conditions. If an installer skips on excavation or underdrain, those soils can shift and create dips, cracks, and even settlement within a few freeze-thaw seasons.
What does sealcoating cost in Allegheny? For most residential driveways in Allegheny, professional sealcoating ranges from $175–$650 per application, with the typical job landing near the middle. The wide range takes into account both the total square footage and the level of prep work (like cleaning and crack filling) needed before sealing. If you get a quote that seems much lower than $175, be cautious — it might just be a quick spray that won’t last through the winter. Proper jobs done with squeegee or brush, and paired with attention to cracks, are what actually slow down aging. Don’t let anyone talk you into yearly sealing; every 2–4 years is enough in this climate.
Are pavers worth the cost in north-central Pennsylvania? Pavers can look great and offer some drainage advantages, but in Blair County, especially Allegheny, they come with real added cost and upkeep. Paver driveways can run well above the concrete range — much more than $11,000 for a full installation. Our climate, with its deep freezes, means paver bases must be excavated and built just as robustly as for asphalt, or else frost heave will disrupt and break the pattern. Repairs after a hard winter are almost guaranteed every few seasons unless base and drainage are perfect. For the money, concrete or properly installed asphalt delivers better performance with less fuss. Unless you have a strong design reason or budget flexibility, pavers are rarely the best value in Allegheny.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? For any work over $500 in Allegheny or anywhere else in Pennsylvania, contractors must have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. You can and should look this up at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing. If the contractor brings a crew (not just the owner working alone), workers’ compensation coverage is mandatory by state law — confirm this through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Always get a written contract, as required by the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, and never deal with anyone offering to forgo insurance deductibles — it’s prohibited. Being thorough upfront will save you from legal and financial headaches down the line.
When is the best time of year to pave in Allegheny? The optimal season for paving in Allegheny is late spring through early fall, specifically when day and night temperatures consistently stay above 50 °F. Asphalt must be placed and compacted hot, and local plants in the Altoona area shut down for winter — you can’t even get the material in February or March. Summer is the busiest period for local crews, so shoulder seasons (late April through May, then September through early October) often offer the best scheduling flexibility and pricing. Rushed or late-season jobs risk poor compaction and premature failure. Unless you want to redo your drive again in a couple years, wait until proper weather and supply lines are open, even if it means waiting a bit.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating serve different, equally important roles in driveway maintenance for Allegheny homeowners. Crack filling is a targeted repair, using flexible material to close up cracks (usually under a quarter inch wide) so water can’t penetrate to the base. This slows or stops base deterioration and is the more critical step for extending driveway life in a climate with frequent freeze-thaw. Sealcoating, on the other hand, covers the whole surface with a protective layer to slow oxidation and prevent fuel/oil damage. Crack filling should always be done before a sealcoat. Costs reflect the difference: basic crack patching can be around $200–$800, while full sealcoating is $175–$650. Both together buy more years before you need a full replacement.
What causes driveway cracks in Allegheny? Driveway cracks around Allegheny mostly come from two sources: frost heave and base movement, and simple surface aging. The region’s freeze-thaw cycles are intense, with ground frost reaching down 36 inches most winters. When water in the limestone or shale subgrade freezes and expands, it pushes the pavement up; when it thaws, the surface settles, and you end up with cracks and depressions. Aging asphalt also loses flexibility over time, letting small shrinkage cracks appear, especially if not sealed every few years. Good drainage, a quality base, and timely crack filling are the keys to holding up through Blair County’s winters. If you’re seeing a network of “alligator” cracks, it means the base is giving way underneath — patch fixes won’t hold at that point, and you’re looking at $3,100–$8,100 for a real repair.
How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in Allegheny? For a long-lasting residential driveway in Allegheny, you want at least two layers: a compacted aggregate base and the asphalt itself. The base should be a minimum of 8 inches of 2A modified stone, fully compacted in 2–3 lifts. On top, you want a total of 3–4 inches of asphalt after compaction, usually split between a thicker binder course and a 1.5–2 inch wearing course. This is more than what milder climates need, but the region’s 36-inch frost depth and heavy freeze-thaw cycles demand it. Skimping here almost always leads to early settlement, cracking, and a short service life. Expect to pay in the $3,100–$8,100 range for a job built to these specs, but it’s the difference between getting fifteen years or just five before you’re back to square one.
