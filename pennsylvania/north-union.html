What a new driveway costs in North Union

In North Union Township, where 63.8% of homes are single-family and 73.7% of households are owner-occupied (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), driveway paving is a major investment and a common need. The decision comes up often here since most homes date back to before 1980 and were built at a time when driveways tended to be narrow—sometimes too narrow for today’s vehicles and driving habits. Heavy freeze-thaw cycles in Fayette County, paired with clay and shale subgrade, make driveway integrity a real concern. Most homeowners see the surface, but the real cost difference between two paving quotes is what goes underneath: excavation, base thickness, and compaction. A properly built base handles ground movement and prevents cracks and rutting, which means your driveway will last through Pennsylvania winters.

Surface repairs and overlays exist for driveways with a sound base, but full replacement is needed when there’s structural cracking or base failure. It’s tempting to choose the cheapest quote, but saving money up front on inadequate base prep will nearly always cost you more over time. Finding a legitimate paver in Fayette County

Fayette County’s thin, rural contractor market means homeowners in North Union need to be careful about who they hire. The best defense is verification using Pennsylvania's required Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Any legitimate paver should also carry workers’ comp, checkable at the state’s WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) if they have employees. Look for a permanent business address right in this county, not just a cell phone or a PO box. A reputable paving contractor will own their equipment—rentals are a red flag for smaller, transient outfits. They should also be able to supply references from jobs in North Union or nearby that are at least three years old, since only time reveals the result of their base work.

Another test: ask what asphalt plant or quarry they buy from. Since Fayette County’s suppliers sit in the Monongahela Valley, a contractor unable or unwilling to name theirs is likely just acting as a broker or middleman, not performing the actual work. You want to know exactly who will be onsite and what materials will be used.

Red flags: Out-of-area phone numbers, refusal to name their supplier, requests for large cash deposits, no business address, and reluctance to provide aged references. These nearly always point to poor quality or outright scams.

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Homeowners in North Union see plenty of advertising about sealcoating, but it’s important to know what it does and what it cannot do. Sealcoating protects asphalt from UV rays, slows down oxidation, and provides some resistance to oil and gas spills—the main hazards outside the garage. It also refreshes faded driveways, making them look “like new.” But sealcoating will not add structural strength, fix cracks, prevent new cracks from appearing, or extend the life of a driveway whose base has started to fail. The belief that sealcoating “saves” old asphalt is marketing, not construction science.

Risks come from overuse. When a driveway is sealed every year, layers eventually crack and peel, trapping moisture below rather than protecting. New asphalt should never be sealed until it has cured fully, which in Fayette County’s climate means waiting at least six to twelve months after installation.

Crack filling—using hot or cold pour material to fill gaps before they widen—is more important for longevity than routine sealcoating. A proper crack-fill job cleans, dries, and spaces the material so it moves with ground shifts. Many “bargain” sealcoat jobs in this area are just thin coatings sprayed on without prep work, which wash off after a season or less.

A fair price for a single application on a standard North Union driveway is $175–$600. If you’re quoted far less, expect diluted product sprayed quickly, which does little good and may actually harm the driveway. An honest answer: sealcoating is preventive, low-frequency maintenance—not a universal cure.

Comparing materials in southwestern Pennsylvania

North Union’s climate—with repeated freezing and thawing, and base soils of clay and shale—favors asphalt for most residential driveways. Asphalt’s natural flexibility means it can flex with the cycles of freeze-thaw that are typical in Fayette County. That’s why you’ll see fewer large cracks in blacktop than in concrete—unless the subgrade is failed.

Concrete, on the other hand, is more expensive and lasts longer when it’s not exposed to de-icing salt. The trouble is, our winters and regular road salting mean concrete is prone to spalling and surface scaling. Once that starts, it’s difficult and expensive to repair—especially true on the hillsides above the Monongahela River.

Gravel driveways still make sense for longer rural drives where upkeep is expected, and interlocking paver driveways offer a long-lasting, repairable surface at a much higher upfront cost. The real choice in most North Union neighborhoods is between asphalt’s flexibility and concrete’s hardness—but remember, both depend on correct base preparation, not just surface material.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In North Union and the rest of Fayette County, a building permit is rarely required to resurface or repave a residential driveway in its existing footprint. You save yourself a call on this one unless you are adding square footage or altering the grade. However, changing your driveway’s width, rerouting its path, or—crucially—modifying the approach where your driveway meets a public road does call for a permit. For state roads, this permit is issued not by the township but by PennDOT Engineering District 12-0.

Expanding paved surfaces can increase runoff. If your project adds impervious area, you may be required to address stormwater with additional grading or drainage. Regardless of permitting, homeowners are responsible for making sure runoff does not flow onto a neighbor’s property; doing so can create civil liability.

For any contract over $500, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract and gives you a three-day cancellation period. Before signing, verify the contractor’s HIC registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov) and check their workers’ compensation status at wcais.pa.gov if anyone but the owner will be onsite. Ignoring these checks in Fayette County can cost you more than money.

The specifications to demand in writing

In North Union’s heavy freeze-thaw climate and with our local clay and shale subgrade, you cannot afford to shop by price alone. Base prep in Fayette County is not the same as most of the country: subgrade here demands thicker aggregate, more diligent compaction, and attention to drainage. Every proposal should list, in numbers, what you are buying—not just “three inches of asphalt” but the full build as compacted.

Ask for all depths as “compacted”—not “laid”—since asphalt and stone settle greatly once rolled. The quote should specify excavation, sub-base and base depths, asphalt binder and surface course, and if those are to be installed in multiple lifts. The aggregate should be Pennsylvania DOT spec, local from the Monongahela Valley, and compacted mechanically. Edge treatment—straight-cut or sloped, with or without stone shoulder—should be stated. “Three inches” could refer to one thick lift, which will not last here, or to two layers properly compacted, which will.

  • Excavation depth below existing grade
  • Type and thickness of aggregate base (as compacted)
  • Compaction method and number of passes per lift
  • Asphalt binder course thickness (compacted)
  • Asphalt surface course thickness (compacted)
  • Mix designation (PG binder, gradation)
  • Edge treatment—sloped, shouldered, or reinforced
  • Drainage provisions and slope direction
  • Joint spacing (for concrete or pavers)
  • Minimum temperature and weather conditions for work
  • Cleanup, restoration, and site protection plans

If it’s not in writing, you’re not getting it. In southwestern Pennsylvania, where ground is unforgiving, this is protection, not overkill.

Why driveways behave differently in North Union

North Union’s subgrade is primarily clay and shale, unlike limestone-based soils one county over. Clay holds water and expands when it freezes, lifting driveways and causing “frost heave.” Shale, on the other hand, can provide some drainage, but often shifts over time, especially on hillside properties above the Monongahela River. That means your driveway needs a thicker, well-compacted aggregate base compared to what might work elsewhere, or the asphalt will rut and crack with each season.

Paired with Fayette County’s 36-inch frost depth and severe freeze-thaw swings, a skimpy base almost guarantees failure. In practice, older driveways—especially the standard narrow strips common to the county’s post-WWII ranch homes—were often built to less strict standards, using minimal base over native clay. As cars have gotten wider and homeowners widen the original footprint, these weak sections become stress points.

A reliable new driveway in North Union calls for proper excavation down to stable material, a thick stone base (often 8–12 inches compacted), and detailed drainage planning. Even a solid surface can’t resist the forces of poorly-draining clay and persistent freeze cycles without this foundation. If you’ve had edge breakup, ponding, or “alligatored” cracks, these subsurface factors are almost always to blame—not just what you see on top.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in North Union

What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating are separate maintenance steps for driveways in North Union. Crack filling involves cleaning out and filling isolated cracks (usually under a quarter inch wide) in your asphalt to keep water out and prevent them from growing in our freeze-thaw cycles. Sealcoating is a thin protective layer applied to the driveway’s surface to block UV rays and slow oxidation. Crack filling addresses the actual structural gaps that let water reach the vulnerable base, which is crucial with North Union’s local clay and shale soils. Sealcoating, meanwhile, is more about surface protection and appearance—it does not fix cracks or cure a failing driveway. Expect to pay about $200–$800 for crack filling and patching, and $175–$600 for sealcoating on a typical driveway here. Done right, crack filling is the repair that actually keeps driveways together long-term in Fayette County.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in North Union? The cost to install a new concrete driveway in North Union runs from $4,400–$10,500 depending on size, slope, site access, thickness, and any custom finishes. Most standard two-car driveways built by reputable Fayette County crews average around $7,000. Concrete is more expensive than asphalt up front, but offers a potential lifespan of 30–50 years if de-icing salts are avoided—important in our climate, since salt can cause spalling. Remember, the actual service life depends more on base prep than anything else, especially with our clay and shale soils. Make sure your quote spells out the excavation and base details, not just the surface slab.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes. In North Union and anywhere else in Fayette County, the approach—meaning the apron where your driveway connects to a public road—almost always requires a separate permit if you’re changing width, grade, or drainage. On state roads, this comes from PennDOT Engineering District 12‑0, not the township office. Even if you don’t need a permit for the driveway work itself, you do need this if the apron is touched. Ignoring this rule can mean being required to tear out the work at your own cost. Expect additional inspection steps and a fee. In most cases, the paver you hire should handle this, but make sure it is covered in your contract.
How far ahead should I book a paver in North Union? In North Union, you should contact a reputable paver at least four to eight weeks before you want the work started—longer if you want spring or fall dates, which fill up fastest due to ideal temperatures for asphalt and concrete. Because Fayette County is a rural market between Pittsburgh and Morgantown, contractors here often juggle long travel times and seasonal surges, especially after wet winters. If you wait until your driveway is in crisis or after a big storm, expect to pay at the top end of the range—$3,100–$7,900 for asphalt replacement—with sometimes long delays. Booking early gives you a better shot at both fair pricing and quality workmanship.
How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in North Union? For a new asphalt driveway in North Union, the minimum total compacted thickness should be four inches, set over at least 8–12 inches of compacted aggregate base—more if the native subgrade is particularly wet or clay-heavy. Most reliable local contractors specify two lifts: a binder course (usually 2.5 inches compacted) and a surface course (1.5 inches compacted). This is more than what you might find in other states, but Fayette County’s deep frost line (36 inches) and our clay/shale mix demand it. Thinner sections or single lifts nearly always fail early in this freeze-thaw climate. Full replacement, properly built, falls in the $3,100–$7,900 price range.
Are pavers worth the cost in southwestern Pennsylvania? Pavers can be an excellent investment in southwestern Pennsylvania for homeowners wanting a unique look and a surface that is easier to repair than poured concrete or asphalt. The higher cost—$7,000–$10,500 installed for a typical driveway—reflects not only material, but the involved installation process. Pavers handle freeze-thaw cycles well because each unit can move slightly and be reset if there’s any settling or frost heave, a real advantage in North Union’s soil conditions. Maintenance is mostly about keeping joints sanded and the occasional reset of settled sections. If appearance, flexibility, or the ability to repair small sections easily matter most, pavers deliver. For basic function on a budget, asphalt is more typical here.
What causes driveway cracks in North Union? Most cracks in North Union driveways are triggered by a combination of heavy freeze-thaw cycles and poor drainage in our clay and shale subgrade. Clay traps moisture; as it freezes and expands, it heaves the base and stresses the surface. Older driveways, especially those installed during Fayette County’s post-WWII building boom, were often built with minimal base and have aged into frequent cracking and alligator-skin patterns. Edge cracking is especially common where driveways were narrowed or where runoff isn’t managed. Regular crack filling ($200–$800) slows down this process, but once base failure sets in, resurfacing or replacement is the honest fix.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in North Union typically costs about the same per square foot as a new installation, since you’re not just adding surface but excavating, building new base, and tying into the old drive. For a standard two-car width expansion, expect to spend $1,300–$4,000 for an asphalt overlay (if the existing base is sound) or $3,100–$7,900 for full replacement with base work. Many ranch homes in Fayette County were built with narrow drives in the 1950s–70s, so widening is a common request—just make sure your paver includes base prep details. Cutting corners here means the new section settles or cracks ahead of the rest.
Does road salt damage driveways in North Union? Yes—road salt is especially harsh on concrete in North Union. Salt accelerates surface spalling and scaling, particularly in our heavy freeze-thaw winters. Concrete driveways, though they last longer than asphalt when protected, are prone to damage if salt-based de-icers are applied repeatedly. Asphalt tolerates salt better, but can still be affected over decades. Expect to pay $4,400–$10,500 for a new concrete drive; if you want long life in Fayette County, avoid salt or use sand for traction instead. If you must have concrete, seal the surface routinely and keep salt far from it—easier said than done on hilly or icy drives in this region.
