Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, driveway paving contractors may be required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before anyone starts cutting or excavating, verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious of “storm chasers” or anyone soliciting driveway work door-to-door after storms without a verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits: in most Pennsylvania municipalities, residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit by itself. However, the portion of the driveway that connects to the public road—the driveway approach—often triggers review for access (and sometimes a driveway access permit) through your local municipality and/or PennDOT requirements. If your project involves changing the street edge, curb-cut area, or apron, you should confirm permit needs before excavation.

Water runoff is also regulated. Pennsylvania stormwater rules (commonly referenced under Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. That means contractors should address grading and drainage so water doesn’t pond or redirect toward neighbors.

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. If a crew is uninsured, you can assume risk on your property.

Step-by-step for Folcroft homeowners: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request a certificate of insurance for general liability and auto (if needed), (3) verify workers’ compensation via the WC database, and (4) ask how they will handle any required driveway approach/access review before they begin.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Folcroft driveway?

In Folcroft, Pennsylvania, the freeze-thaw cycle is the defining factor in choosing asphalt versus concrete. Both materials expand and contract, but they fail differently when water gets into cracks and the ground repeatedly freezes and thaws. Asphalt is flexible, which can better tolerate movement and can be a strong choice in residential driveways—particularly when you keep up with maintenance like sealcoating every 2–3 years. Concrete is rigid; it can last decades, but it’s more prone to cracking patterns and spalling when freeze-thaw cycling, road salt, or drainage problems lead to repeated moisture intrusion.

That matters more in Delaware County because many driveways are on older subgrades. With about 91.9% of area homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), it’s common to find driveways that are past their original lifespan or built on stone that no longer drains well. When that’s the case, full replacement is often the right call—whether you choose asphalt or concrete—because an overlay won’t fix a failing base.

Here’s a practical cost example: if your asphalt driveway needs full replacement due to severe cracking and edge settlement, you may land in the $5,500–$14,000 range for asphalt replacement. Concrete replacement is commonly $7,000–$18,000. The higher upfront cost can be worth it when you want a “set it and maintain it minimally” approach—provided drainage is corrected and the base is built properly. If your base is borderline and you need to prioritize repair first, asphalt is often the more forgiving option—especially when paired with sealcoating and proper crack treatment.

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in Folcroft?

For Folcroft driveways, sealcoating is a preventive maintenance step—not a fix for failed base. A typical schedule is every 2–3 years if the asphalt is serviceable (no widespread potholes, major base failure, or deep structural cracking). Sealcoating costs commonly run about $350–$1,200 for a standard driveway, depending on size, preparation, and number of coats.

The timing matters because sealcoating must be applied when temperatures and surface conditions are right. Pennsylvania freeze-thaw cycles can open up minor cracks, so you want to catch the driveway when the surface is clean and dry enough for proper adhesion. If your driveway has hairline cracking only, that’s usually the ideal window for sealcoating plus crack filling. If you’re seeing ruts, repeated potholes, or settlement at the edges, you’re likely looking at replacement (or at least a resurfacing/overlay assessment), because sealcoating won’t correct movement underneath.

Your contractor should recommend the best approach after inspecting drainage and crack severity, since water intrusion is the main reason asphalt fails early in Delaware County winters.

What causes driveway cracks in Folcroft?

Driveway cracks in Folcroft are usually caused by a mix of moisture and movement—especially in Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle. Water can infiltrate through fine cracks or poor edge joints, freeze underneath, and expand, which pushes pavement upward and widens cracking over time. In older homes (many built before 1980), it’s also common to find base layers that were not originally built to modern drainage and compaction standards, which increases settlement.

Typical local contributors include: inadequate slope that allows ponding, undersized or poorly compacted stone base, and inconsistent patchwork repairs that create weak spots. Temperature swings also play a role—materials contract and expand, but they last longer when they’re installed over a properly prepared, compacted base.

How cracks are addressed depends on severity. Minor cracks under about 1/4 inch may be treated with crack filling and patching only, often priced around $200–$800 per application for smaller sections. But if cracking is widespread, linked to settlement, or accompanied by heaving/rutting, maintenance won’t be enough—you may need full asphalt replacement in the $5,500–$14,000 range.

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

In Pennsylvania, asphalt thickness depends on whether you’re installing new pavement or resurfacing an existing driveway, and—most importantly—what the base condition is. For Folcroft homes, the freeze-thaw cycle makes base preparation a key part of performance. A driveway installed on a weak subgrade or shallow base will fail early no matter what thickness is used on top.

As a general planning guide, asphalt resurfacing/overlay projects are often designed with a thinner asphalt lift when the base is structurally sound (that’s one reason resurfacing can land in the $2,800–$7,000 band). Full replacement requires more complete reconstruction and commonly includes a thicker asphalt system—driving many projects into the $5,500–$14,000 range—particularly when excavation and recompaction are needed.

The right answer for your driveway is determined after an inspection and, when warranted, measurement of base depth and assessment of settlement or ponding. A reputable contractor will discuss thickness as part of a system: compacted stone base + proper drainage + appropriate asphalt lift.
