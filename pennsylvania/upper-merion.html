Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, the key regulatory starting point for most homeowners is contractor licensing. If the work exceeds $500, paving contractors typically must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify credentials at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious of “storm chasers” who solicit driveway work door-to-door after severe weather without a verifiable HIC registration—no license verification is a major red flag.

Permits: many residential driveway paving projects do not require a building permit within most PA municipalities, but the driveway approach (where your driveway meets the public road) may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. In practice, the apron/approach area is where approvals, bonding, and inspection requirements can add time—especially around the street interface. Always confirm before excavation.

Water runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater expectations under Chapter 102 mean the project should not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Proper grading and drainage solutions help prevent water from pooling and then damaging the pavement through freeze–thaw.

Workers’ compensation: verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose you to liability.

Step-by-step for Upper Merion homeowners: (1) verify HIC registration online; (2) request a Certificate of Insurance and confirm general liability is current; (3) verify workers’ compensation at wcais.pa.gov; (4) ask whether any street-approach permits or inspections apply to your specific driveway entrance and get the plan in writing.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Upper Merion driveway?

In Upper Merion, Pennsylvania, the freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor for many homeowners choosing asphalt versus concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible and typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better when the base is built correctly and the pavement has enough thickness. Concrete is rigid; it can be an excellent long-term surface, but it can spall or crack under heavy freeze–thaw cycling and road salt exposure if the base and drainage aren’t right.

Maintenance is where the difference shows up. Asphalt driveway care usually includes sealcoating every 2–3 years to protect the binder from oxidation and to keep the surface water-resistant. Concrete generally requires less routine “coating,” but it may need occasional crack repair treatments to prevent water intrusion into the slab edges. Because many Upper Merion homes are older—about 60% built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—driveways often reach or exceed expected service life, which makes full replacement more common than resurfacing.

Here’s a practical dollar example: if your asphalt driveway can be rebuilt and overlaid/resurfaced appropriately, many homeowners fit into the resurfacing budget band around $2,400–$9,000. If inspections show base failure or widespread alligator cracking, the full replacement band (about $4,500–$16,000) becomes the realistic path—and that’s often where asphalt and concrete comparisons tighten because the “new base” cost is already in motion. If you’re starting from a structurally failing section, the material choice becomes less about curb appeal and more about how you want to manage maintenance over time in a Pennsylvania winter climate.
