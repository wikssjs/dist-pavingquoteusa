Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In Sharon Hill, most residential driveway repaving jobs do not require a building permit if you are not changing the driveway’s size or footprint. This holds true across much of Delaware County: repaving or resurfacing an existing residential driveway can usually proceed without trips to the borough office, which saves time and hassle. However, the driveway approach—the portion that meets the public road—is a separate matter. For driveways that connect to a state road, a Highway Occupancy Permit is needed from PennDOT District 6. If the approach is on a local street, the relevant township’s permit process applies. Don’t skip this step: working without it can trigger fines or force you to redo work.

Adding width or length, or turning lawn into paving, increases your property’s impervious area and may trigger stormwater management rules—especially if the extra runoff flows toward the street or a neighbor’s property. Even if you’re within code, directing water onto someone else’s lot can make you liable for damages if it causes problems after heavy rain.

When hiring a paving contractor for projects over $500, verify their Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. All legitimate contractors in Pennsylvania must carry workers’ compensation insurance—confirm this for your safety through the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Written contracts are mandatory in this market under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, and you have a three business-day right to cancel any agreement over $500.

Getting thirty years out of a driveway

The biggest bargains in extending a Sharon Hill driveway’s life are never the most expensive. Start with crack filling—getting new sealant into cracks before water enters the base is the move that wards off deep failures. After that, keep the driveway edges supported with compacted soil or stone; exposed edges crumble first. Direct water away, not just off the surface but also from where the driveway meets the lawn, gutter, or walkway. Parking point loads—like dumpsters or trailers—on boards instead of right on the surface will prevent local sinking and denting. Any new asphalt needs time: don’t park for 3–7 days, and avoid sharp turns while stationary to reduce early scuffing or rutting.

De-icing practices make a difference in southeastern Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw climate. On concrete, salt can speed spalling and surface flaking; if you must melt ice, choose calcium or magnesium chloride and use sparingly, accepting the extra cost because the alternative is expensive repairs. On asphalt, salt doesn’t attack the pavement, but it triggers freeze-thaw cycles—the problem comes from water working into cracks, then expanding under the surface as it freezes. Using sand or cat litter costs less and won’t damage the pavement, though you’ll need to sweep it up come spring. Sharon Hill’s winters are moderate by Pennsylvania standards but always plan for at least one or two cycles of refreeze each season.

Sharon Hill's ground and what it means for paving

Under Sharon Hill’s driveways, glacial till and compacted urban fill dominate, left from decades of construction and redevelopment in Delaware County’s inner-ring suburbs. Glacial till—a dense, mixed deposit of stone, clay, and silt—drains much more slowly than coarse sand or gravel, holding water against the base. Urban fill introduces unpredictable layers—sometimes rubble, sometimes clay, often with poor drainage. These subgrade conditions mean that a shallow stone base won’t last long: more depth, heavier compaction, and solid drainage are non-negotiable for any driveway meant to outlast Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycles and 30-inch frost depth.

Older driveways here, especially those dating from the big building wave between 1920 and 1960, were rarely installed with today’s standards for base prep or water management. Many have thin, uneven, or even absent stone beneath the surface—one reason so many Sharon Hill driveways show edge failures or chronic settling. If you’re resurfacing, it pays to investigate what’s under your current pavement before signing a contract. For new installations, most local contractors will recommend at least six inches of compacted aggregate base for asphalt (more for concrete), especially on problem soils, to keep frost heave and rutting at bay. Skip this, and it’s only a matter of time before the driveway will need expensive correction.

What a real paving quote contains

Comparing driveway quotes in Sharon Hill only works if you’re comparing real details. A good quote starts with the measured square footage—not a guess, but a stated number—and describes how deep excavation will go, with disposal of old material included. It spells out the type and thickness (in inches) of aggregate base, and how that base will be compacted (in lifts, not just rolled once). For asphalt jobs, you should see two courses named—binder and surface—with their compacted depths listed. Attention to edge support, slope, and the water’s direction is standard; if it’s missing, move on. Cleanup is part of the job, as is a restoration plan for lawn edges or sidewalk gaps. Payment should be a modest deposit, with the bulk due after completion. Warranty matters, but know that most formal paving warranties don’t cover cracking—just loose stone, major settling, or raveling in the first year or two.

A one-line quote (“Asphalt paving: $5,300”) with no specifications can’t be compared to anything else. This is how most owners overpay for underbuilt work. Get the details, or keep looking.

Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

The foundation under your Sharon Hill driveway is what determines if you get 30 years—or just a few good ones—before failures begin. Here is how the assembly works from the ground up: First is the subgrade, the natural ground or fill that’s compacted and shaped. On top of this sits the aggregate base (often called “crushed stone” or “2A modified”), compacted in 2–3 inch lifts to a finished depth of at least six inches for asphalt. This base, not the blacktop, spreads the load and prevents sinking. The final layer is the asphalt, laid as a binder course then a surface course, also compacted separately. For concrete, the principle’s identical—only the slab is poured, not paved. Each layer’s job is to distribute weight evenly to the one below it, so a thick, beautiful asphalt mat over a thin, muddy base will break up long before its time.

With Sharon Hill’s glacial till and compacted fill soils, freeze-thaw cycles amplify every base flaw. Water pooled in the base freezes, expands, and lifts the slab (“frost heave”), cracking the pavement from underneath and leaving it uneven for good. That’s why you can’t cheat on base thickness, aggregate compaction, or drainage here the way contractors might in milder climates. Shortcuts in excavation or base depth—like overlaying new asphalt on a broken base, or compacting stone in one thick, loose layer—can drop your installed cost, but don’t be fooled. The problems will show up in year three, not on day one, when the top looks perfect but it’s already breaking up underneath.

Before you sign any contract, insist that the base depth (in inches), aggregate type (2A modified or similar), and that compaction will be done in separate lifts, are all spelled out in writing. That’s what protects you from the shortcuts you’ll never see—but will eventually pay for.

Drainage: the failure nobody plans for

No matter how nice the finish, water control is where most Sharon Hill driveways fail first. The goal is a minimum slope of 2% (a quarter inch per foot) to carry water off the surface—either shed to both sides (crowning), or sloped to one side (cross-slope). The worst scenario is a driveway sloped toward your house or garage, which sends every rainfall under doors or into basements. Where slopes can’t be corrected, trench or channel drains—set into the pavement—intercept runoff and pipe it to a safer outlet. If the site allows, “daylighting” that pipe to the curb or yard edge is best; otherwise, a dry well can hold the water until it soaks in.

Local soil matters: Sharon Hill’s glacial till and urban fill hold water close to the surface, so drainage design can’t be skipped. Water trapped in these soils will keep the base saturated, leading to frost heave as soon as winter swings back and forth. Ponded water on or near the driveway, even for a few hours, nearly guarantees early surface failures. That’s why responsible contractors build the right slope from day one, even if it means more excavation or adding drains.

Lastly, Pennsylvania property owners are liable for damage if runoff is redirected onto a neighbor’s lot—regardless of what the permit says. Good drainage design keeps you and your neighbors out of trouble, now and years from now.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Sharon Hill

Does road salt damage driveways in Sharon Hill?

In Sharon Hill, the answer depends on your driveway surface. Road salt doesn’t chemically attack asphalt, but the repeated freeze-thaw cycling it causes can worsen surface cracking by refreezing meltwater inside the pavement. For concrete driveways, salt is more problematic—it accelerates spalling, which is the flaking and breaking seen on older slabs across Delaware County. If you must use a de-icer, calcium or magnesium chloride are safer for concrete, though they’re pricier than traditional rock salt. Avoid fertilizer-based products, which can also stain and degrade surfaces. If your driveway is already showing minor cracks or areas of wear, annual sealcoating can help ($200–$700 per application), but regular plowing and gentle shoveling remain the best ways to handle Sharon Hill’s moderate winters without risking permanent damage.

What does an asphalt overlay cost in Sharon Hill?

An asphalt overlay, also called resurfacing, is often the best solution in Sharon Hill if your base is sound and the main issue is surface wear or shallow alligator cracking. For most driveways in the borough, the cost runs between $1,450–$4,300, with the average closer to $2,900. It’s important that overlays are only used on driveways without significant settling or soft spots, because those problems will come back through the new layer in just a few seasons, especially with our local freeze-thaw cycles. Always have the contractor inspect the base’s firmness and drainage before choosing this route. In many Sharon Hill neighborhoods, overlays are a good stopgap, but if your home falls in that majority built before 1980, budget for a possible full base replacement within a decade.

Can I pave over my existing driveway?

In many Sharon Hill properties, paving over your existing asphalt—called an overlay—can work, provided the base isn’t failing and you aren’t dealing with deep cracks or settling. This is a budget-friendly way to refresh a tired driveway, with costs in the local range of $1,450–$4,300. However, if the underlying stone is thin, gaps have formed, or you see serious depressions, overlaying just covers up issues that will reappear soon—wasting the investment. With so many driveways here laid on older glacial till or urban fill, and built decades ago, most contractors will recommend a closer inspection before proceeding. Remember, if you add height to the surface, you may also create drainage or slope problems, so make sure the plan doesn’t send water toward your house or neighbor.

How much does it cost to widen a driveway?

Widening a driveway in Sharon Hill usually involves more than just adding asphalt or concrete—you’re increasing impervious area, which can trigger new permits or stormwater requirements. If you’re extending an existing surface and preparing a proper base, expect per-square-foot pricing that falls within the full installation range (for asphalt: $3,400–$8,700; for concrete: $4,800–$11,500). For example, adding one car width to a typical 35-foot-long driveway usually runs an additional $1,500–$3,500 depending on site access and base work required. Always check with the local code office about permit needs (especially for larger additions), and consider how much water the new hard surface will send into your yard or the street.

How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania?

In Pennsylvania, and specifically in Sharon Hill, contractors working on driveways for more than $500 must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC). You can check a contractor’s registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, which lets you verify license status, insurance, and consumer complaints. All employers must provide workers’ compensation coverage for their crews—this can be double-checked by searching the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). For work that involves cutting into public roads or sidewalks, make the contractor show permits from PennDOT or the township office as proof they have legal authority for approaches and aprons. Remember, with the saturated Philadelphia metro market, you have choices, and there’s no need to settle for someone who won’t produce paperwork. Written contracts are required for any job over $500; don’t proceed without one.

How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Sharon Hill?

With Sharon Hill’s moderate freeze-thaw climate and summer humidity, a single sealcoat application on asphalt should last two to four years. Too-frequent sealing can lead to buildup and peeling, so timing matters. For most homeowners, every three years strikes a balance between protection and cost (with each sealcoating running $200–$700 for a typical driveway). Look for fading, hairline cracks, or a brittle surface as signs that a new coat is needed. Older driveways, especially those built on compacted fill or glacial till, will benefit the most since sealing keeps water from penetrating cracks and eroding the base. Don’t sealcoat new asphalt for the first 6–12 months; let it cure fully for best adhesion.

What causes concrete to spall or flake in Sharon Hill?

Concrete driveways in Sharon Hill are prone to spalling—surface flaking or peeling—because of repeated freeze-thaw cycles and the use of de-icing salts in winter. When water seeps into tiny concrete pores and freezes, it expands, breaking down the surface layer over time. Salts accelerate this by drawing more water into the slab and attacking weak bonds. Problems begin showing up a few years after installation if the mix was too wet, finished in cold weather without curing blankets, or poured right over poorly drained glacial till. Repairs usually involve resurfacing, with costs sometimes approaching new installation ($4,800–$11,500), so in old driveways, prevention is cheaper than repair. Avoid harsh salts, repair cracks early, and divert runoff to extend your concrete’s life.

When is the best time of year to pave in Sharon Hill?

For Sharon Hill, late spring through early fall—generally May through September—is the best window for paving or sealing driveways. The warmer temperatures (above 50°F and staying that way overnight) allow asphalt and concrete to cure properly before the freeze-thaw cycles begin again. Humid summer weather isn’t a problem for the materials but can extend cure times by a day or two, so factor that in if you need to drive on the new surface quickly. Trying to pave late in the season risks surface raveling, slow curing, and substandard compaction—not worth the gamble for an expense in the $3,400–$8,700 range. Booking earlier also helps avoid material shortages and contractor backlogs common at the end of the year in Delaware County.

What is the difference between binder and surface course?

Binder course is the lower, thicker layer of asphalt—laid first and built for strength—not looks. It uses a coarser aggregate mix to absorb the flexing and weight of vehicles, spreading the load across your Sharon Hill driveway’s base. The surface course, also called the “wearing” or “top” course, uses finer aggregates and more asphalt cement for a smoother, tighter finish. This is what you see and walk on. Both layers are compacted separately; skipping one or thinning out either is a common way to cut initial costs (sometimes getting a “bargain” at $1,450–$4,300 with an overlay). For new installs, insist the quote lists both layers with their compacted thickness: typically 2–2.5 inches for binder and 1–1.5 inches for surface, on top of a properly prepared base.
