Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, the paving contractor you hire must be properly registered if the job exceeds $500. Many paving projects fall under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) rules, administered through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Also be cautious about “storm chasers”: door-to-door contractors after hail, heavy rain, or wind events who can’t provide verifiable PA HIC registration are a significant red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most Pennsylvania municipalities, but the driveway approach—where your driveway ties into the public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. Check before excavation, especially if you’re changing curb lines, the apron, or the connection to a roadway.

Runoff rules matter. Under PA stormwater regulations (Chapter 102), paving projects should not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or into public rights-of-way. A reputable contractor will handle grading properly and may include drainage corrections where needed.

Liability comes down to insurance. Verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. To verify credentials step-by-step, (1) check HIC registration online, (2) request a certificate of insurance showing general liability and auto coverage if applicable, (3) confirm workers’ comp via the WC registry, and (4) ensure the proposed scope matches what’s listed on the contracts and receipts.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your South Londonderry driveway?

In South Londonderry, Pennsylvania, the freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor when choosing asphalt versus concrete. Asphalt and concrete both move a little with temperature swings, but they fail in different ways under Central PA conditions. Asphalt is flexible and generally handles freeze–thaw movement better, but it needs routine protection—typically sealcoating every 2–3 years—so the binder doesn’t oxidize and crack faster. Concrete is rigid and long-lasting, but under heavy freeze–thaw cycling and road salt exposure, it can spall, heave, or crack, especially where drainage isn’t ideal or where the slab base was underbuilt.

Local housing age plays into your odds of needing full replacement. With about 39.8% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways are already at or beyond the typical life of their original base. That often means the safest “right now” choice is full-depth asphalt replacement (commonly $4,500–$11,000) or a concrete install when you want a long-term surface—rather than chasing longevity with an overlay.

Here’s a practical example: if your driveway has alligator cracking and heaving near the apron, an overlay is often false economy; paying for overlay in the $2,500–$7,000 range can lead to another rebuild sooner. If the driveway base is sound and the damage is mainly surface cracking, resurfacing (often $2,500–$7,000) can be justified and cheaper than a full replacement. If you’re trying to decide today, the best question isn’t “which material is better?”—it’s “does my base need to be rebuilt for Pennsylvania freeze–thaw?”

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes—not just a lump sum. A good quote breaks out labor and materials: excavation/removal, base stone type and depth, asphalt tonnage and thickness (or concrete mix/stone base), compaction steps, edging/apron work, and drainage/grading. Read the scope for exclusions (for example: “no drainage correction” or “existing base assumed stable”). Make sure insurance claim documentation is included if your driveway ties into an insurance scenario, and confirm what happens if hidden base issues are found.

Warranty matters. Ask for workmanship warranty length and how long materials are guaranteed. For payment, never pay more than about 10–15% upfront, and hold back retainage until the job is complete and defects are corrected. Finally, request an arrival estimate and a completion window in writing, since spring–fall lead times in Lebanon County are often 3–8 weeks in busy periods.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in South Londonderry

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

In South Londonderry and across Lebanon County, asphalt thickness is designed around the freeze–thaw cycle and the driveway’s base. For a full-depth asphalt driveway replacement, many residential projects use about 2.5–3 inches of finished asphalt, placed in two lifts, over a properly compacted stone base (often 6–8 inches of well-compacted aggregate when rebuild is needed). If you’re resurfacing an existing driveway, the thickness is typically less—around a 1.5–2 inch overlay—but only when the base is structurally sound and cracking is limited to the surface. A contractor should evaluate drainage, edge condition, and whether you have base pumping or heaving at the apron; those are signals that you may need full replacement rather than thin overlay. If you tell a paving company you want thickness “based on the look,” you can still end up with premature failure. A properly designed thickness and base rebuild is what keeps water from turning into winter damage.

Full replacement projects that include excavation, removal, base rebuild, and drainage/grading can take longer, sometimes stretching to 3–5 days for complete prep and placement. What are the signs of a failing driveway base in South Londonderry?

Failing base is usually the reason “pavement problems” keep coming back after patching in Pennsylvania. In South Londonderry, look for alligator cracking that’s spreading across the wheel paths, edge heaving near the street apron or where the driveway meets a walkway, and areas that pump fines or get muddy/soft after rain. If you notice low spots that hold water, that’s a strong indicator the grading and drainage aren’t working—standing water plus freeze–thaw can destabilize the base. You may also see repeated patches that crack at the seams, or the asphalt breaking up in a pattern that suggests movement from underneath rather than simple surface aging.

A reputable contractor will evaluate these clues before recommending sealcoating or resurfacing. Sealcoating (often $350–$900 per standard driveway) and crack filling are preventive maintenance—not structural fixes—so they’re not appropriate when the base is pumping. If the base is unstable or the asphalt is badly cracked through, full replacement is usually more cost-effective than overlay because it rebuilds the system that freeze–thaw is attacking.
