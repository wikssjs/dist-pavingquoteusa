Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors generally must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before you sign anything in Limerick, verify the contractor’s HIC status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Door-to-door “storm chase” activity after heavy weather is a major concern—only proceed if the company can provide verifiable PA HIC registration.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most Pennsylvania municipalities, but the driveway approach where your driveway meets the public road may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or a local municipal permit. That’s especially important if your project touches the apron, changes the slope, or alters drainage near the right-of-way. Also, some municipalities (including parts of Montgomery County) may have inspection or bonding requirements that add modest time.

Stormwater: Pennsylvania’s Chapter 102 stormwater rules require that paving work not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should address grading and drainage so water flows as intended. If your driveway slopes toward a neighbor’s yard, the fix may include grading adjustments and/or drainage solutions.

Workers’ compensation: verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews on your property can expose you to liability.

Step-by-step, start with the PA HIC online registry, then request a current certificate of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation. Ask for dates to match the work window, and confirm your contractor’s license/registration details before work begins.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Limerick driveway?

In Limerick, the freeze–thaw cycle is what usually determines whether asphalt or concrete is the better long-term move. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, which helps it better tolerate freeze–thaw movement when it’s built on a properly compacted base and designed with adequate thickness. Concrete is rigid; when subgrade or drainage issues let water move under slabs, concrete can crack or spall—especially with winter conditions and road salt exposure common across Montgomery County.

Local housing age matters here too. With about 19.7% of homes built before 1980, many driveways were originally constructed with older site assumptions: thinner base, less mature drainage, and more settlement over time. That’s why full replacement is often the realistic choice rather than a simple resurfacing when you have alligator cracking, heaving, or a contaminated base.

Sealcoating is where asphalt planning becomes important. When your asphalt still has structural integrity (tight surface, limited cracking, no base failure), sealcoating every 2–3 years is mainly protective and can stretch service life. Asphalt resurfacing typically adds about +8–15 years when the base is sound, while full replacement aims for a 20–30 year target in the right build conditions.

Concrete can make sense when you want lower day-to-day maintenance and are willing to address drainage correctly at the start. But if your driveway has ponding, salt exposure, or marginal base, asphalt often gives you better “flex” performance after repairs. A common decision point: if resurfacing at $2,400–$9,000 is being offered but the base shows contamination or movement, that cheaper path may cost more later. Spending toward full replacement in the $4,500–$16,000 range can be justified when it prevents repeated patch-and-overlay cycles.
