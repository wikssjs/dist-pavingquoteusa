Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

Ask any Dormont contractor with a few winters under their belt, and they’ll tell you: it’s never just the asphalt. The entire structure underneath is what makes or breaks your driveway. Here’s how it goes together under southwestern Pennsylvania conditions. First, you’ve got your subgrade—often heavy clay or shale on local hills—graded and compacted. Above that sits 6 to 8 inches of compacted crushed limestone aggregate, acting as the main load distributor. Only then does the asphalt go down, typically 3 to 4 inches, compacted hot.

Why so much base? Our region’s freeze-thaw cycles turn water trapped in the subgrade into ice, expanding as much as 9% by volume. That heaves the whole drive, cracking it from below, and doesn’t return to flat when it thaws. If the stone base is too thin or poorly compacted, that frost heave lifts and breaks the surface after just a few years—especially on Dormont’s common clay, which drains poorly and holds every drop.

This is why price differences between contractors usually come down to what you can’t see. The cheapest jobs use thinner stone, skip excavation, or lay new asphalt over a failed base. You won’t notice on paving day, but you’ll pay for it in year three when cracks and settling appear.

If you want the job to last here, demand these specifics in writing:

  • Minimum 6 inches compacted aggregate base (8 inches on clay is better)
  • 2A limestone or approved DOT aggregate for the base, not recycled concrete fill
  • Base installed in lifts (layers) and each lift compacted separately
  • Final asphalt thickness after compaction: at least 3 inches residential, 4 for two-car drives or heavy use
That’s the difference between a surface that lasts decades and a repair cycle every few years.

Diagnosing a failing Dormont driveway

If your Dormont driveway is showing signs of age or distress, a quick look can tell you a lot before you ever call a contractor. Start by looking at patterns. “Alligator” cracking—an interconnected web across broad areas—almost always means the base has failed. Resurfacing here is money burned; the cracks will return. If you see clean, straight cracks under a quarter inch wide, that’s usually just surface drying, which can be sealed and maintained.

Are there low spots or “birdbaths” where water pools? That’s subgrade movement, usually from frost heave on our local clay or shale. Crumbling along the driveway edges points to insufficient support and needs to be rebuilt with proper edge compaction—or even a curb. If you find potholes, water has gotten below; patching may plug the hole, but a full-depth repair or replacement is probably next.

Anyone quoting an overlay without first probing or boring to check your base’s real condition is skipping the most crucial step. Base failure can’t be seen from the surface, but you’ll see the result soon enough if it’s ignored.

How often should a Dormont driveway be sealed?

Sealcoating has become a standard maintenance suggestion for Dormont asphalt driveways, but it’s important to know what it can—and can’t—do. A proper sealcoat guards against surface oxidation, slows down water, salt, and chemical damage, and revives faded color, making your drive look new. But it does not add any real structural strength. If your driveway base has failed, sealcoating won’t help. Likewise, cracks wider than a hairline need to be filled first, since sealant can’t bridge gaps or restore lost support.

What many contractors don’t say: sealcoating too often is a mistake. Layers can build up and start to peel or flake, especially if done annually. New asphalt needs at least 90 days—and typically a full warm season—to cure before sealcoating, or you can trap volatile oils and hurt the pavement’s longevity.

The right sealcoat interval in Dormont’s climate is usually every 3–4 years, not every season. Before any sealcoat, invest in thorough crack filling; in a freeze-thaw region like southwestern Pennsylvania, that’s what really keeps water from breaking your drive apart. A quality job lays down sufficient sealant (not a watery spray), fills cracks first, and avoids the edges unless cleaned thoroughly. The best work runs $200–$700 per application—if someone offers to do it for much less, it’s likely just water and dye, and won’t last through winter.

Permits and approvals for paving in Dormont

If you’re planning to repave or resurface your existing Dormont driveway and you’re not expanding the footprint or changing the connection to the street, a building permit is often not required—at least in much of Allegheny County, and there’s nothing in public right-of-way unless you alter the approach. But any work affecting the curb, sidewalk, or apron where your drive meets the public street (the “approach”) typically requires a permit from the Department of Mobility and Infrastructure in Pittsburgh or the appropriate local authority in other boroughs. For driveways connecting to state routes, a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 11-0 is mandatory.

Expanding your driveway or adding impervious surface (beyond what was there before) can trigger stormwater controls—a real concern given Dormont’s hilly layout and clay soils. Even with all proper permits, you are still liable if you redirect stormwater onto a neighbor’s property or public land. Always ensure that water is managed on site or directed to approved storm drains.

Before any contract over $500, Pennsylvania law requires HIC registration (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov), proof of workers’ compensation coverage (via WCAIS), and a written contract outlining your three-day right to cancel.

Where the water goes

In Dormont, nothing shortens a driveway’s life faster than poor drainage. Water left to sit or soak into the base is the start of heaving, potholes, and crack cycles. Every new driveway needs at least a 2% slope (a quarter inch per foot) to shed water away from the house and garage—never toward them. On local lots with little room for runoff, crowning the drive (center slightly higher than the edges) or setting a gentle cross-slope works best, so water finds daylight and not your basement.

If your driveway slopes down toward the home or the street, trench or channel drains—set at the garage threshold or apron—can catch runoff and route it to daylight or a dry well. Dormont’s hills and heavy clay mean water won’t percolate quickly, so soakaways or local inlets must be properly sized. Never direct your runoff directly onto your neighbor’s lot; Allegheny County’s topography and dense housing make runoff disputes common grounds for legal trouble.

Remember, freeze-thaw is relentless here: any water trapped in the base expands in winter, cracking and pushing up the surface. Proper grading, an adequate stone base, and surface sealing after cracks are filled all combine to keep water moving out and away, not lingering for weeks after a storm. The extra attention to drainage in Dormont isn’t just best practice—it’s what prevents your new driveway from failing in half the time it should last.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Dormont

Is sealcoating a scam? Sealcoating is not a scam—if done properly and at the right intervals. In Dormont, sealcoating adds protection against the elements, slowing oxidation and guarding the binder against oil and salt. But it does not add structural strength or fix major cracks. If your driveway needs full replacement, sealcoating can only hide the symptoms for a season or two. The right job costs $200–$700 and should include thorough crack filling, edge cleaning, and a proper squeegee or full-strength spray coat. Be wary of anyone offering a suspiciously low price or recommending it every year; layering will eventually peel or flake. Good sealcoating extends surface life but can't cure base failure.
Is gravel a cheaper option in Dormont? Gravel is the most affordable driveway surface in Dormont, especially for long or steep runs, with installation running $900–$3,500 depending on size. It’s commonly used on rural and semi-rural properties in Allegheny County, but less often in Dormont’s denser neighborhoods. Local shale and clay means frequent regrading is needed, and winter plowing can push the stone off to the sides. Gravel works if you need a quick, budget solution or for a secondary parking area, but expect dust, tracking, and annual maintenance. For small lots or sloped approaches, asphalt or concrete remains a better value for most.
What does compaction in lifts mean? Compaction in lifts means the base stone for your Dormont driveway is installed in layers, usually 3–4 inches at a time, and compacted thoroughly before the next layer goes on. On Allegheny County’s clay and shale, this step is not optional: it ensures all voids are closed, preventing settlement and water infiltration that lead to frost heave. A single, thick dump of stone can never be compacted right, inviting ruts and cracking. When you see a quote for full replacement—$3,400–$8,800—make sure it states compaction will be done in lifts, not just “rolled once.”
Can I pave over my existing driveway? Paving over an existing driveway, called an overlay, can be a sound option in Dormont only if your current base is intact—no widespread cracks, heavy depressions, or potholes. If the base is failing, an overlay is just a band-aid that won’t last through our freeze-thaw cycles. A proper overlay for a typical drive runs $1,450–$4,400. Have a contractor test the base with a core sample or borings before going ahead. If they suggest resurfacing without examining the structure beneath, move on to someone else.
How far ahead should I book a paver in Dormont? In Dormont, peak paving season runs from late spring through early fall. Reputable contractors can book up weeks or even several months ahead, especially after a rough winter or wet spring. For a full replacement, which costs $3,400–$8,800, you’ll want to secure a spot at least two to three months in advance to get the best crews and avoid being rushed into decisions. Last-minute deals often go to traveling crews, which may not stand behind their work. Early planning also lets you check references and get the best window for installation—ideally with several dry days.
Does road salt damage driveways in Dormont? Yes, road salt is hard on Dormont driveways—particularly concrete. Salt can accelerate surface spalling, causing the top layer to flake, and it weakens the binder in asphalt over time. Our frequent freezes and thaws compound this, as salt pulls moisture into the surface which then expands and cracks with winter cycles. Regular sealcoating ($200–$700) can help protect asphalt, and sealing concrete every few years is a good preventive measure. Shoveling before salting and using calcium chloride instead of rock salt reduces long-term damage.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania—and particularly for Dormont—start with the Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For any driveway job over $500, this is required by law and lets you check complaint history. After that, verify workers’ compensation coverage at WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov); all legitimate contractors must have it. Ask about liability insurance, and get a certificate in your name. Good contractors will also have a fixed business address and references at least three years old. Don’t overlook local knowledge: if they can’t name their asphalt plant or material supplier, find someone who can. For overlays or full replacements ($1,450–$8,800), it’s worth doing all these checks.
How much does driveway paving cost in Dormont? For a typical Dormont home, expect asphalt driveway replacement with a new base to run $3,400–$8,800, with resurfacing an existing good base at $1,450–$4,400. Concrete costs more, usually $4,900–$11,500, but can last twice as long. Prices depend on square footage, base condition, and site grading. Maintenance like crack filling or sealcoating is less expensive per visit but adds up over years. Always compare quotes on base prep, not just the top layer. Dormont’s climate and subgrade demand careful, documented site work for any job to last.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? Depressions—“birdbaths”—in Dormont driveways almost always mean movement in the subgrade, usually due to water trapped in clay or shale underneath. Over time, repeated freeze-thaw cycles swell and settle these soft spots, especially where drainage is poor. If water can’t drain away, it seeps below and shifts the base, leading to dips and eventually cracks or potholes. Proper repair involves excavating the failed section, improving drainage, rebuilding the base in compacted lifts, and then patching with new asphalt. In Dormont, this type of repair typically costs $3,400–$5,400. Regular crack sealing and ensuring positive slope away from flat areas can help prevent new ones from forming.
