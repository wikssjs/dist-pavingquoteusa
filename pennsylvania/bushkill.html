With local aggregate supply right in the county, anyone dodging that question is likely just a broker passing your job to the lowest bidder.

Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

The part of your Bushkill driveway that matters most isn’t visible when the paver leaves—it’s underneath. Every driveway is a three-part system: the subgrade (native soil), a compacted aggregate base, and the pavement on top (asphalt or concrete). Each layer must be built properly to transfer vehicle loads down to the ground. In our region’s Lehigh Valley limestone karst soils, poor drainage and freeze-thaw cycles combine with a deep frost depth (36 inches) to create frost heave: water trapped in the subgrade expands as it freezes, pushing your driveway up and breaking it from below.

Rushed or cheap jobs often skip proper excavation, lay thin or poorly compacted aggregate, or — worst of all — pave over a failed or soft base. No one can see it once the hot top goes down, but three years of Pennsylvania winters will reveal every shortcut: alligator cracking, sunken spots, pooling water, and edge failures. This is how two bids that look nearly identical, or a “deal” for a few thousand less, can cost you double when you’re repaving after five years instead of 25.

For Bushkill, you want in writing:

  • Excavation down to solid subgrade — removing all soft spots
  • At least 6 inches of clean ASTM #2A modified aggregate, compacted in 2-inch lifts with a vibratory roller
  • Proof that each lift was compacted before adding more
  • Aggregate from a Bethlehem or Easton-area supply, not trucked in days ahead and sitting loose
  • Drainage features—edge grading, swales—so water never sits atop or soaks below the base

A reputable contract should specify: aggregate type and thickness, compaction schedule, and how drainage is handled. “Proper base” means nothing unless numbers are attached to it. Don’t let any contractor work off a handshake or “the usual.”

Permits and approvals for paving in Bushkill

In Bushkill and most Northampton County townships, replacing or resurfacing a driveway within the same footprint rarely requires a building permit. This saves hassle for most homeowners, so repaving doesn’t typically mean a visit to the township office. However, if you plan to expand, relocate, or add a new driveway, you may trigger zoning or permitting review—Bethlehem, for instance, usually requires it for any expansion or new installation.

What often trips up homeowners is the driveway approach: the portion between your property and the public road. If your property faces a state-maintained road, you need a driveway approach permit from PennDOT District 5-0, not just your local township. For local residential streets, your own township handles curb cut approvals.

Stormwater is the other major consideration. Expanding the impervious surface—widening your driveway or paving a previously gravel area—can trigger drainage requirements, and you are always liable if runoff affects a neighbor’s property, regardless of permits.

Always verify your contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration via hic.attorneygeneral.gov and check that they carry active workers’ compensation insurance using the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Pennsylvania law requires a written contract for jobs over $500, which provides your legal protections under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, including a three-business-day right to cancel.

What to put in a Bushkill paving contract

If every Bushkill paving contract specified the right numbers, we’d see half as many collapsed driveways and base failures. In this region, with deep frost and limestone karst soils that can settle unpredictably, it’s not enough to accept a contractor’s “we’ll put down three inches” claim. Three inches as laid can compact to two or less, and the difference isn’t visible until the surface cracks in the winter freeze/thaw cycle.

Insist every bid specify:

  • How deep the existing driveway will be excavated (inches below final grade)
  • How thick the new aggregate base will be (measured after compaction—minimum 6 inches here)
  • Aggregate type (e.g., ASTM #2A modified, locally sourced)
  • That the base will be laid and compacted in lifts (not all at once)
  • Compaction method (heavy vibratory roller—not just a plate tamper)
  • Asphalt binder and surface course depths listed as compacted, not as spread (e.g., 2-inch binder, 1.5-inch surface, compacted)
  • Asphalt mix designation (from which local plant)
  • Edge treatment—sawcut or hand-tamped, not just thrown over the grass
  • How slopes and drainage will be addressed
  • How disturbed lawn/landscaping will be restored

A phrase like “three inches of blacktop” with no qualifiers is ambiguous. Demand “three inches compacted thickness”—because in this climate, compaction and correct depth are what prevent a short-lived driveway.

Why driveways behave differently in Bushkill

In Bushkill and greater Northampton County, the subgrade is predominantly Lehigh Valley limestone karst, which differs greatly in behavior from soils a county or two away. Limestone karst can create voids that lead to settlement, so a heavy, well-compacted base is not just recommended—it’s critical. Unlike clay that holds water—leading to frost heave and upheaval, or glacial till that drains quickly but can shift—a limestone base naturally encourages water migration and can create sudden dips where voids open beneath the aggregate.

Combine that with a 36-inch frost depth and relentless freeze-thaw cycles, and it’s no surprise older driveways, especially those laid before modern standards, frequently develop sunken patches or long cracks after a decade. That’s made worse in Easton and Bethlehem’s 1900–1940 housing stock, built before base requirements were standardized. Many driveways still rest on little more than packed dirt. When you see driveway after driveway replaced across these neighborhoods, it’s not by chance—it’s the ground conditions next door, not just the winter weather.

So if you want a driveway to last in Bushkill, you plan for what’s under it and make sure the base prep and drainage spec matches our unique local ground, not just a generic “Northeast” standard.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Bushkill

What does an asphalt overlay cost in Bushkill? In Bushkill, an asphalt overlay—also called resurfacing—typically runs $1,650–$5,000 for an average residential driveway, depending on square footage and site conditions. This pricing assumes your existing base is solid, with only surface cracks or slight wear, and that no major excavation is required. One reason overlays are popular locally is because most driveways already have the right width, but older pavements (especially in neighborhoods built before 1980) may need their base rebuilt rather than just covered. Don’t let anyone talk you into an overlay if you have deep cracks or potholes—those problems nearly always come back through.
Do I need a permit to repave a driveway in Bushkill? Most Bushkill homeowners do not need a building permit to repave a driveway in the existing location and footprint, based on typical Northampton County township requirements. This means you can usually proceed with resurfacing or replacing asphalt at your current width without a trip to the municipal office. However, if you’re looking to widen, relocate, or install a new driveway, a zoning or building permit will almost always be required. For the connection to a public or state-maintained road, a separate approach permit from PennDOT District 5-0 or your local township may also be necessary. It’s safer to check before starting, as fines and rework can get expensive.
What does sealcoating cost in Bushkill? For a single application of sealcoating on a typical driveway in Bushkill, expect to pay $225–$800. Most homeowners fall near the middle of that range for standard single-family driveways, which matches the makeup of local properties (almost all detached homes). The low end covers smaller or DIY-ready surfaces, while the high end is for large driveways, more prep, or obstacles to hand-brush around. Remember, sealcoating only protects decent pavement—it won’t fix divots, cracks, or structural issues already present.
Does Bushkill have local asphalt plants? Yes, Bushkill and nearby areas of Northampton County benefit from multiple local asphalt plants and quarries clustered around Bethlehem and Easton. This short haul distance means material supply is seldom a problem, even during peak paving season. Expect most reputable contractors to name a specific plant they use—if someone can’t or won’t, that’s a red flag. Local supply also tends to hold down trucking and material costs, which is reflected in Bushkill’s competitive pricing bands.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? In Bushkill, every driveway should be built with a minimum slope of 2%—about a quarter-inch per foot—to direct water off the pavement, according to best practices for regions with freeze-thaw cycles and limestone subgrade. Bushkill’s frequent snow, rain, and deep frost make it especially critical: without enough slope, water stands, seeps into cracks, and freezes, damaging the surface and the base. For new construction, demand this in writing in your paving contract. If an existing driveway can’t be regraded, plan on extra crack filling or corrections.
How far ahead should I book a paver in Bushkill? For the best local contractors in Bushkill, booking two to three months ahead is typical—sometimes longer in peak season (late spring to early fall). Even with short haul times from the Bethlehem and Easton plants, demand in the Lehigh Valley metro is high, and you’ll have more options if you schedule early. If your job is under $5,000, you might get worked in sooner, but don’t count on fast turnaround in May through September. Avoid anyone pressuring you for “today only” specials; the good crews are busy for a reason.
Is an overlay worth it or should I start over? In Bushkill, an asphalt overlay is a reasonable investment only if your existing driveway has a solid, intact base and any cracking is limited to the top layer. Overlays cost $1,650–$5,000 and add 8–15 years’ life. But if you have heaving, deep cracks, soft spots, or repeated sinking (not unusual here due to limestone karst and freeze-thaw damage), overlays are usually a waste; underlying problems will reflect through quickly. In those cases, full removal and base rebuilding at $3,900–$10,000 is the only real fix. Sometimes “doing it cheap” twice costs more than doing it right once.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? In Bushkill, driveways often develop “birdbath” depressions or settled sections due to improper base prep—common in neighborhoods with older driveways built on limestone karst. Over time, the ground can settle unevenly, or water trapped beneath the base can freeze and heave, then settle out, leaving low spots. These areas collect water, which speeds up asphalt breakdown. Fixing these usually requires removing and rebuilding the affected base and surface, not just patching. Expect repairs to cost anywhere from $200–$800 for small areas up to overlay or full rebuild pricing if large sections are affected.
Does sealcoating fix cracks? Sealcoating is a preventive maintenance measure, not a repair for cracks. In Bushkill’s climate, where freeze-thaw cycling is severe, sealcoating covers only hairline surface flaws and restores color, but will not fill or repair any cracks of size. For functional repairs, cracks must be cleaned and filled before sealcoating, especially to keep water from entering and worsening heaving over winter. Crack filling and patching typically costs $200–$800 depending on quantity and severity. Applying sealcoat over open cracks is just cosmetic—and the cracks will come right through again after weather cycles.
