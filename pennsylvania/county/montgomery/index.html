Driveway paving costs in Montgomery County — 2026

Montgomery County, Pennsylvania is home to 222,149 owner-occupied households, and about 54.0% of those are single-family homes (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). For most, a driveway is their largest paving job — and with that comes cost and complexity that shouldn’t be overlooked. Local climate adds another factor: southeastern Pennsylvania has moderate freeze-thaw cycles, so driveway failures here reflect subgrade, base, and drainage just as much as the wear on the surface. In neighborhoods like Cheltenham and Abington, where underlying soils are mostly glacial till and silty clay, both quality and depth of the stone base make or break the project.

Most of the price difference between two paving quotes isn’t in the visible work. It’s in what’s under the asphalt or concrete: excavation, the base stone, and whether proper compaction was done. Asphalt or concrete for a Montgomery County driveway?

Montgomery County’s climate — humid summers, moderate freeze-thaw cycles, and about 30 inches frost depth — shapes which driveways last. Asphalt is designed to flex with ground movement. In our winters, frost heave can move the subgrade by inches, but asphalt recovers by deforming, not shattering. Concrete, by contrast, is rigid. It can last twice as long if installed perfectly and left untreated by de-icing salt, but salt is unavoidable on driveways in southeastern Pennsylvania. When salt penetrates, it breaks down the surface paste and causes scaling or spalling — far more common here than cracking from loads. No sealant will fix this once it starts.

The up-front cost of concrete — $5,500–$13,500 — runs higher than asphalt, but no driveway outlasts a well-poured, well-cured slab kept dry and salt-free. The catch is behavioral: most owners do use salt, and most concrete spoils from it decades before the material itself would fail. Use concrete if you never want to seal or resurface — and can avoid salt. Asphalt is repairable, lower cost, and better suited to our region’s ground movement. Pavers offer a premium look and can be re-set if disturbed, but matching materials for repairs stands out over time. Gravel is a real option for rural homes and cuts cost — but requires grading and topping up every few years.

The driveway scams that work in Montgomery County

Montgomery County is in a dense, highly competitive paving market, especially along the US 202 and PA 309 corridors. But with the steady flow of visible driveways and large owner-occupied base, scams remain common. Paving draws predatory operators who move through suburban neighborhoods, sell quick jobs, then vanish before the problems show up. The classic scams work because poor installation usually doesn’t fail until a few freeze-thaw cycles have passed — and by that point, the crew is long gone.

Watch for these: the “leftover asphalt” pitch from a vague neighboring job, “today only” discounts, crews in unmarked pickups with out-of-area plates, quotes delivered verbal-only or written on a scrap, and insistence on cash before any work starts. Another common Montgomery County scam is oil-based spray applied thinly and sold as a proper sealcoat — it looks jet black for a few months, but offers zero real protection.

State law protects you: for any job over $500, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract and gives you three business days to cancel, for any reason. Never be rushed. Anyone offering to waive your insurance deductible is breaking Pennsylvania law.

Five minutes beats most scams: verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, check workers’ comp at wcais.pa.gov, ask for supplier receipts, insist on a real business address, and never pay in full until satisfied with the job.

How to vet a paving contractor in Montgomery County

Choosing a contractor for driveway work in Montgomery County means focusing on proof, not smooth talk. Start by verifying Home Improvement Contractor registration for any job over $500 on hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Skip anyone who can’t provide a real local office address — not just a cell number. Locals own their own paving equipment and can answer simple questions: which asphalt or aggregate plant do they buy from? (In Montgomery County, nearly all legit contractors use suppliers along the I-276 or US 202 corridors.) If the answer is vague, you’re likely dealing with a broker, not a builder.

References matter — but you want addresses of jobs done at least three years ago, not from last month. A fresh-looking driveway tells you nothing. No contractor should hesitate to provide insurance, supplier invoices, or explain their process from excavation to compaction.

How to tell if the base is gone

For any Montgomery County homeowner, the most important (and expensive) part of a driveway is the base — not the blacktop. Here’s what you can check yourself before you call someone in. Wide “alligator” cracking means the supporting base has failed: if you lay new asphalt on top, it will split again within a year or two. If you see isolated, straight cracks but the rest seems solid, that’s sun and water aging the surface, not a failed structure. Depressions or puddle “birdbaths” mean the subgrade below has settled — these spots need full-depth patch, not a quick overlay. Crumbling along edges is common where the driveway meets lawn — and signals little or no stone base at the perimeter. Potholes mean water is already breaking up the support below, and patching is just a short-term fix.

If a contractor quotes you an overlay without checking the base with a probe or sawcut, that’s not a serious assessment. Money spent on a fresh overlay over a bad base is lost — it will crack and fail again, often in the same pattern.

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is recommended by many in the trade, but what it does and doesn’t do is often misrepresented. In Montgomery County’s climate, a proper sealcoat protects asphalt from sunlight (UV oxidation), fuel, oil drips, and gives the surface a fresh appearance. That’s reason enough to do it every few years, if the underlying asphalt is sound and the base is not failing. What sealcoating does not do: add any real structural strength, fix significant cracks, repair birdbaths, or extend the life of an asphalt drive with poor drainage or a broken base. It’s as much about appearance as preservation.

The downsides: sealing too often or too thick builds up layers that trap moisture, flake, and can actually make future repairs more difficult. If you seal a new surface before it finishes curing (usually six months to a year), you risk locking in solvents and softening the surface. A realistic interval in Montgomery County is every three to four years — not every season. Crack filling is usually more urgent than sealing: open cracks let water destroy the base, while a perfect surface with open seams fails from below. A proper sealcoat is a squeegee or brush-applied coat about 1/16 inch thick, not a watery black spray that disappears after the first winter.

The current band for sealcoating a typical drive is $225–$750. If you’re quoted far below that, expect a quick spray that won’t last through spring.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In Montgomery County, most townships do not require a building permit simply to repave an existing driveway in its current footprint. For example, Lower Merion Township does not require a permit for routine sealcoating of a residential asphalt driveway. However, expanding, widening, or replacing a driveway where you alter the curb, sidewalk, or street apron — especially in the Municipality of Norristown — typically triggers the need for either a building or street opening permit.

The spot often overlooked is the approach where your driveway meets a public road. In Montgomery Township, for example, you need a road occupancy permit from the Public Works Department to touch that portion — not from the normal building code office. Expanding the impervious surface (making your driveway bigger or wider) also brings potential stormwater concerns. Many Montgomery County townships have runoff rules, and you cannot legally direct driveway water onto a neighbor’s property, no matter what permit you receive—it’s a civil liability.

Every contractor should be verified for HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov and for active workers’ compensation at wcais.pa.gov. Those are quick online checks, and you should never skip them in this county.

Why Montgomery County driveways fail from the bottom up

The single most important thing in a Montgomery County driveway is what’s under the surface. Every pavement is a sandwich: first, the subgrade (native soil, often glacial till or silty clay in eastern Montgomery County), then compacted crushed stone, then the asphalt or concrete slab. Freeze-thaw cycles force water in these soils to expand as it freezes, which lifts up sections of the driveway unevenly — that’s frost heave. If the aggregate base is too thin or not compacted enough, the load falls right back on the soil, which ruts, shifts, and lets water through. Surface repairs won’t last more than a couple years if the real problem is down below.

Most Montgomery County paving failures and callbacks happen because of shortcuts in base prep. Skipping full-depth excavation, laying stone too thin (sometimes as little as two inches, when local soils often need four to six), or using the wrong type of aggregate. Proper compaction after each lift (two to three-inch layers) is what prevents base settlement over time. If you’re comparing bids, make sure yours commits in writing to:

  • Minimum 4–6 inches of compacted crushed stone base (more for eastern soils)
  • Aggregate type: clean 2A modified or similar, not bank run fill
  • Compaction in lifts, verified by plate compactor or roller
  • Drainage grading so water sheds off, not toward structures

The catch: you won’t know if these steps were skipped until the third year, when cracks, depressions, or edge failures show up. On any driveway bid in Montgomery County, base prep is where a cheap quote gets cheap — and where lasting value is built or lost.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Montgomery County

How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Montgomery County?

For most driveways in Montgomery County, a sealcoat every three or four years is both sufficient and safe. Our region’s winters and humid summers mean you want protection from sun and moisture, but sealing too often can cause the coating to crack or peel, especially on older driveways where the last layer hasn’t worn away. Always wait at least six months, preferably a year, before sealing fresh asphalt to let it cure fully. The current cost for a single application is about $225–$750 depending on size and access, but a suspiciously low price often signals a quick spray that won’t last. Remember: sealing won’t fix cracks or structural issues—deal with those first, or you’re only trapping water inside.

Do I need a permit to repave a driveway in Montgomery County?

For most municipalities in Montgomery County, repaving your driveway in its existing size and layout does not require a permit. For example, Lower Merion Township does not need you to pull a permit to seal or resurface a typical asphalt drive. If you’re changing the width, adding a new curb cut, replacing a sidewalk crossing, or touching the apron at the street, then a permit is commonly necessary—especially in the Municipality of Norristown. Always check your specific township, as each one enforces separately. For driveway approaches on township roads, you’ll deal with agencies like the Montgomery Township Public Works Department directly. The bottom line: simple resurfacing, you’re usually safe; anything that changes layout or impacts public right-of-way, expect to get a permit.

What does compaction in lifts mean?

Compaction in lifts is a foundational step on every durable Montgomery County driveway. It means the contractor spreads the stone base in thin layers — typically two to three inches thick — and uses a vibrating roller or plate compactor to compact each layer before adding the next. This prevents the base from settling or rutting after paving, especially important with our freeze-thaw cycles and local silt- and clay-heavy soils. If you dump all the stone at once and compact only the top, loose fill remains down below, which leads to dips, cracks, or edge failures in year two or three. Explicitly ask your contractor to spell out lift thickness and compaction for your bid. Your investment—at $3,900–$10,000 for a new asphalt driveway—relies on this step being done right.

Is gravel a cheaper option in Montgomery County?

Yes, gravel is always the lower up-front cost for driveways in Montgomery County—usually in the range of $900–$3,500 for a typical home. It’s a valid option for rural properties, long driveways, or homeowners prioritizing budget over finished look. Maintenance is ongoing: expect to regrade and top up every couple of years, especially after winter heaving or washouts from storms. In some cases, townships have restrictions on gravel surfaces near the road or sidewalks, so check local regulations. Gravel’s main advantages are price and drainage — it’s far less likely to heave or crack from our region’s freeze-thaw cycles, but it’s not suitable everywhere in the county if dust or periodic maintenance is a concern.

How far ahead should I book a paver in Montgomery County?

In Montgomery County, peak paving season is April through October, and schedules for reputable contractors fill fast — sometimes two to three months ahead, especially after a wet spring or heavy winter. The busiest periods see backlogs of 4–8 weeks, with full replacement jobs like asphalt or concrete ($3,900–$13,500 range) prioritized over minor repairs or sealcoating. Off-season or shoulder months may offer more flexibility, but weather delays are common. If you need work done by a certain date (like for a home sale or before winter), schedule early and don’t be surprised if the most in-demand contractors can’t fit you in for several weeks. Reliable operators never rush or cut corners just to squeeze you in.

Does Montgomery County have local asphalt plants?

Yes, Montgomery County sits right in the middle of an established asphalt and aggregate supply corridor. Nearly all reputable paving contractors in the county buy from plants along I-276 and US 202, which keeps both haul times and costs down for local paving jobs. If a contractor can’t name their regular supplier, treat that as a red flag — “plant tickets” showing your asphalt’s source are standard proof. This access helps keep typical new asphalt driveways in the $3,900–$10,000 range, and means emergency repairs and patching can usually be scheduled without waiting for distant deliveries.

Are pavers worth the cost in southeastern Pennsylvania?

Pavers are at the high end of the cost spectrum in Montgomery County: generally $8,900–$13,500 for a typical single-family driveway. The value is partly appearance (color and pattern options), but also durability. Paver driveways don’t crack in the same way as solid asphalt or concrete; instead, they settle and can be reset. In our freeze-thaw climate, this is no small advantage. The tradeoff: repairs and releveling are labor-intensive, and patching often stands out if the original pavers are no longer produced. Pavers are best for homeowners wanting looks and willing to pay for the flexibility and higher material/labor costs. For most, asphalt delivers better cost/longevity, but a quality paver job can match or exceed well-installed concrete for long-term performance if maintained.

What does sealcoating cost in Montgomery County?

Sealcoating for a standard single-family driveway in Montgomery County runs $225–$750, with size and prep needed (cleaning, minor crack filling) as the main variables. Price at the lower end is for small, straightforward jobs; larger or more complex layouts trend higher. If you’re quoted well below this range, expect a thin, quick spray — not a brush or squeegee-applied product that actually protects the asphalt. Remember, sealcoating is mostly for appearance and UV protection, not a fix for deteriorated surfaces. Every few years is sufficient; don’t let anyone sell you on annual applications, as they can build up and flake off, especially after a harsh southeastern Pennsylvania winter.

What causes concrete to spall or flake in Montgomery County?

The most common cause of concrete spalling or flaking in Montgomery County is the use of salt-based de-icers—almost unavoidable here given our winter freeze-thaw patterns. Salt penetrates the concrete’s surface, holds moisture, and with repeated cycles, causes the top layer to peel off in thin flakes or chips. The region’s 30-inch frost depth amplifies movement and breakup near the surface. Poor curing, excessive water in the mix, or finishing in cold weather can make it worse, but even a perfect pour will eventually succumb if regularly salted. Resurfacing or patching is possible, but prevention—minimizing salt and sealing every couple of years—prolongs life. When weighing a new install at $5,500–$13,500, know that salt management is as important as the initial pour quality.

How deep should the aggregate base be here?

For most driveways in Montgomery County, a compacted aggregate base of 4–6 inches is minimum. In areas like Cheltenham and Abington, where the subgrade is silty clay or glacial till, you may want even deeper — up to 8 inches for heavy vehicles or poor drainage spots. This base serves as the cushion and drainage layer beneath your $3,900–$10,000 asphalt or $5,500–$13,500 concrete drive. Anything thinner risks settlement, rutting, and early cracking, especially with our winter-spring thaw cycles. Always specify base depth in the contract, and insist on compaction in two- to three-inch “lifts”—this is where the difference between a 5-year and a 25-year surface is made.

Are there stormwater rules on paving in Montgomery County?

Montgomery County municipalities take stormwater seriously. Expanding or widening your driveway almost always means increasing impervious surface, which channels more runoff to streets, neighbors, or storm sewers. Many townships (and Norristown itself) impose requirements about where that water goes — in some places you’ll need to manage or retain it on your own property, or prove the work doesn’t increase runoff. Even beyond permit requirements, Pennsylvania law holds you liable if your driveway regrades and pushes water onto a neighbor’s land. For a standard resurface in the $1,650–$5,000 range, minor grading is usually included, but larger changes may trigger inspection or engineered plans. Contractors in our area should be ready to explain both permit and stormwater checks in your township before digging in.
