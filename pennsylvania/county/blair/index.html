What a new driveway costs in Blair County

Blair County, with its 68.9% single-family housing share and over 27,260 owner-occupied households (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), sees steady demand for driveway paving and repair. Typical jobs range from new installations on steep hillside lots around Altoona and Hollidaysburg to patching and resurfacing older driveways built when most homes were constructed before 1980. In this region, costs are shaped not only by surface material but by what lies underneath—freeze-thaw cycles are harsh, and the local limestone and shale subgrade frequently require thicker aggregate bases and sometimes underdrains to prevent long-term settlement and cracking.

The most important aspect to understand: most of the price gap between quotes is buried. You can't see compaction, base thickness, or whether the right materials are used. Two driveways may be paved with the same asphalt, but if one skimps on excavation or includes too little aggregate, it will fail early and cost more in the long run. Good prep is costly, but cutting corners is costlier still.

What you need before paving in Blair County

In most Blair County municipalities, you won’t need a building permit just to repave an existing driveway if you don’t alter its footprint. For example, standard repairs or overlays in Logan Township usually move ahead without separate driveway permits. However, if your project involves widening, changing the layout, or modifying where the driveway meets a public street—especially in Altoona—you’ll likely need a local paving or occupancy permit. For driveways connecting to U.S. 220 near Hollidaysburg, Highway Occupancy Permits come from PennDOT District 9, while municipal offices handle local street approaches. Check with your borough or township before any change to access or driveway size; enforcement details vary municipality by municipality.

Increasing the size of a driveway brings other considerations: adding impervious surface can trigger local stormwater requirements because more runoff can overload old drainage systems or cause problems for neighbors. Directing water onto adjacent properties creates liability, regardless of what the permit says. Always plan water runoff as part of any paving or expansion job.

For contractor verification, Pennsylvania requires all contractors taking jobs over $500 to have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, issued by the Office of Attorney General. You can verify a paving contractor at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Every employer must also carry workers’ compensation insurance; you can check this via the state’s WCAIS system at wcais.pa.gov.

The specifications to demand in writing

If you want a driveway to last through Blair County’s freeze-thaw cycles and shale-limestone soils, specs matter more than price. Any bid should tell you, in numbers, exactly what will be done below grade and above. Quotes that just say, “three inches of blacktop,” are meaningless unless they specify whether it’s total thickness, what kind of aggregate is below, whether compaction was done in lifts, and if the measurement is before or after rolling. Asphalt and base compact substantially—if you pay for three inches loose and only get two finished, you’ve lost a third of your money up front.

Our local ground freezes down to 36 inches and heaves if water is trapped, so base depths here must be deeper than what you’d see in milder states. The wrong depth or missing underdrain, especially on hilly lots around Altoona and Hollidaysburg, guarantees early cracks and potholes. Demand these details in the contract, not just verbal agreements.

  • Excavation depth (minimum 8–12 inches below finished grade for new installs)
  • Type and thickness of aggregate base (6–8 inches compacted 2A or AASHTO #57 stone)
  • Compaction details (equipment type, number of passes, done in lifts)
  • Asphalt binder course thickness (2–2.5 inches compacted)
  • Surface course thickness (1–1.5 inches compacted)
  • Mix designation (PennDOT ID-2 or appropriate municipal mix)
  • Aggregate source (plant location—near Altoona or Duncansville helps with quality and timing)
  • Edge treatment (taper, supported, or with retaining as required for transitions or slopes)
  • Drainage correction details (regraded for fall, underdrain if required by clay or shale soils)
  • Written warranty period and exclusions

The paving season in north-central Pennsylvania

Paving in Blair County only happens when the climate allows. Our winter design temperature hits 2 °F, and the paving season is shut down by both the weather and local asphalt plant closures—usually from late November through March. Hot-mix asphalt mixes from Altoona and Duncansville plants can’t be laid below around 45 °F air and surface temperature, because they cool too fast to properly compact. If someone offers to pave in December here, they either don’t know what they’re doing or don’t care about the outcome. Concrete faces its own constraints: even if bagged mixes work year-round, a real concrete driveway needs above-freezing temperatures for the first few days to cure properly and reliably.

What does this mean practically? Prime paving season for asphalt runs from late April to October, though early spring and late fall can be workable if the crews watch weather and start early in the day. Summer is the busiest, with more demand and tighter booking. If you find a slot during shoulder season (April, May, late September), you may find more available contractors and better attention to detail—plants are open, crews are experienced, but it’s not the full rush.

Never accept an offer to pave when plants are closed or there’s a cold snap coming—porosity, improper compaction, or scaling can cut years off a driveway’s lifespan. Good contractors will tell you firmly when they can and can’t work, and in this climate you’ll pay more for rush jobs but get far better results by waiting.

Comparing materials in north-central Pennsylvania

Our freeze-thaw cycles are brutal—repeated freezing and thawing pushes up any weak base, cracks rigid materials, and chews through anything with drainage issues. Asphalt is the workhorse here because it flexes with the ground instead of cracking. Most Blair County driveways see twenty or more freeze-thaw cycles per year, so asphalt’s flexibility lets it survive with only minor maintenance even as the subgrade shifts. The up-front installed cost for a new asphalt driveway is $3,300–$8,600, delivering 20–30 years with proper base and maintenance.

Concrete costs more initially—$4,800–$11,500—and when done right, can last 30–50 years. But it’s susceptible to de-icing salt, which causes scaling and spalling in snow-belt regions like ours. Most concrete driveways here fail from salt damage, not cracking, and unless you strictly avoid chloride de-icers, you’ll see surface deterioration in as little as five years.

Pavers and gravel are both viable for specific cases. Pavers’ higher cost reflects flexible installation that allows manual replacement, making them good for short runs where aesthetics matter. Gravel is most common in rural Blair County; it drains well but needs regular grading and topping. If cost, maintenance, or access are tough, it’s still legitimate.

How often should a Blair County driveway be sealed?

Sealcoating in Blair County matters, but not as much as most driveways-in-a-day ads would have you believe. Properly applied, it forms a layer that protects asphalt from UV, weather, and chemicals. This slows aging, reduces raveling of stones, and keeps the surface looking clean and uniform. What it cannot do: sealcoating does not add structural strength, fix alligator cracks, or keep a sinking base from getting worse. If the base is shot, sealing is cosmetic.

Done too often, sealcoating actually becomes a problem—layers peel, water gets trapped and freezes, and the appearance suffers. Sealing a brand new driveway before the asphalt has fully cured (usually 90–180 days in Blair County’s climate) will lock in volatiles and weaken the binder. The right interval is every 2–4 years, judged by fading color and worn appearance, not the calendar. Price for a typical single application is $200–$650; cheaper offers commonly reflect thinned product or too-light coverage. On big driveways, anything dramatically below the low end usually means the crew is cutting material or coverage.

Crack filling is more critical than sealcoating—it keeps water out of the base, which does more for longevity in our freeze-thaw climate. A proper sealcoating job will block fuel and oil, but not solve drainage, cracks, or base failure. Demand two coats brushed on edges and hand-filled cracks, not just a quick spray. A good contractor won't tell you to seal every year and will hold off recommending it until you've gotten a few winters out of your new surface.

Door-knocking crews and leftover material

Paving is a prime target for transient and “gypsy” crews because money changes hands on the day, the work is visible but defects are invisible until months later, and enforcement can be spotty in Blair County's more rural townships—especially away from Altoona. These crews use the same approaches every year: claim they have “leftover asphalt from a job down the road,” offer you a one-day price that’s only good if you pay cash now, show up in unmarked trucks often with out-of-county plates, and give you a number for the work with no written contract. These are all red flags—especially if they propose a thin oil-based spray as “sealcoating” for $99 or $150.

Blair County’s moderate metro market sees more of these operators than you’d expect, particularly in lightly populated areas where neighbors are far apart and word spreads more slowly. Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract for any job over $500, and gives you a three business-day right to cancel, no matter what the crew claims. Never feel pressured to oblige a crew at your door—these laws exist for your protection, and a reputable operator will expect you to exercise them.

To stay safe, verify the contractor’s HIC registration on the state site, demand a written contract, check that they have current workers’ compensation via WCAIS, and never pay in full up front. Five minutes on your phone defeats nearly every scam in the trade.

Where the water goes

Water is what ruins most driveways in Blair County. If a job leaves you with ponding, allows runoff to sit at the base, or slopes toward your foundation, frost will take care of the rest in a hurry. The surface should slope at least 2% away from buildings—the equivalent of a quarter inch per foot—to keep runoff moving. For driveways down a hill, crowning the middle or a gentle cross-slope will send water off the edges. A driveway sloping toward your garage or house is a recipe for leaks and interior water damage; if it absolutely can’t be changed by regrading, a channel or trench drain is the only responsible fix.

The region’s limestone and shale soils often drain poorly, holding water by the base—the reason underdrain and thicker aggregate are sometimes essential around Hollidaysburg or other hillside lots. If your jobsite is all clay, expect longer drying times and a greater need for subdrain to prevent heave (ice lenses under the base lift the asphalt every winter). Get a plan for water in writing—grade, drains, and where the runoff will go. Remember, directing water onto a neighbor’s yard risks both damage and lawsuit, whether you have a permit or not. In our market, proper drainage isn’t an upgrade; it’s basic survival for any new or replacement driveway.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Blair County

How long does a driveway installation take? The duration for a driveway install in Blair County depends heavily on size, base prep, and weather. For an average single-family home, a full asphalt replacement typically takes 2–3 days: one day for excavation and base work, and another for paving and finishing. Concrete driveways demand more time—usually three or four days for forming, pouring, and initial set, plus several days' cure before you can park. Rain and strict paving season constraints (asphalt plants near Altoona and Duncansville close in winter) add unpredictability. Most local contractors plan projects back-to-back during the busy months, so be prepared for a short wait after you sign. The fastest jobs—sealcoating or patching—can often be completed in 1 day, with typical pricing for sealcoating around $200–$650.
How much does driveway paving cost in Blair County? A new asphalt driveway for a typical Blair County single-family home runs $3,300–$8,600, with the median being $5,200. Asphalt overlay, which is only an option when the base is sound, costs less: $1,450–$4,300. Concrete options generally start at $4,800 and can exceed $11,500 on complicated sites. The older, hillside terrain in Altoona and around Hollidaysburg adds cost when extra base or drainage is needed—often the hidden difference between two quotes. For homeowners in more remote rural parts of the county, modest increases in aggregate hauling can nudge up the price. Always request a written quote that breaks out base preparation, materials, and any drainage correction.
Are door-to-door pavers a problem in Blair County? Yes, the combination of visible driveways, a moderate metro market, and thinner population in townships like Antis and Allegheny means Blair County sees repeated waves of transient door-knocking paving crews. Their pitches—leftover asphalt, “today only” pricing, cash up front—rely on fast decisions and minimal scrutiny. Pennsylvania law requires a written contract for jobs over $500 and gives you three business days to cancel—even if the work was offered on the spot. If the contractor can’t show a valid Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, or their registration doesn’t match the business card or truck, walk away. Scams often involve overcharging for spray-on sealcoating (sometimes just $99 for worthless coverage), not the legitimate rates of $200–$650 you’ll find from real local firms.
Asphalt or concrete for a Blair County driveway? In Blair County, asphalt is the norm for good reason. Our climate’s deep freeze-thaw cycles and high snow load mean flexible asphalt tolerates more ground movement, while rigid concrete cracks or spalls under salt unless meticulously maintained. Asphalt costs less up front—typically $3,300–$8,600—and can be maintained by sealcoating and patching. Concrete lasts longer—$4,800–$11,500—but only if you avoid salt-based de-icers, which is tough in our snowy winters. If you want low initial cost and easy repairs, asphalt is the better bet; if you never use salt and want to minimize upkeep, premium concrete with drainage upgrades can work, but it’s usually not worth the premium for most local homeowners.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? A Blair County driveway should have a minimum 2% slope—about 1/4 inch per foot—moving water away from your house and toward the street or drain. This is especially important given our intense freeze-thaw cycles: water that pools and soaks in will freeze, heave the base, and break up even new pavements. On hillside lots around Hollidaysburg, cross-slope (crowned or edged grading) is common to shed water. Where the grade can’t be changed, a trench or channel drain may be needed. For new installs, correcting base and grading is part of what you pay for—one reason the real cost of asphalt replacement ranges from $3,300–$8,600 depending on the complexity and length.
When can I seal a brand new asphalt driveway? In Blair County’s climate, you should wait at least 90 days—and preferably until the next spring—before applying the first sealcoat to new asphalt. This gives the surface time to cure, letting excess oils dissipate through hot, dry weather. Sealing too early (sometimes pitched right after a new install) traps volatiles, softens the binder, and can lead to premature surface problems or peeling. The standard in this market is to wait until the color has faded to a medium gray and the surface loses its surface sheen. After that, seal every 2–4 years. The going price for a proper single sealcoat is $200–$650, depending on driveway size.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? For any Blair County job over $500, Pennsylvania law requires the contractor to have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration. You can check a license number or business name instantly at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Genuine contractors will provide the HIC number on paperwork. For workers’ compensation compliance, verify coverage on the state’s WCAIS system at wcais.pa.gov. Do not rely on flyers or verbal assurance alone—every reputable paving contractor will give this without hesitation. A valid registration is your proof of insurance and contract rights, which apply on jobs like asphalt installs (typically $3,300–$8,600) or overlays.
What are the signs of a failing driveway base? Blair County driveways commonly show base failure through deep cracks that run in a block or “alligator” pattern, broad dips or rutting where tires sit, new puddles that appear after rain, or a sudden worsening of potholes after a freeze-thaw cycle. Cracks under a quarter inch wide are easily filled, but widening cracks and heaved sections tell you the aggregate base has shifted or the subgrade’s settled—especially around limestone or shale soils. Resurfacing only helps if the base stays solid. When the base is compromised, full replacement (in the range of $3,300–$8,600 for asphalt) is usually needed; anything less is throwing money away on short-lived fixes.
Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door? Yes, Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act gives every Blair County homeowner a 3 business-day right to cancel any home improvement contract signed at your property or elsewhere (not at the contractor’s office). This covers jobs large and small—if the contract was for more than $500 and wasn’t initiated at the business, you are protected even if work is scheduled immediately. The contractor is required to provide written notice of this right. Make any cancellation in writing, keep a copy, and send it as soon as possible to avoid confusion. This law is designed specifically to safeguard against high-pressure door-to-door offers or “leftover asphalt” scams, which often target rural or suburban neighborhoods here.
Is gravel a cheaper option in Blair County? Gravel remains a practical and budget-friendly choice, especially for longer rural runs in Blair County's outer townships. For areas with infrequent plowing or as private laneways, it’s the most forgiving in our shale and limestone soils—because it drains well and is easy to top up each spring. Installed costs are typically $900–$3,500 depending on length and base prep. Regular grading and replenishing are needed, but you avoid most of the base heave problems of paved surfaces. In urbanized areas like Altoona, however, local codes and curb appeal usually push owners toward paved finishes, but for cost alone, gravel is a legitimate choice.
How deep should the aggregate base be here? In Blair County’s climate and soil conditions, a durable driveway needs at least 6–8 inches of compacted aggregate base beneath asphalt or concrete—more for heavily loaded aprons or hillside locations common around Altoona and Hollidaysburg. Our local subgrade of limestone and shale shifts with water, so a thicker base (sometimes up to 12 inches on soft or poorly draining sites) pays off quickly in fewer repairs and longer lifespan. Never accept a price-cutting proposal that skips excavation or uses less base; the biggest price difference between quotes (asphalt jobs here run $3,300–$8,600) is usually in what you can’t see. Always demand a written specification that states base depth after compaction, not just before.
