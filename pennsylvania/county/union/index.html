What a new driveway costs in Union County

In Union County, where 67.5% of homes are single-family and about 2 out of 3 houses are owner-occupied (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), the driveway isn’t just a detail—it’s one of your largest exterior investments. Costs here are shaped by our demanding freeze-thaw cycles and the silty, alluvial soils along the West Branch Susquehanna River. Long heating seasons and snow loads mean the ground shifts more each year than in much of Pennsylvania, so the “cheap” job up front often turns expensive down the line.

The visible surface—whether it’s asphalt or concrete—is only a fraction of what you’re paying for. Most of the price difference in Union County quotes comes from what’s under your driveway: base stone depth, quality, and whether proper compaction was actually done. Two jobs that “look” identical on day one can age very differently by year three. This is where you find out if that second-lowest bid saved you anything at all.

How paving fraud actually operates

Paving scams in Union County follow predictable moves, but the low population density and long distances between neighbors make this area easier for fly-by-night crews aiming to vanish before problems show up. If you hear about “leftover asphalt from a job down the road,” a “once-only price,” or see an unmarked truck with out-of-area plates, you’re looking at classic warning signs. The same goes for anyone pushing for cash up front, refusing to put details in writing, or quoting a suspiciously low price for what turns out to be a quick oil-based spray, not real sealcoating.

Because driveways are so visible, unlicensed crews target rural counties like this one, looking for easy sales with minimal oversight. Their work often looks fine to start, but if the base was never prepped or the asphalt was extra thin, your problems don’t show for years. The Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act takes aim exactly at this situation: any paving or sealing job over $500 must have a written contract, and you have three business days to cancel—no penalty. Never accept a verbal promise in place of real paperwork, and don’t let anyone rush you into a decision on “today only” terms.

If you suspect a scam: check the contractor’s PA HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, confirm workers’ comp at wcais.pa.gov, and ask for proof of insurance. Make sure the contract includes specific scope and pricing, and avoid anyone who pushes you away from taking a day to think it over. Five minutes spent on these checks is all it takes to avoid most driveway fraud.

The paving season in north-central Pennsylvania

In north-central Pennsylvania, the paving season is set by weather—hot-mix asphalt cannot be laid year-round in Union County. With a winter design temperature of 2 °F and over 6,300 heating degree days each year, there’s simply no way to get durable results after the first severe frost. Asphalt cools too quickly outside the right temperature window, and the local plants that supply mixes from Williamsport close for winter, so material is literally unavailable several months each year.

For new concrete, you face a similar cutoff: concrete needs above-freezing days (and preferably nights) to cure. The risk isn’t just a poor finish—improperly cured slabs can spall or scale, shortening their lifespan dramatically in our climate.

Paving crews here book up quickly once the weather breaks, and the summer peak (mid-June through August) is booked out first. The best scheduling—sometimes with better pricing—is during the shoulder months when crews run between jobs and have unexpected openings. If someone offers to pave in late November or December in Union County, it says more about their need for your deposit than about the actual suitability of the weather. Wait for the right season: you’ll get better results and leverage with local contractors.

Why Union County driveways fail from the bottom up

Most driveways in Union County do not fail at the surface—they fail from poor base prep buried below. The typical assembly has your subgrade (existing soil), a compacted aggregate base (varies in thickness), and asphalt or concrete on top. Each layer distributes the load of your vehicle to the next one down. Our local alluvial silty soils along the river behave differently than clay or sand: they hold more water, so a driveway over a shallow or poorly compacted base is at much higher risk for frost heave.

Here, water trapped under the driveway freezes and expands, lifting and cracking the slab from below. The 36-inch frost depth in our area is no joke – if a contractor skips excavation or lays down the bare minimum of stone just to get the job, their price will look great but the driveway will show depressions, surface cracking, and wet spots within a few winters. Gravel thickness is everything in Union County’s freeze-thaw cycles.

The main ways contractors cheap out are: skipping proper excavation, laying new over failed base, using thinner or dirty stone, and not compacting in lifts. None of this is visible when they leave. The trouble starts with your first spring thaw three years later—by then, your warranty has usually expired.

If you remember one thing, it’s this: demand the following, in writing:

  • Minimum 6–8 inch base stone (deeper in poor soils)
  • Type of aggregate (2A modified stone is typical locally)
  • Compaction in lifts — not all at once — and confirmation by plate tamper or roller

This is what separates a good driveway from a failed one, no matter how pretty the surface looks at first.

Permits and approvals for paving in Union County

For repaving your existing driveway in Union County, a full building permit is often not required—but always check because rules shift by township. In East Buffalo Township, any driveway work, even repairs, requires a permit, and other townships have their own enforcement. If you’re changing where your driveway meets the road, that’s a different permit entirely: any connection to a PA state road needs a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT Engineering District 3-0, not your township office.

If your project expands the paved area, you can trigger local runoff rules, especially if impervious surface increases. Regardless of paperwork, you’re still liable if your driveway drains water onto a neighbor’s property or the public right-of-way—it will cost you later even if you had a permit in hand.

Protect yourself: every contract over $500 must be with a PA-registered Home Improvement Contractor (verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For employed work crews, confirm workers’ compensation status at wcais.pa.gov. Taking five minutes to check both covers you from contractor non-compliance and insurance gaps before any ground is broken in Union County.

Reading a driveway proposal

Comparing driveway quotes is about details—not just the bottom line. Look for a proposal that lists measured square footage, excavation depth and disposal plan, aggregate type and thickness, compaction method (should be done in lifts), asphalt or concrete courses with their own compacted depths, edge and finish details, drainage slope, and a cleanup plan for the lawn edge. The payment schedule should require only a small deposit upfront, with balance on completion, and the warranty should spell out exactly what is covered and—more importantly—what isn’t. Most driveway warranties exclude cracking, which surprises a lot of people.

If a quote just throws out a dollar total and promises a “proper job,” you have no way to compare it to a real bid. This is the common route people overpay for underbuilt work—demand specifics in writing to ensure you’re buying more than surface appearance.

Comparing materials in north-central Pennsylvania

Choosing between asphalt and concrete for a Union County driveway is about more than appearance or initial price. With our region’s 40 psf snow loads, 36-inch frost depth, and frequent freeze-thaw cycles, flexibility is king—which gives asphalt a local advantage. Asphalt flexes and recovers from ground movement, resisting most cracks until the base itself fails. Concrete, while lasting decades if kept dry, is rigid and spells trouble once you introduce salt deicers for winter, as most households in our snowier zone eventually do. The result is surface spalling and scaling, not cracks, and this is the most common way a concrete drive fails here.

Traditional pavers handle ground movement well (since each stone can shift a little), but installation and maintenance are steeper. Gravel remains a real contender for long rural driveways where budget or access trumps everything else; nothing drains like stone, but ongoing upkeep is part of the deal. If you want a concrete drive to last in north-central Pennsylvania, skip salt deicers—even if it means waiting for sun or sand.

Caring for a new Union County driveway

Proper maintenance adds real years to a Union County driveway, no matter what it’s made of. Crack filling before water can reach the base is by far the cheapest and most effective step—once moisture gets down, freeze-thaw cycles and silty soils do their worst. Keep the edges supported; unsupported edges break down first from both traffic and heaving. Ensure water drains off rapidly—not pooling, not running along the joints, not across the drive. Regularly clean away leaves and soil at low spots, which trap moisture.

Don’t park dumpsters or jack stands right on the slab for long periods—point loads crush asphalt and leave permanent dents. For new asphalt, wait a minimum of 2–3 days in hot weather, or a full week in spring and fall, before parking, and avoid turning wheels while stationary to reduce scuffing.

If you use de-icers, know that salt attacks concrete directly—scaling and surface pitting are common by year five when it’s used. Asphalt doesn’t suffer chemically, but using salt means more water seeps in, melt-refreezes, and pushes the freeze-thaw effect farther. Alternatives for ice control are sand, abrasives, or calcium magnesium acetate (more expensive but gentler on pavement). In our snow-belt county, the honest answer is to shovel early and often, and use only what you need.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Union County

Is sealcoating a scam? Sealcoating itself is not a scam when done on a sound, crack-free asphalt driveway—the real product extends life by shielding from UV and water. But the scam comes from crews calling a thin oil-based spray “sealcoating” and charging legitimate prices, which in Union County run $200–$700 per application. If a price sounds too good to be true, or the crew offers just a quick hand-spray without cleaning or filling cracks, they’re likely selling you little more than used oil. Real sealcoating includes power cleaning, crack filling, and two full coats by squeegee or sprayer, with clear written details.
Why are paving quotes in Union County so different from each other? In Union County, most of the price gap between two driveway quotes hides below the surface—not only in material, but in stone base thickness, depth of excavation, and real compaction. A full asphalt replacement here ranges from $3,400–$8,800, and the thin rural contractor market means local travel surcharges and material haul distance from Williamsport play a part. One quote may propose a full dig-out and proper base, while another plans to pave over your old problems. Always demand the full written scope, including measurements and thickness of every layer, because “surface only” jobs don’t show issues until a few freeze-thaw seasons have passed.
How far ahead should I book a paver in Union County? Booking a reputable paving contractor in Union County should be done months out—ideally 2 to 4 months in advance. The rural nature of the county, combined with limited local crews and material being sourced from Williamsport, means slots fill quickly once spring arrives. The summer peak is booked solid first, especially in Lewisburg and Mifflinburg where older driveways are replaced every year. Waiting too long may force you to accept less reputable contractors or pay travel surcharges. Overlay work, which costs $1,450–$4,400, can sometimes be squeezed in, but full replacements require more planning.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? Every driveway in Union County should be built with a slope of at least 1 to 2%—that’s about a quarter to a half inch per foot of run—to drain water away from the house and avoid standing puddles. In our north-central PA climate, with heavy snowmelt and silty soils, anything flatter risks ponding, which speeds pothole formation. Proper grade is just as important as the right base and is specified in better proposals. On a standard 40-foot drive, this means a drop of 10–20 inches edge-to-street. Even overlays at $1,450–$4,400 must address slope, or you’ll see problems within a year.
Is an overlay worth it or should I start over? An asphalt overlay in Union County is only worth your money if the existing base is still sound—no soft spots, rutting, or deep alligator cracking. The overlay process typically costs $1,450–$4,400 and adds 8–15 years of life, but if the base is already failing, it just covers up the problem and makes fixing it later more expensive. For driveways with deep cracks, depressions, or a soft-feeling underlayer, full replacement with new base at $3,400–$8,800 is the only lasting answer. The decision boils down to what’s under the surface, not what you see on top.
Are door-to-door pavers a problem in Union County? They are—especially in rural counties like Union where neighborhoods are spread out and local verification is harder. These pavers rely on high-pressure tactics, saying they have “leftover material from another job.” They’ll usually ask for payment in cash up front and may lack state HIC registration, which is required for jobs over $500 in Pennsylvania. If approached, ask for written identification, proof of registration, and never sign or pay for anything at the door. A real contractor will give you the space and time to verify their credentials.
Is gravel a cheaper option in Union County? Absolutely—gravel remains the most economical surface for long or rural driveways here, typically costing $900–$3,500 installed. It handles drainage and the silty subgrade better than asphalt or concrete, and repairs are a matter of regrading and new stone, not patching or re-pouring. It does require periodic top-dressing and grading, about every 2–3 years, but if dust and occasional ruts are acceptable, it’s cost-effective for many Union County homes, especially in outlying areas where paved surfaces have less value.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? In Union County, depressions or “birdbaths” usually come from a poorly prepared or insufficient base, compounded by freeze-thaw cycling in local silty soils. Water soaks into the subgrade, freezes over winter, and shifts the drive unevenly. Eventually, the asphalt or concrete settles, leading to visible low spots. This is often the result of shortcuts during installation—especially on jobs priced at the low end, around $3,400 for asphalt. Regular crack sealing, immediate repair, and ensuring water drains fast from the driveway are critical to prevent these issues.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes—if your driveway connects to a Pennsylvania state route in Union County, you need a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT Engineering District 3-0. This applies whether your project is new installation or a significant change to your driveway’s entry point. These permits are processed separately from township or borough approvals, so don’t assume one covers the other. Approach work is about more than paperwork: the authorities want to be sure your driveway doesn’t dump water or gravel onto the public road. Costs will depend on project scope, but approach permits are often required even for overlays at $1,450–$4,400.
What is the difference between binder and surface course? An asphalt driveway built to last in Union County will have two layers: the binder course is installed as the lower, thicker layer for strength, while the surface (or wearing) course is the top, finer layer for smoothness and weather resistance. Skipping or thinning the binder course is one way a cheap quote gets cheaper—don’t let a proposal just say “3 inches total” without specifying how much is base and how much is finish. For a standard driveway, a proper breakdown will run you between $3,400–$8,800. Always require these details in the proposal.
Do I need a permit to repave a driveway in Union County? In most parts of Union County, simply resurfacing or repaving an existing driveway in the same footprint does not require a building permit, but exceptions exist. For example, East Buffalo Township requires permits for any driveway installation or repair. If you change your driveway’s size, location, or the way it connects to a public road, additional approvals—like a PennDOT Highway Occupancy Permit—may be needed. Even for straightforward overlays costing $1,450–$4,400, you should always call your township first to avoid issues. When in doubt, ask your contractor to verify permit responsibilities in writing.
