Allegheny County paving prices by scope — 2026

Allegheny County, with its 67.1% single-family homes and 316,359 owner-occupied households (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), sees high demand for residential driveway work—especially since most homes rest on hilly, clay- and shale-based land. In our region, freeze-thaw cycles are harsh and the base below your driveway matters more than any visible surface. The difference between a $3,500 and $9,000 quote for the “same” new asphalt driveway is what gets done where you cannot see: depth of base stone, how well it’s compacted, and proper drainage correction.

Paving decisions in Allegheny County are shaped by subgrade needs. Clay and shale require sturdier bases and careful work or you’ll suffer heaves, cracks, and settlement. If you’re comparing quotes, understand that a thin overlay on a tired driveway can look fresh for a year but fail quickly if the old problems below are ignored. Newer postwar subdivisions, common in Penn Hills and McCandless, often need a real structural overlay—not just a cosmetic sealcoat—due to aged, failing pavement bases. How often should a Allegheny County driveway be sealed?

Sealcoating in Allegheny County is neither snake oil nor a miracle solution. It does protect the asphalt binder from our tough freeze-thaw cycles; it blocks sunlight, slows oxidation, and helps resist surface damage from oil and gasoline. You’ll also get that rich black look, which makes even an old driveway look fresh for now. But what sealcoating does not do is fix the underlying cracks, reinforce a failing base, or give a new life to asphalt that’s already turned brittle from Pittsburgh’s weather swings.

Some contractors push you to seal annually, but that’s for their schedule—not your driveway’s health. Sealing too often, especially in Allegheny County’s climate and with our clay-heavy subgrade, causes build-up. Old layers crack and peel, and sealing new asphalt too soon only traps outgassing volatiles, hurting durability. Wait until new asphalt has cured; 6–12 months is responsible. After that, every two to three years is a balanced interval if you want preservation, not appearance alone.

More critical than sealcoating is crack filling. Water exploiting half-inch cracks is the beginning of heaved, broken pavement when it freezes—far worse than UV wear. Ask what material your contractor will use: a professional-grade, hot-applied crack filler is far better than a cold, quick patch that won’t last through winter. The cheapest sealcoat jobs, especially quoted at the low end of $200–$700, are often thin spray-ons with little prep. These wear off unevenly in a season and can make future maintenance harder. Scheduled, quality sealcoating helps, but don’t let anyone sell you service the base doesn’t need. Crack filling and honest timing matter more for Allegheny County driveways.

What to put in a Allegheny County paving contract

If you’re paving in Allegheny County, the best defense against wasted money is simple: demand clear specifications in your contract. Most contractors here work honestly, but with our region’s tough freeze-thaw cycles and problem clay and shale soils, you cannot afford to let a sales pitch or a single line quote dictate your outcome. The numbers on your quote are what you’re actually paying for, and in this trade, the differences that matter most are invisible once the last roller passes.

Every serious bid should list actual excavation depth, the type and thickness of base material (measured after compaction, in inches), the method of compaction (and whether it’s done in layers, or “lifts”), and the depth and mix type for both binder and surface asphalt as compacted—not as laid, since an unrolled mat looks much thicker than it will be by the time that roller leaves. Ask for details: “three inches” without a qualifier can mean almost anything, especially in a county where below-grade frost depth reaches 36 inches and home driveways see winter like few places south of Interstate 80. Below are the specification items you should require in writing:

  • Excavation depth and area measured in square feet
  • Aggregate base thickness and type (e.g., 2B or 2A limestone)
  • Base compaction method and confirmation done in lifts
  • Asphalt binder course thickness (compacted)
  • Asphalt surface course thickness (compacted)
  • Asphalt mix design (type, e.g., ID-2 or ID-3)
  • Edge treatment—tapered, angled, or backed by added base
  • Drainage and slope toward road (at least 2%)
  • Cleanup and restoration of disturbed lawn or landscaping
  • Warranty terms—what is (and isn’t) covered, in writing

Comparing paving estimates in Allegheny County

Anyone can hand you a single number, but you can’t compare that to real work—especially in Allegheny County, where the hill, the clay subgrade, and the winter make short work of underbuilt driveways. A usable estimate starts with measured square footage and details every step: excavation (with depth and disposal), kind and thickness of aggregate, how compaction is handled, the exact asphalt course breakdown with their compacted depths, edge treatments (which are critical for hilltop homes), slope for drainage, and how disturbed lawn or landscape will be left. Payment schedules here should never be full cash up front—a modest deposit, balance on completion is county standard. Finally, every warranty should be in writing. Don’t assume cracking, settlement, or drainage is covered: most are not.

The most common way Pittsburgh-area homeowners overpay is by accepting a vague, one-line quote. Not only can you not compare it, you also have almost no recourse if corners are cut beneath the surface. Here’s what you should see—plus the red flags you don’t want to:

Why you can't pave in February

Paving in February in Allegheny County simply isn’t possible—nor should it be. Hot-mix asphalt, which is the standard for all quality residential driveways, must be paved and compacted when both air temperature and ground temperature are well above freezing. Our winter design temp, set at 8 °F, means not only does the asphalt cool too fast to be worked, but you physically can’t get quality material: local asphalt plants in the Pittsburgh area close through the cold months. No contractor can get proper mix delivered, and anyone saying otherwise is not offering work worth having.

Even with concrete, you’ll run into the same problem. Concrete needs above-freezing conditions to cure well; pouring it onto frozen clay or shale risks permanent cracking and pop-outs when thaw returns. For these reasons, the paving season in southwestern Pennsylvania really ramps up in April, peaks in June through August, and tapers off by November—sometimes late October if cold comes early. Asphalt crews book solid at the summer peak, but shoulder season (April, May, September) are your windows for prompt service and fairer pricing. If anyone offers to pave your driveway in late November or after, in this climate, you have reason to question what corners they’re cutting. Reliable Allegheny County contractors do not lay real pavement in winter because the work simply won’t last.

Permits and approvals for paving in Allegheny County

For most homeowners in Allegheny County, repaving an existing residential driveway in its current footprint doesn’t usually require a building permit. For example, private driveway resurfacing in Ross Township is commonly handled without any separate right-of-way permit. However, if your project extends to the driveway “approach”—that is, where your driveway meets the public roadway—you’ll likely need a different kind of permit. For any approach connecting to a state road, you need a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 11-0. In Pittsburgh itself, altering curbs, sidewalks, or the apron in the right-of-way triggers a permit from the Department of Mobility and Infrastructure.

If your paving plan increases the amount of hard, impervious surface (by widening or adding parking pads), check for local stormwater runoff requirements. Even if a permit isn’t needed, draining water off your new driveway onto a neighbor’s lot is always a liability in Allegheny County and doesn’t need a code violation to make you responsible for damage.

Only hire a paving contractor who can show valid Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor registration, issued by the Attorney General’s office (search at hic.attorneygeneral.gov). All employers must carry workers’ compensation, which you can check in WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). These basics matter more than price alone in protecting your home and wallet.

Beyond replacement: layout changes

Driveway work in Allegheny County often goes beyond simple replacement—especially in older neighborhoods or postwar subdivisions that see more cars per household today than in 1950. Common layout projects are widening for a second vehicle, extending to a backyard garage, adding a turnaround or extra parking pad, and upgrading a gravel run to asphalt.

Once you change the footprint—anything beyond a surface resurface—you’re starting over with base prep on undisturbed clay and shale. That means a brand new base must be installed and compacted from scratch under every added section, or you risk new sections heaving and sinking. The seam between old and new will always be a potential weak spot; there’s no getting around that. Also, many municipalities in the county limit how close you can pave to your property line and how much total impervious area you may add, to control runoff into the hilly terrain and neighboring yards.

Permitting for these changes can vary, but driveway approaches (connections to public roads) and increased hard surface usually trigger review or approval—check with your local office and, if connecting to a state road, PennDOT District 11-0. Plan both for surface drainage and the paper trail before excavating anything new.

Drainage: the failure nobody plans for

Water is the main reason driveways in Allegheny County fail. Grading isn’t just one step in paving—done wrong, it’s the most expensive fix you’ll face later. In our region, the minimum slope for effective drainage is 2% (a quarter inch per foot), aimed away from structures, but clay-heavy soil resists drainage and holds water close to the base. Crowning the driveway—making it highest in the center, sloping to both sides—or a steady cross-slope are both common, but it depends on the lot and what you’re draining toward.

If the driveway slopes the wrong direction, sending water toward a garage or house, you’ll need a trench drain or a channel drain at the base; these carry water away before it has a chance to sit, seep, and freeze. In some lots you’ll be forced to “daylight” water to the street or use a dry well, but heavy soils like ours mean infiltrated water lingers, freezing and heaving the subbase. None of this is cosmetic. Most base failures in Allegheny County driveways start with a little standing water, which in our subgrade freezes and lifts the pavement—cracks follow in no time.

A final warning: sending your runoff across a neighbor’s property is a liability issue no matter what your permit says. In crowded hilly neighborhoods, runoff must be handled on your own lot—or you’ll buy someone else’s drainage lawyer.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Allegheny County

Does Allegheny County have local asphalt plants?

Yes, Allegheny County is part of the greater Pittsburgh metro market and has multiple local asphalt plants and quarries. This means that most residential driveway paving in the county benefits from short material haul distances, which helps keep costs down compared to more rural areas. Having several plants within the county also increases competition among contractors and helps ensure you get timely service in peak season, since supply bottlenecks are rare. Still, paving prices reflect the deeper freeze-thaw cycle, clay and shale subgrade, and hilly lots—not just material cost. A typical full asphalt replacement, including local material delivery, runs $3,500–$9,000 in Allegheny County in 2026.

How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Allegheny County?

You do not need to sealcoat your Allegheny County driveway every year—despite what some ads claim. In our climate, sealing every two to three years is right for most driveways, especially once new asphalt has fully cured (typically 6–12 months after installation). Over-sealing leads to cracking and peeling, as layers build up and trap moisture or uncured volatiles. Focus more on good crack filling between seal coats, since water infiltration in freeze-thaw conditions is what kills driveways here. A typical sealcoating visit costs $200–$700, but quality prep is everything; the cheapest quotes often skip cleaning, patching, or proper coverage and will wear off quickly in our weather.

Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door?

Yes, in Pennsylvania, if you sign a paving (or any home improvement) contract at your home, the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) gives you three business days to cancel in writing without penalty. This is a statewide rule and applies in Allegheny County. The law also requires written contracts for work over $500, and it's illegal for contractors to offer to waive your insurance deductible. Always check for a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, which is required for any contract over that amount. Be careful with high-pressure, on-the-spot deals—use your time to verify their registration and references. A typical driveway contract locally could be anywhere from $1,500–$9,000, so never commit without reviewing the full written terms.

What does an asphalt overlay cost in Allegheny County?

An asphalt overlay—meaning a fresh layer of new asphalt placed over a sound existing base but with some surface wear or cracking—is a popular option in Allegheny County’s older subdivisions. In 2026, expect typical overlay costs of $1,500–$4,500 for a standard single-family driveway. The actual price depends on square footage and how much prep the base needs. If the underlying base is failing, has widespread cracks, or shows signs of heaving, you’ll need a full tear-out and replacement instead, which is pricier. Always get the base evaluated before accepting an overlay bid—shortcuts won’t last in our heavy freeze-thaw climate.

How long does a driveway installation take?

For a typical single-family asphalt driveway in Allegheny County, full tear-out and replacement (including new gravel base and compacted asphalt) takes most crews two to three days start to finish. Overlay projects often finish in one long day. Concrete driveways take a little longer—typically three to five days—since the concrete must cure before you drive on it. Scheduling is tightest in the summer peak, so book ahead if you want the best crew slot. Weather delays are common here, especially in the spring and fall. For a full replacement, expect to pay $3,500–$9,000, depending on size and site grading needs.

Why are my driveway edges crumbling?

Crumbling driveway edges are a widespread problem on older Allegheny County driveways, especially on hilly lots found around Pittsburgh and neighboring boroughs. The most common cause is thin or unsupported pavement at the edges, often from the original builder saving on material by feathering out the margin. With each winter, freeze-thaw cycles and our local clay soil soften the edge, and traffic or plowing then break it up. The fix is proper edge treatment: paving contractors should install a thicker, supported edge on a solid base and may also taper the asphalt to prevent abrupt drop-offs. Full replacement (with proper edge detail) ranges from $3,500–$9,000 depending on length and layout.

Are there stormwater rules on paving in Allegheny County?

Yes—expanding, widening, or paving new areas does bring stormwater requirements into play in most Allegheny County municipalities. Enlarging your driveway can increase runoff, and most townships require that you do not direct new water flow onto a neighbor’s property, the sidewalk, or into the street without approval. Some areas may cap the total impervious surface on your lot or require you to add drainage features if the footprint is expanded. These reviews happen when you apply for permits, especially if your driveway connects to a public or state-maintained road. Base county permits for existing driveways are less strict, but any layout change should be discussed with both municipal and, if it’s a state road, PennDOT District 11-0. For added drainage or trench work, plan for some added cost over the usual $3,500–$9,000 replacement job.

Is gravel a cheaper option in Allegheny County?

Gravel is definitely the budget-friendly driveway surface in Allegheny County, especially for longer rural or semi-rural drives where paving would be expensive. In 2026 you can expect a typical gravel driveway install to cost $900–$3,500, depending on length and base work needed. Gravel drives do need regular maintenance—grading and re-topping every couple of years, especially after our freeze-thaw seasons and heavy spring rains, are the local reality. But for many homeowners outside the dense city core, it’s a practical option if you don’t mind ruts and dust. It also gets around some of the stormwater rules because it’s not counted as an impervious paved surface in most municipalities.

What is the difference between binder and surface course?

Asphalt driveways are built in at least two layers—binder course below and surface course on top—everywhere in Allegheny County where you want lasting work. The binder (sometimes called the base or intermediate) course is thicker and coarser, designed to provide strength and absorb movement from the clay and shale below during freeze-thaw. The surface course (wearing course) uses a finer mix, creating a smooth, tight, and weather-resistant finish. For a typical full replacement (usually $3,500–$9,000), you want both layers specified in your contract, with compacted depths called out: most County driveways see a 2–3" binder course and a 1–1.5" surface course. Never accept a quote that just promises "three inches of asphalt"—make them break down the layers.

Should I repair or replace my Allegheny County driveway?

If your Allegheny County driveway has isolated cracks under a quarter inch, small patches and crack filling—usually $200–$800—are sensible and delay bigger costs. But driveways with deep alligator cracking, major heaves, settling, or widespread crumbling edges (common in clay and shale areas) are rarely worth patching. The underlying base is probably failing, and a thin overlay or cheap patch job, even with sealcoat, will not hold up through our winter cycles. Full replacement or structural overlay is usually required when the base can’t drain or support traffic anymore. Have a reputable local contractor probe the base before you invest in anything—spending good money on patching won’t help a base problem.

What slope does a driveway need for drainage?

A driveway in Allegheny County should have at least a 2% slope—that is, a quarter inch per foot—aimed away from buildings to keep water moving off the pavement. Given our heavy freeze-thaw, flat driveways or those sloping toward a garage are a recurring failure. If your lot makes this difficult, a trench drain or channel drain installed at the low point can help. Clay soils make drainage even more crucial because water lingers at the base and freezes, lifting and cracking the pavement. Good grading—more than any product—extends the lifespan of any paving work, whether it’s a full replacement at $3,500–$9,000 or just a resurfacing. Always have drainage and slope in writing on any contract.
