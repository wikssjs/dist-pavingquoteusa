This is why prices here can seem wide apart: two quotes for the “same” job might differ by thousands, and the reason is usually what’s done under the surface, not what you see on top.

For a new driveway, the lion’s share of your money goes to excavation, hauling, stone base, and base prep — not just asphalt or concrete. Weak subgrade and inadequate base are why driveways in Greensburg and North Huntingdon fail when they’re still young. Simple sealcoating or patching usually addresses only the surface, and is appropriate only if the base is still solid. Choosing a paving company

A solid paving job in Westmoreland County starts with the crew you hire. Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for all jobs over $500 — you can and should verify this directly at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For workers’ compensation, check via WCAIS, not just a piece of paper handed to you. Honest paving contractors will provide their physical business address (not just a cell number), use owned equipment (rarely rented if they’re local), and should be able to reference completed driveways from four or five years ago — because the real workmanship test comes after several winters, especially with our area’s freeze-thaw cycles and clay/shale subgrade.

Ask which asphalt plant they source from. Here, most reputable local companies use plants in Hempfield Township or quarries in the Greensburg area. If a contractor can't name their supplier, you’re likely talking to a broker or a “traveling crew” — not someone you want on your property.

  • Workers’ compensation coverage (verify via WCAIS)
  • Physical local business address — not just a mobile number
  • Owned and maintained equipment
  • References from work done over 3 years ago in Westmoreland County
  • Supplier name for asphalt/aggregate (should be local, not out-of-county)
  • Detailed written scope: excavation, base depth, compaction method
  • Written contract with full pricing and scope (required by state law over $500)
  • No upfront payment beyond material delivery costs
  • No offer to “waive” your insurance deductible — that’s illegal in PA
  • Crew appears with durable work trucks and signage, not unmarked pickups
  • Clear explanation of base preparation and drainage approach

Red flags: door-knockers, “today-only” deals, no address, no supplier, or contractors pushing overlays on failed bases. If what’s under the driveway isn’t in writing, keep looking.

Comparing materials in southwestern Pennsylvania

Here in southwestern Pennsylvania, with our 30 psf ground snow load and heavy freeze-thaw cycling, material choice makes a difference in both cost and longevity. Asphalt is flexible and can “move” with the shallow heaving our shale and clay soils see in winter. That’s why in the majority of Westmoreland County’s driveways — especially post-1960 subdivisions — asphalt remains the default. Over time, even freeze-thaw can’t easily break it; it simply forms gradual ruts or surface cracks. Concrete can last far longer but has its own weakness: de-icing salts. With the amount of salt and plowing on local aprons, most concrete failures happen from spalling and scaling, not from typical cracking.

Concrete’s up-front cost is higher, but if you genuinely avoid salt (most owners don’t, especially on slopes common in Unity and Hempfield), it can last 30 to 50 years. On asphalt, salt isn’t as critical — the risk is water seeping and freezing, so regular crack filling is enough. Pavers work on stable bases for turnarounds and patios, though are labor-intensive. Gravel remains practical for rural, long runs in Unity or Penn Townships; it’s affordable if you maintain it every few years with fresh stone.

Caring for a new Westmoreland County driveway

The simplest way to extend the life of any driveway in Westmoreland County is regular crack filling, especially before any freeze-thaw cycle can drive water to the base. Spring and fall are prime times for this maintenance. Supporting the edges is next — nothing undermines a new driveway faster than allowing wheels or lawn equipment to break the sides. Good drainage is essential: water left to run or puddle will shorten even a perfect driveway’s useful life.

On new asphalt, wait at least 5–7 days before parking, and avoid turning the wheels in place for several weeks. Dumpsters and heavy trucks on fresh asphalt cause permanent ruts. For concrete, a full cure can take a month — don’t put vehicles on it before then.

De-icing is a major issue. Salt eats at concrete, especially at joints and edges. On asphalt, the main risk is thaw-refreeze, which opens cracks further. Alternatives like calcium magnesium acetate are effective on both, but cost more per use. Sand is the safest, if you can manage the mess. In our climate, expect to weigh cost, safety, and the long-term health of your driveway all winter.

Widening, extending or adding a parking pad

Adding onto your existing driveway — whether it’s for a second car, a turnaround, or to reach a detached garage — means building new sub-base over undisturbed (likely clay or shale) soil. This is not the same as resurfacing what you own; the new section will always be at risk of future settling compared to the original, and the joint is a permanent weak spot. In much of Westmoreland County, including Greensburg, expanding your driveway or adding a new pad may also require a building or zoning permit, especially if you alter curb, sidewalk, or site drainage. Setback rules can stop you from paving right up to the property line, and don’t forget that impervious surface limits sometimes trigger further review for runoff.

Don’t overlook the need for a driveway approach permit if you tie in at a state-maintained road: this isn’t a municipal issue but goes through PennDOT District 12 via the Westmoreland County maintenance office. If adding or replacing gravel with asphalt, budget for significant base prep — and know that aggregate supply for rural extensions, especially in Unity, often means higher trucking costs from Greensburg-area quarries.

Why Westmoreland County driveways fail from the bottom up

Every paved driveway in Westmoreland County is only as sound as the ground it sits on. Here’s the basic stack: below your driveway is the natural subgrade (usually a mix of clay and shale in this county), then a compacted aggregate base — ideally 6–8 inches deep — and finally the paving layer itself, be it asphalt or concrete.

In our climate, frost heave is the relentless enemy. Winter cold (as low as 8 °F design in this region) forces water in the clay/shale subgrade to expand and push upward, lifting sections of the driveway unevenly. If the aggregate base isn’t deep or compacted enough, or if water isn’t drained away, this lifting cracks the pavement from underneath. Heavy freeze-thaw and hilly layouts only make it worse.

When your quote comes in much lower, compare the written scope. Cutting corners here means less or no excavation, reusing a failed base, thin or poorly compacted stone, maybe skipping compaction between lifts. It’ll look fine as the crew leaves, but those hidden savings surface as cracks and potholes — usually in the third spring. Always require, in writing:

  • Excavation to at least 8 inches below finish grade
  • A minimum 6-inch compacted aggregate base of crushed limestone
  • Base installed and compacted in 3-inch lifts — never all at once
  • Drainage plan included (swales, pitch, or piping if necessary)
  • Compaction proof — roller rating, passes per lift
  • Top asphalt (or concrete) thickness called out, not just “pave”

If a contractor will not specify these numbers, keep shopping.

Diagnosing a failing Westmoreland County driveway

If your driveway in Westmoreland County is showing distress, the right next step depends on the base condition — which contractors may misstate to tilt you toward a higher- or lower-dollar job. You can self-diagnose most needs with a quick walk and a straightedge.

Alligator cracking (tight, widespread cracks resembling scales) means the base has failed: an overlay will not hold and you’ll waste your money. Isolated straight cracks are normal for aging asphalt; a crack fill and seal usually suffices. Potholes and depressions (“birdbaths”) mean water or traffic has compromised the underlying stone, which probably means partial or full base replacement. Edge crumbling signals that your driveway was built with low, no, or failed edge support. Spot repairs may do, but if the decay is widespread, plan for full work soon.

An overlay offered on a base that’s moving or saturated is a short-term fix at best. Any contractor who doesn't probe the base before quoting is guessing, and often at your expense. Here’s a summary for local conditions:

What you need before paving in Westmoreland County

In most Westmoreland County municipalities, simply repaving your driveway in its existing size and shape does not require a building permit — you can save a call if you’re not modifying curbs or drainage, especially in places like Hempfield Township, where sealcoating generally proceeds without review. Expanding, replacing, or changing your driveway in a way that affects sidewalks, drainage, or curb lines typically does trigger a zoning or building permit, especially in the City of Greensburg. For any tie-in or approach at a state-maintained road, the authority is PennDOT District 12 via the Westmoreland County maintenance office, not your borough or township.

Increasing the paved area also means more runoff. Stormwater rules often limit the total impervious surface you can add, and channeling water onto a neighbor’s land — even by accident — creates liability, permit or not. When you hire a contractor, verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov as required for contracts over $500, and use WCAIS to confirm workers' compensation. If your house was built before 1978, ask about EPA lead-safe practices (essential for concrete demo, less often needed for asphalt).

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Westmoreland County

Is an overlay worth it or should I start over? If your Westmoreland County driveway has surface cracks but a base that is still solid, an asphalt overlay can be an economical way to extend its life by 8–15 years. Typical cost for an overlay in this area is $1,450–$4,300, which is much less than full replacement. However, overlays are only worthwhile if the underlying gravel base and subgrade are stable and show no signs of shifting or alligator cracking. If there are birdbaths, soft spots, or crumbled edges, overlays are temporary fixes — you’ll end up paying again to fix persistent problems. Make sure your contractor tests the base first, and never accept an overlay quote without that up-front investigation.
What is the difference between binder and surface course? In Pennsylvania, a proper asphalt driveway is installed in two layers: a binder course and a surface course. The binder layer (often called the “base course”) is thicker, coarser, and provides structural strength — crucial in Westmoreland County, where clay and shale soils can shift during freeze-thaw. The surface course is finer and provides a smooth finish. Skipping the binder to save money will dramatically shorten your driveway’s life; you might see problems after just a couple winters. A quality job blends both, often at a full replacement cost of $3,400–$8,600, depending on size and prep.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? For a typical extension or widening project in Westmoreland County, costs run from $3,400–$8,600 for a full-depth build with proper base and compaction. The lower end applies to modest pads; wider additions, steep sites, or jobs farther from aggregate supply centers (like parts of Unity Township) can push you higher. Always include the cost to match or repair seams, as well as the likely permit and drainage review if you’re in Greensburg or expanding near the curb/road. Do not let a contractor talk you into just “paving over” the lawn: new base must be built for stability.
Are there stormwater rules on paving in Westmoreland County? Yes, especially if you are adding impervious surface by extending your driveway or adding a new parking pad. In much of Westmoreland County, including city areas like Greensburg, increasing paved area can require a permit or stormwater review, with specific runoff limits. You must keep water from running onto your neighbor’s property, regardless of permit. Expect to need a drainage plan for projects above 400 square feet. Permits themselves are usually not required for sealcoating (which costs $200–$650) or minor patching, but any major change in size or grade brings scrutiny.
How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Westmoreland County? In this climate, most asphalt driveways benefit from sealcoating every 3–4 years. With our local freeze-thaw cycles and moderate snow load, sealcoating helps keep water and salt from penetrating small surface cracks. In Greensburg and Hempfield Township, sealcoating does not require a permit. You’re looking at $200–$650 per single application (typical is around $375), but that’s money well spent compared to major repairs. Sealcoating every year is unnecessary and, in some cases, can actually build up too much material near the house or curb.
How much does driveway paving cost in Westmoreland County? As of 2026, expect to pay $3,400–$8,600 for a full asphalt replacement with a new base in Westmoreland County, and $4,800–$11,500 for concrete. Resurfacing an existing asphalt drive is less: $1,450–$4,300. Square footage, subgrade condition, and approach work (especially on state routes) drive cost. Concrete carries higher upfront expense but has a longer service life, so factor maintenance into your decision. Sealcoating and minor repairs are the best value for money if the base is still good.
What does compaction in lifts mean? “Compaction in lifts” means installing the aggregate base in thin layers — usually 3–4 inches at a time — and compacting each layer thoroughly with a roller before adding the next. In Westmoreland County’s clay and shale subsoils, this step is critical: it eliminates air pockets that would later fill with water and cause frost heave. Doing the full 6–8-inch base all at once without lifts invites immediate and future settling, and undermines your investment of $3,400–$8,600 or more in new asphalt. Always require your contractor to specify lift depth and compaction method in the contract.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? In Westmoreland County, wait at least 5–7 days before driving on freshly laid asphalt. The first 48 hours are most critical; after that, light foot traffic is okay but avoid parking or turning wheels when stationary. Full cure (especially for trucks or dumpsters) takes several weeks, particularly in cooler weather. Concrete demands even more patience: you’ll usually wait 2–4 weeks. Rushing it risks ruts and surface marks — and given that you’re paying $3,400–$8,600 for quality work, there’s no good reason not to wait for proper curing.
Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door? Yes. In Pennsylvania, under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, you have a three-business-day right of cancellation for any home improvement contract signed at your residence. This applies throughout Westmoreland County, no matter what a salesman may say. You don’t owe a reason — simply deliver written notice within three business days. This rule covers all jobs over $500, including typical driveway work ranging from $1,450–$8,600. If a contractor tries to rush you out of this right or asks for a non-refundable deposit, that’s a red flag.
Does road salt damage driveways in Westmoreland County? Road salt absolutely accelerates wear on driveways here, but the effect depends on the material. Salt and de-icers attack concrete, leading to surface spalling and scaling, particularly near driveway approaches treated with plow salt (a frequent issue in Greensburg and Murrysville). On asphalt, the main risk is that salt melts snow, which then seeps into cracks and refreezes, prying them open further. A concrete replacement in this area costs $4,800–$11,500, making it especially important to avoid using rock salt. For asphalt, regular crack filling will control most of the damage.
What causes driveway cracks in Westmoreland County? The main culprits are freeze-thaw cycles, clay/shale subgrade shifting, water penetration, and edge breakdown. Southwest Pennsylvania’s winters produce repetitive expansions and contractions that stress even new drives. Older, pre-1980 homes (over 72% of those in the county) are especially vulnerable if their base prep was thinner than modern standards. Regular crack filling (costing $200–$650) and good drainage are your first line of defense. Most cracks start small — address them early before water reaches the base, which otherwise leads to far bigger repairs or total replacement.
