Clinton County paving prices by scope — 2026

Clinton County homeowners face a unique set of realities when it comes to driveway paving. With only about a third of local homes (33.6%) being single-family and just 1,107 owner-occupied households, most driveway projects here are for detached homes — and that's where the price and the value both land. Our region is known for hard winters, frequent freeze-thaw cycles, and a shale subgrade that ruts and heaves if underbuilt. In this environment, most of the cost difference between two driveway quotes isn’t visible at the surface — it's in the excavation depth, the aggregate base thickness, and the time spent on site preparing a subgrade that actually lasts.

Don’t let sticker shock fool you. Hauling asphalt and stone into Clinton County costs more than you might expect because the nearest plants are in Centre and Lycoming Counties. The longevity of your driveway comes down to how those materials are used below grade. A cut-price proposal almost always means less base, thinner asphalt, or weaker compaction — and those are the shortcomings that show up as cracks, soft spots, and failed edges years later, long after the check has cleared.

Asphalt or concrete for a Clinton County driveway?

Choosing between asphalt and concrete in Clinton County comes down to what this climate does to your pavement. In our region, with ground frost running 36 inches deep and some of the highest snow loads in Pennsylvania, freeze-thaw cycles are relentless. Asphalt moves with the ground — it flexes. That means asphalt can survive decades of heaving and freezing, provided the base is thick enough to handle our local shale soil and the frequent expansion-contraction cycles. It’s no accident asphalt is still the default here for replacement driveways.

Concrete, on the other hand, lasts longer — but only if you don’t use de-icing salt. That’s a tall order in Clinton County’s winter. Salt accelerates scaling and spalling, leading to surface breakdown long before its advertised lifespan. Plenty of concrete driveways here have failed within 10–15 years, not because of poor pouring, but simply from regular salting. If you’re meticulous about avoiding salt and want a crisp, lighter look, concrete is still an option, but prepare for a higher upfront cost.

Pavers and gravel both have their place. Pavers drain better, can be repaired by swapping out single units if a problem occurs, and handle movement well — but they’re the most expensive to install. Gravel works best on long, rural runs where plowing isn’t constant and regular grading is expected. It’s the go-to for driveways well off the main road and keeps initial costs down.

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is one of the most widely pushed services in Clinton County, but a lot of what’s said about it is sales pitch, not fact. Here’s the honest take: Sealcoating acts as a sunblock and a shield against gas, oil, and minor spills. It slows oxidation and keeps your asphalt looking clean, which is worth something for curb appeal. What sealcoating does not do is add actual thickness, strength, or solve any fundamental problems below the surface. If your base is failing or you have alligator cracking, no sealcoat in the world will fix it — you’re putting a coat of paint over a cracked wall.

The other side is overdoing it. Applying sealcoat every year just piles up layers that eventually peel or turn brittle, especially with our wide temperature swings. The first sealcoat should go on only after the asphalt has cured completely, and from there, every 2 to 4 years is smarter maintenance. Too-early sealing, especially in this climate, traps outgassing oils and can reduce the pavement’s service life.

Crack filling is the maintenance that pays dividends here. Sealing cracks before water gets in prevents freeze-thaw from splitting the pavement, and protects the base from erosion. For a standard size driveway in Clinton County, expect to pay $175–$600 for a professional sealcoat — but if you’re being quoted $99 or $125 by someone going door to door, that’s a thin spray-on job, not a real application. If you’re not seeing a squeegee machine or a proper prep process, you’re buying little more than a temporary blackwash.

Reading a driveway proposal

Most Clinton County homeowners wouldn’t accept a one-line proposal for a roof — but with paving, that’s what gets handed out most often. A detailed driveway quote separates the real contractor from fast-in, fast-out operators. You should see the measured square footage, the planned excavation and disposal, the aggregate type and thickness (in inches), the compaction details (and whether it’s done in lifts, which is critical for durability), the thickness of each asphalt layer after compaction, drainage handling, and exactly what’s happening at the edges. Good quotes include cleanup and even a modest repair to the lawn edge, because a neat edge protects the driveway from crumbling outwards.

Payment schedule matters too. You want a small deposit to get on the schedule, with the balance due only after completion. Most importantly, check what the warranty actually covers. In our trade, almost no standard warranty covers cracks — only potholes or clear material failure. If a quote just says “thick new blacktop, $X,” there’s no way to compare it or to hold anyone to the result. This is how most people in Clinton County pay for three inches of asphalt and get two compaction, or pay for a subbase upgrade and get a thin dusting of crusher run.

What to put in a Clinton County paving contract

Nothing locks in a decent driveway like a job quoted by specification, not just price. Too many contractors base proposals on a wink and the words “three inches of new blacktop,” which means nothing without context. In this county, with deep frost and shale base that loves to shift, the numbers matter — especially excavation and aggregate base depth. Unlike milder climates, Clinton County jobs need more excavation, a thicker layer of stone, and true compaction in lifts. The thickness of each layer must be stated “compacted,” not “laid,” because asphalt shrinks 20–25% when rolled.

This is the single most effective way to get durable results. Ask for each of these items to be stated in writing. If a contractor can’t supply numbers and specific product names, keep shopping. With material and haul costs higher in Clinton County, the only way to compare value is to compare apples to apples by spec.

  • Measured square footage of driveway, not just a width and length guess
  • Excavation depth below finished grade, stated in inches
  • Aggregate base thickness and specific material (crusher run, AASHTO #57, etc.)
  • Compaction method — vibratory roller or plate, and confirmation it's done in lifts if more than 4 inches
  • Binder course: thickness in inches, as measured after compaction
  • Surface course: thickness in inches, after compaction, and plant mix designation (e.g. 9.5 mm or 19 mm)
  • Edge treatment for unconfined edges (shouldered with additional stone, or set by hand)
  • Drainage slope in percentage, and statement on water direction away from structures
  • Cleanup scope — lawn repair, leftover debris, access restoration
  • Warranty details — years, coverage, and any crack exclusions

How to vet a paving contractor in Clinton County

In Clinton County, where travel surcharges and imported materials drive up paving costs, picking the right contractor is all about checking details no scammer can fake. Every home improvement contract over $500 must be with a company holding a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration — verifiable at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For hiring anyone with employees, make sure they carry workers’ compensation, which you can check online via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). But don’t stop there.

A trustworthy paving contractor in this area will have a local business address — not just a mobile number — and will know exactly which hot mix asphalt and aggregate supplier they use. In Clinton County, if they can’t name which plant in Centre or Lycoming County provides their mix, they aren’t ordering it directly. You’re better off with a contractor who owns their equipment and can provide references from at least three years ago, because a “good” driveway can look fine for the first year or two before cracks or ruts reveal shortcuts. Watch for non-local plates or out-of-region company names. Quality work stands up over time on Lock Haven’s postwar infill lots and up the valleys alike.

  • Verify HIC registration for contracts over $500
  • Verify workers’ compensation coverage through WCAIS for any employees
  • Get a Clinton County or neighboring-area business address, not just a cell
  • References from at least three years back — not just recent jobs
  • Evidence of owned, maintained rolling and compacting equipment
  • Ability to name the Centre or Lycoming County plants supplying asphalt/aggregate
  • Proof of full-time crew, not just casual hires or driveway brokers
  • Line-item contract with specs (not “by the job” quotes)
  • Documented warranty, stated in writing
  • Insurance certificate issued specifically to your address
  • No request for full payment or large cash upfront deposits
  • Physical address for service of process — beware PO boxes only

Major red flags: out-of-state tags, refusal to put details in writing, knocking on doors with leftover mix, or offering “today only” deals. These tactics are how you pay for two rows of stone and a blown-on blacktop that fails in three winters.

When paving can actually be done in Clinton County

Paving in Clinton County isn’t just a question of preference — our seasons set absolute limits. Hot-mix asphalt must be laid at the right temperature to compact properly, usually only when daytime highs are at least 50°F. With a winter design temperature of 2°F and more than 6,000 heating degree days, our paving window is strictly April to early November. Local asphalt and aggregate plants, all located in Centre and Lycoming Counties, close down for winter. That means in December through March, there is simply no local supply, no matter what a contractor might claim. Concrete needs above-freezing temps to cure, so winter pours are out unless expensive additives are used — and even then, it’s not advisable for a residential driveway.

Scheduling gets tight fast. From June through early September, lock in your spot weeks ahead, as that’s prime season after schools let out and before first frost. If crews offer late November or December dates here, they are either unaware of the local plant shutdown or they’re cutting corners with leftover or cold material — bad news for longevity. The best values, if you can line them up, are in early spring or early fall, when crews have capacity but the weather still cooperates. Don’t let anyone sell you paving work during a cold snap.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

For homeowners in Clinton County, a building permit is usually not required for simply resurfacing or repaving a driveway in the same spot and size, but this can vary between municipalities. Where regulations do kick in is at the road — any connection to a public road (the “approach”) often requires a driveway permit. For houses in Greene Township, for example, any new driveway or change that connects to a township road requires a township driveway permit. If your driveway meets a state highway, then PennDOT District 3 must issue an approach permit. In Lock Haven and nearby towns, each code office runs its own process.

If you widen your driveway or increase the amount of impervious surface, stormwater runoff becomes your responsibility. Directing water onto a neighbor’s property can make you liable for damages whether or not you get a permit. Drainage has to be managed so it runs onto your land, not across someone else’s, and steep front yards need special care.

Regardless of the project size, verify your contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, and if they have employees, check for workers’ compensation insurance through WCAIS at wcais.pa.gov. Only hire contractors who put this information in writing — it’s required by Pennsylvania law for jobs over $500.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Clinton County

How deep should the aggregate base be here? For Clinton County, with its shale subgrade and severe freeze-thaw cycles, aggregate base should run deeper than in most of Pennsylvania. The typical minimum is 6 inches of compacted stone for light-use driveways, but on riverfront or soft ground in the Lock Haven area, 8–12 inches is standard to prevent rutting and heaving. Cutting corners here is the biggest cause of early failure. It’s one of the reasons a full-depth replacement can range from $3,100–$7,900: you’re paying for excavation and stone volume, not just the surface blacktop. Make sure your contract spells out the depth (compacted) by number, not just “good base.”
What is the difference between binder and surface course? In asphalt paving, the binder course is the strong, coarser first layer that sits on top of the stone base. Its job is to add load-carrying ability and absorb movement from the ground. The surface course is a finer, smoother layer placed on top to provide a clean, tight finish. In Clinton County, a driveway needs a solid binder course to handle shifting shale soils and freeze-thaw cycles. You’ll see good contracts list both with thicknesses stated as compacted (like 2.5" binder + 1.5" surface). Skipping the binder or skimping thickness is how $2,000 jobs become quick failures. Expect a properly built two-course asphalt drive in the range of $3,100–$7,900.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating do different things. Crack filling uses a flexible material to plug individual cracks, keeping water from getting underneath and freezing, which is the top cause of early driveway failure in places like Clinton County. Sealcoating is a thin liquid applied over the entire surface to block UV light and repel oil, but it’s cosmetic and prevents some surface drying rather than fixing problems. You shouldn’t pay for sealcoating on a driveway with open cracks — the cracks must be filled first, or water will get in and undo the job. For a typical drive: crack filling might cost $200–$800, while full sealcoating runs $175–$600.
Do I need a permit to repave a driveway in Clinton County? For most Clinton County homeowners, repaving or resurfacing an existing driveway does not require a building permit as long as you do not change its size or layout. However, if you connect to a public road, especially if you expand or move the entrance, a driveway “approach” permit is likely needed — this comes from your township (like Greene Township) or PennDOT District 3 for state roads. Cities like Lock Haven have their own enforcement office. Always check before starting, since violating permitting rules can delay projects and cause headaches. For reference, a standard asphalt overlay runs $1,300–$4,000 if no expansion or major change is made.
Are there stormwater rules on paving in Clinton County? Yes. Adding or replacing a driveway can trigger stormwater rules in Clinton County, especially if you increase the area that’s paved (impervious surface). Local authorities want to be sure runoff doesn’t flow onto a neighbor’s land, street, or toward sensitive riverbanks. Even without permit requirements, Pennsylvania law can hold you liable if you change drainage patterns that damage other properties. If you’re switching from gravel to asphalt or concrete — particularly in town or close to the West Branch Susquehanna River — plan on discussing your grading and runoff management with your contractor. Well-planned drainage may increase costs (e.g., $3,100–$7,900 for full replacement with regrading).
Asphalt or concrete for a Clinton County driveway? For Clinton County’s climate, asphalt is usually the safer, lower-maintenance choice. Our freeze-thaw cycles and long winters mean asphalt can flex rather than crack, and repairs are more affordable. Concrete has a longer lifespan when cared for — but only if you avoid de-icing salts, which most people end up using in our snowy winters. Concrete installations cost more up front ($4,400–$10,500) and may fail early if exposed to salt or shifting soils. While some homeowners still prefer the crisp look of concrete, most opt for asphalt’s balance of flexibility, repairability, and price in our area.
Does road salt damage driveways in Clinton County? Yes, especially concrete. Clinton County’s winters demand heavy use of de-icing salt. Salt accelerates surface breakdown in concrete, leading to scaling and spalling, sometimes within a few years (even though the base could last decades). Asphalt can soften under heavy salting, but mostly shrugs off the effect; its main risk is from water getting in through cracks, then freezing. If you want long life out of concrete, you must avoid salt and shovel instead — a tall order when sidewalks and driveways get icy. If salt damage is a concern, stick to asphalt, which can take it better and be repaired at moderate cost ($1,300–$4,000 for overlay).
Why are paving quotes in Clinton County so different from each other? Bids in Clinton County range widely because most of the real cost of a driveway is buried under the blacktop — excavation, depth of stone, haul distance for materials, and time on proper base compaction. Cheaper quotes usually mean less stone or thinner asphalt, not a better deal. In our rural market, hauling aggregate from Centre or Lycoming County adds real costs. Be wary of any proposal that doesn’t spell out thicknesses, material specs, or includes a “leftover mix” discount. Most differences are not in the surface, but in what you can’t see — a full-spec, durable job often runs $3,100–$7,900 for asphalt with new base.
What does sealcoating cost in Clinton County? A professional sealcoat job on an average single-family driveway in this county costs between $175–$600. Be suspicious of anything under $150 — real work here takes prep, crack cleaning, and enough material to apply by squeegee, not just a blown-on mist. Door-to-door offers and late-summer “leftover” deals undercut the process and rarely protect your investment. Remember, sealcoating offers cosmetic improvement and some weather resistance, but alone won’t fix cracks or extend the life of a driveway whose base is failing. For best results, plan on sealcoating every 2–4 years, not every season.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? Depressions or “birdbaths” in Clinton County driveways nearly always trace to either a thin aggregate base or poor compaction on installation. Our shale soil and heavy snow loads demand a deep, stable base — if that’s shortchanged, asphalt sags over time wherever cars sit or water pools. In freeze-thaw regions, trapped water quickly worsens the problem as ice lifts and settles the pavement. Repair can involve patching (about $200–$800 per area), but if depressions spread, a full-depth rebuild is sometimes the only cure. Always inspect new work for ponding before final payment.
When is the best time of year to pave in Clinton County? Peak paving season here runs from late April through October, when temperatures are reliably above 50°F and asphalt plants in Centre and Lycoming Counties operate at full speed. Early spring and early fall offer the best scheduling flexibility and sometimes slightly lower prices. Summer is high demand: plan ahead. Never let a crew pour hot-mix in cold snaps — asphalt will cool too quickly and won’t compact properly, leading to weak, porous pavement. If you’re quoted work past early November, be skeptical of quality. For reference, full asphalt replacement runs $3,100–$7,900, so timing matters for long-term value.
