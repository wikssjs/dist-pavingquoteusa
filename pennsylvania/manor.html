What a new driveway costs in Manor

In Manor, where 54.5% of homes are single-family and the homeownership rate stands at 75.8% (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), driveways are both a necessity and a long-term investment. Unlike attached communities, the cost here is almost always on the owner — and the price can vary widely, often for reasons hidden below the surface. What most homeowners don’t realize is that much of your driveway cost isn’t from what you see above ground, but from what’s built beneath: base preparation, drainage, and the quality of materials used under the asphalt or concrete.

Manor’s freeze-thaw climate, with our pronounced temperature swings and winter lows down to 12 °F, is punishing to poorly built surfaces. Water expands as it freezes, so any neglected base, shortcut in compaction, or subpar drainage will lead to heaving and cracking within a few years. Limestone-based subgrade is not the rule in Manor as it is in some neighboring townships, but it still matters: the further your site is from central Lancaster, the more haul cost factors in from plants nearer the city. And while material might look the same everywhere, below-average base prep or improper drain design will almost guarantee you’ll pay twice in the long run — especially in a market like ours where most of the price difference is literally underground.

How to vet a paving contractor in Manor

Choosing a paving contractor in Manor isn't just about who answers the phone first. This is a market where hauling costs and base preparation drive final results, and the wrong operator can be impossible to reach when problems appear. Pennsylvania requires contractors to have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for jobs over $500. Always verify them at the official Attorney General site (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Workers' compensation is mandatory for any employer; check their coverage at WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov).

Deal directly with a business that’s local, has an address you could drive past, and owns their paving equipment. Ask to see references from jobs done at least three years ago — fresh asphalt always looks good, but Manor’s climate tells the truth in year three. Demand to know which asphalt plant and aggregate quarry they buy from (there are local quarries and plants near Lancaster; a real contractor will know them by name). If they hedge or mention a supplier outside Lancaster without good reason, they’re likely bringing in out-of-area crews or brokering.

  • Verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov
  • Check workers' compensation status at WCAIS
  • Physical Lancaster-area address, not just a mobile number
  • Owner-operated or clearly documented local crew
  • Owned paving and excavation equipment (not all rentals)
  • References from jobs done over three years ago, in Manor/Lancaster area
  • Insurance coverage certificate—never take their word for it
  • Asphalt supplier and aggregate quarry (should be local to Lancaster)
  • Written warranty terms specific to subgrade and drainage issues
  • Contract with full scope, not just a lump sum bid
  • No “special today” discounts; beware pressure for cash payment
  • No excuse for working without a contract above $500

Biggest red flags: vague scope, refusal to name suppliers, out-of-town plates on all vehicles, and no references past the last season.

Why you can’t pave in February

Paving in Manor is strictly a seasonal business, not a matter of preference but of physics and plant schedules. Hot-mix asphalt must be laid and compacted while it’s still hot — the window for this closes fast when the outside air is cold. Our winter design temperatures hit 12 °F and the region’s freeze-thaw swings mean that freshly laid asphalt will never bond or compact properly below roughly 45–50 °F surface temp. When it’s cold, the material stiffens before it can be worked, leaving it porous and prone to early failure.

A bigger constraint is material — the local asphalt plants serving Manor and greater Lancaster County simply don’t run in winter. Once the freeze sets in, they shut down until mid-March or even later, depending on weather. Concrete has its own rules: if it’s below freezing, it won’t cure correctly, and you’re almost always left with scaling and flaking come spring.

The practical upshot: April through October is paving season here. Bookings fill months in advance, peaking in late June and July. The shoulder seasons — April–May and September–October — are the best time as crews have better availability and pricing tends to be sharper. If someone offers to pave in late November or February in Manor, that’s a sign they’re not in this business for the long haul — or you’ll get leftover mix and a result that won’t last through its first winter freeze.

Numbers, not adjectives

Most homeowners in Manor shop driveway quotes by price, but that’s the wrong game. What protects you is specification — write it down and require real numbers. Generalities like "three inches of asphalt" are meaningless if you don’t know whether that’s compacted thickness or just what comes out of the dump truck (asphalt can lose up to a quarter of its depth during compaction). Ask for the thickness of each layer after compaction. Same for the stone base: our region's freeze-thaw cycling demands a deeper, well-compacted base than milder climates — at least 6–8 inches of clean 2A limestone aggregate for most Manor driveways, more if the soil below is soft or wet.

Demand these numbers in your quote, not adjectives:

  • Excavation depth below final grade (in inches)
  • Aggregate base type (2A limestone, etc.) and compacted thickness
  • Compaction method and proof (vibratory roller, compaction test if required)
  • Asphalt binder (base) course thickness as compacted
  • Asphalt surface (top) course thickness as compacted
  • Asphalt mix designation (what type and where it’s from)
  • Number and thickness of lifts for both stone and asphalt
  • Edge treatment (finished flush, backed up with topsoil, etc.)
  • Drainage plan (where stormwater runs—written, not verbal)
  • If overlays, tack coat type, and how transitions/edges are handled

When you have these figures, you can compare apples to apples. If a contractor hesitates or can’t specify in writing, that should end the conversation.

What you need before paving in Manor

For most Manor Township homeowners, repaving an existing asphalt driveway in its current footprint does not require a township permit or right-of-way approval — Lancaster County townships generally allow resurfacing and same-size replacements without a street opening or public works review. However, if you're changing where your driveway meets the street (the approach or apron), or adding a new driveway connection, that triggers a permit handled not by the county or township, but by the appropriate authority. In the City of Lancaster, for example, the Bureau of Engineering has control over approaches, while PennDOT District 8-0 gets involved if your drive meets a state road.

Any time you expand your driveway or change surface drainage, you could trigger stormwater management requirements: adding impervious area focuses water runoff. Note — even if not required by code, directing stormwater toward a neighbor’s property opens you to civil liability. Good contractors will identify drainage paths in their scope.

Always verify that your contractor holds current HIC registration (hic.attorneygeneral.gov) and has active workers' compensation coverage (WCAIS). Pennsylvania mandates a written contract for any job over $500, and you retain a 3-day right to cancel.

Why Manor driveways fail from the bottom up

Every driveway in Manor, whether asphalt or concrete, rests on a layered system built from the bottom up: natural subgrade soil, compacted aggregate base, then your surface. What type of base, how it’s installed, and how it drains are everything in Lancaster County’s climate — much more than the surface material itself. This is a region with pronounced freeze-thaw cycles: when water saturates the ground beneath a driveway, then freezes, it expands and exerts immense upward pressure (frost heave), lifting and cracking the surface above. Simply pouring thicker asphalt or concrete won’t fix a weak or wet base — in fact, it makes problems show up faster and bigger.

The subgrade in Manor varies, but what matters most is that it’s properly compacted and drained before any aggregate is laid. Over this, we install a base layer of clean, angular stone — locally, 6–8 inches of 2A limestone is standard, compacted in 2–3 lifts with a vibratory roller. Further north or in heavy clay, you may need more. Testing compaction isn’t something most paving contractors do, but it’s absolutely worth paying for if you want the job to last 20–30 years.

Here’s where the price difference comes in: a “cheap” job usually means they’ve skimped on base prep. Common shortcuts — laying over an old base, failing to dig out soft spots, skimping on aggregate, compacting too little or not in lifts — are invisible until year three, when the surface starts to settle, rut, and alligator crack. Write into the contract: minimum 6–8 inches of 2A limestone (compacted, in lifts), excavation to remove all soft or organic material, and written confirmation of equipment and compaction method. Don’t pay extra for a thicker asphalt mat on top if the base underneath isn’t right.

Why driveways behave differently in Manor

The subgrade below your Manor driveway isn’t the same as what you’d find just a few miles away. Much of East Hempfield, for example, sits on limestone karst that's prone to settling and needs extra-thick bases with particular drainage design. Manor’s soils are somewhat less tricky, but you still need to pay close attention to how water moves: a poorly drained or compacted base over local soils will fall victim to freeze-thaw heave by year three.

Our regional frost depth is 30 inches — meaning anything left wet or weak below that depth is at risk for heaving and settlement. Patch jobs that hold up for years on sandy or gravelly soils in other counties will fail early here unless base and drainage are correct for our actual conditions. Lancaster city’s older housing stock (much of it pre-1940) means many driveways were built before modern base specs: if you’ve got a narrow shared or apron-style drive, odds are good the original base is marginal or nonexistent. These locations often show edge cracking and rotten drainage, and any replacement must be an upgrade.

If your contractor isn’t talking about tailoring the base to what’s below — and not just selling you topping or a thicker mat — they’re not addressing why driveways in Manor age as they do.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Manor

Why are paving quotes in Manor so different from each other? The price spread on Manor driveway paving comes mainly from what happens beneath the surface, not just the visible asphalt or concrete. Base preparation drives most of the cost, and that work is invisible the day the crew leaves. If one quote for a full-depth asphalt replacement is $3,600–$9,200 and another is much lower, it's likely the cheaper crew is skipping excavation, laying less aggregate, or doing minimal compaction. This is especially risky in Manor because our freeze-thaw cycles and subgrade require quality base work to prevent cracks and heaves. Always demand exact written specs: depth of excavation, type and depth of stone, compaction in lifts, and proof of local experience. Price reflects not just labor, but lasting quality under local conditions.
Does Manor have local asphalt plants? Yes, the Lancaster area has local asphalt plants and limestone quarries that serve Manor and neighboring townships. Most reputable driveway contractors should be sourcing material directly from these plants, which minimizes haul distance and keeps costs competitive. Some outlying Manor sites may have slightly higher transportation costs since the plants are clustered around central Lancaster, but you shouldn’t see massive upcharges unless the job is unusually remote. If a contractor can’t say where their material comes from or is vague about plant location, be wary—they may be brokers or subbing the work out. For a typical Manor driveway replacement, you should see prices in the $3,600–$9,200 range, with materials sourced locally.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? In Manor—and across Lancaster County—a good driveway should slope at least 1–2% away from buildings to drain water efficiently. That's about 1/8 to 1/4 inch drop per foot of width. On older, pre-1940 drives in Lancaster, poor grading is a common cause of early failure and standing water: adding new pavement over a bad grade just traps water next to the house or leaves puddles. Your paving contract should specify the finished slope, not just that “drainage will be addressed.” For most homes, correcting grading is part of a full-depth replacement (typically $3,600–$9,200), but overlays alone won’t fix underlying drainage issues. Make sure you see the slope requirement in writing.
Does sealcoating fix cracks? Sealcoating does not repair actual cracks in asphalt—it’s a protective coating, not a structural fix. In Manor, where our freeze-thaw cycles are hard on pavement, small cracks rapidly enlarge with water and freezing. Sealcoating helps slow weathering if the surface is still solid, but cracks need to be cleaned and filled before any coating goes on. If your driveway has more than minor cracks, consider crack filling and spot patching (usually $200–$800), then sealcoating ($200–$700) for preservation. If cracks are deeper than a quarter inch or spreading, that points to a failed base and it’s time for a bigger repair.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? After a new asphalt driveway is installed in Manor, wait at least 48–72 hours before driving regular traffic over it—longer if the weather is very hot or humid. Asphalt stays soft for weeks after paving and can be easily scarred by tires, sharp turns, or heavy vehicles. For concrete, curing time before vehicle traffic is 7 days or more. Remember, the investment is significant—installing a new driveway runs $3,600–$9,200 for asphalt and $5,100–$12,500 for concrete—so resist the urge to rush. Keeping vehicles off strengthens the bond and extends service life.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes, if you’re installing a new driveway or altering the width or location where your drive meets a public road in Manor, you’ll likely need a permit—but not from the same office as building permits. In Lancaster city, the Bureau of Engineering handles driveway approach and curb cut permits, while PennDOT District 8-0 covers connections onto state highways. For simple resurfacing in your existing footprint, no permit is typically required in township areas. Always clarify with your contractor, as this is the one place a paving project can get unexpectedly expensive or delayed, especially if the public sidewalk or curb is affected. Approach costs are usually included in a full replacement bid ($3,600–$9,200), but fees and inspections are extra.
Should I repair or replace my Manor driveway? The answer depends on the severity of your driveway’s issues and what’s happening underneath. If you have isolated cracks under a quarter inch, crack filling and patching ($200–$800) can buy you 1–3 more years. Surface-only distress usually justifies resurfacing ($1,550–$4,600). But if you see widespread sinking, alligator cracks, or potholes—especially in Manor, where freeze-thaw and drainage are tough on old bases—it’s often smarter to do a full-depth replacement ($3,600–$9,200) than keep paying for repairs that don’t last. A good contractor will dig test spots to check base condition before giving advice.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in Manor? Concrete driveways in Manor typically range from $5,100–$12,500, depending on width, length, and site-specific factors like grading or drainage. Concrete costs more upfront than asphalt but generally lasts longer—30–50 years with minor maintenance. With 38.2% of local homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many replacement projects in Manor require correcting poor old bases or addressing cracked approaches, which can add to the cost. Prices do rise with length and any custom finishes, but the main driver is always base prep and proper subgrade compaction.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? Depressions in Manor driveways are usually caused by poor base compaction, drainage failures, or settling subgrade. In this climate, every freeze-thaw cycle exaggerates minor imperfections, and water left standing accelerates deterioration. Older driveways—especially those in Lancaster’s pre-1940 areas—often lack proper stone base or have clay spots that settle over time. The fix involves removing the soft area, rebuilding with 6–8 inches of compacted aggregate, then restoring the surface. Minor patching costs $200–$800, but if depressions are widespread, a full replacement is likely the only real fix. Any quote for repairs should include written details about the base work—not just a “surface patch.”
