Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, most residential paving is straightforward, but you should still verify contractor credentials and check whether your specific approach to the street triggers permits. Pennsylvania requires paving contractors to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If a contractor is soliciting driveway work door-to-door after storms (“storm chasers”) and can’t provide verifiable HIC registration, treat that as a major red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most PA municipalities, but the driveway approach where it meets the public road may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. Before excavation, ask your contractor what approvals are needed and confirm with the township/borough—especially if you’re widening a driveway, changing the curb cut, or working near sidewalks and rights-of-way.

Water runoff: under Pennsylvania stormwater rules (Chapter 102), driveway work can’t increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should address grading and may need drainage solutions if water is currently ponding or flowing toward neighbors.

Workers’ compensation: verify at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews expose you to liability.

Step-by-step for Lehigh homeowners: (1) Confirm HIC registration online, (2) request a current certificate of insurance (general liability) and proof of workers’ compensation, (3) ask whether a driveway access permit is required for the curb/road approach, and (4) get a written scope that explains grading and runoff control before work begins.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Lehigh driveway?

For Lehigh homeowners, Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor. Asphalt and concrete both expand and contract with temperature swings, but they tend to fail differently. Asphalt is more flexible, which helps it handle repeated movement in the base; when maintained with sealcoating every 2–3 years, it typically performs well through Lehigh winters. Concrete, while durable, is more rigid—when the subbase shifts or road salt accelerates surface deterioration, concrete can spall or develop cracks that widen over time.

Housing age matters in Northampton County. With about 52.8% of homes built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways were constructed with older base assumptions that may not meet modern vehicle loads and drainage expectations. When the base is even slightly compromised, the “best-looking” material won’t outlast a proper foundation rebuild—this is why full replacement is often favored over resurfacing. Sealcoating and crack filling can extend a pavement’s life when the base is sound, but they won’t correct a failing foundation caused by water infiltration and freeze–thaw movement.

A concrete example: if your current asphalt is alligator-cracked but the base tests solid, resurfacing in the $2,500–$7,000 band may be justified. If the driveway has heaving at the edges, soft spots, and widespread structural cracking, full replacement is the right conversation in the $4,500–$12,000 band—choosing concrete “on top” of a weak base usually won’t eliminate the underlying issue. The “right” material is the one paired with the right base prep.

For additional contractor compliance, verify Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration at the PA Attorney General’s website if the project exceeds $500.

Next, get 2–3 written, itemized quotes—not lump sums. Ask for a breakdown covering labor and materials (mill/excavate, base aggregate, compaction, asphalt/concrete mix, edging/curbing, drainage/grading, and cleanup). Clarify what happens if you see settlement within the first year, or if sealcoat coverage isn’t performing as expected due to early water intrusion. What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Lehigh?

Failing base conditions in Lehigh often show up through patterns that repeat every winter. Look for areas where the pavement “punches” underfoot, depressions that re-form after snowmelt, or cracks that spread in multiple directions and worsen quickly after freeze–thaw cycles. Edge raveling—where the asphalt breaks away from the sides—can indicate the base is losing support or washing out. Alligator cracking with adjacent settlement, pumping behavior when a crew removes a patch, and standing water after rainfall are also signs your base may be soft or improperly graded. Another clue is the mismatch between surface appearance and performance: a driveway that seems to hold up in summer but heaves in winter usually has a water/void problem below the asphalt. In cases like this, sealcoating and crack filling are not a fix; they’re preventive steps for relatively sound pavements. If you have widespread structural cracking and softness, contractors commonly recommend full replacement in the $4,500–$12,000 band because overlays in the $2,500–$7,000 range won’t correct base failures and can fail again sooner in PA’s freeze–thaw climate.
