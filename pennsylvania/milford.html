Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, driveway paving usually falls under the same contractor rules as other home improvement work. If a job exceeds $500, contractors are generally required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. You can verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If you’re approached by a contractor after a storm (“storm chasers”) and they can’t provide verifiable PA HIC registration, treat that as a major red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving often does not require a building permit in many PA municipalities, but the driveway approach—where your driveway meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or the local municipality, especially if work affects the apron, curb line, or drainage. Check before excavation.

Water runoff matters. Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should verify grading and may need drainage solutions (for example, improving slope to prevent ponding).

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage using wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews on your property can expose you to liability.

Step-by-step for Milford homeowners: (1) confirm HIC registration online; (2) ask for a current certificate of insurance (general liability and workers’ compensation); (3) keep copies of the COI and HIC number; (4) ask whether your scope triggers any driveway access permit; (5) request the proposal to spell out drainage/grading responsibilities.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Milford driveway?

For Milford homeowners, Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle is the defining factor. Asphalt and concrete both move with temperature changes, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better when the base is well-built and drainage is correct. The tradeoff is maintenance: asphalt should be sealed every 2–3 years if you want to slow oxidation and keep the surface from drying out. Concrete is rigid; it can look great long term, but it can spall or crack when freeze–thaw stresses and road salt exposure combine—particularly if the base underneath is not stable.

Because about 38.4% of Milford homes were built before 1980, many existing driveways are at or past the point where “band-aid” repairs make sense only temporarily. (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024). That’s why we often recommend full replacement for failing bases instead of overlaying over structural problems. In the Bucks County climate, resurfacing works best when the base is sound and surface distress is limited. If you’re already seeing depressions, pumping, or widespread alligator cracking, resurfacing can simply trap the problem under new pavement.

Here’s where the dollars usually justify the option. If your driveway is structurally failing, asphalt full replacement (often $4,500–$15,000) tends to beat “cheap fixes” because it restores the base system. But if your driveway is in decent shape and you want an intermediate step, asphalt resurfacing (often $1,800–$8,400) can be the better value—provided drainage and base prep are addressed. Concrete becomes most compelling when you’re willing to invest upfront and want a long-lasting, low-maintenance surface, and when the base is properly engineered for Milford conditions.

How to choose a driveway paving contractor in Milford

Choosing the right contractor in Milford is about verification first, then scope clarity. Start with Pennsylvania licensing: for qualifying home improvement work, confirm HIC registration through the PA Attorney General’s Office at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. If a contractor references industry certifications such as IICRC, ask for documentation and check that their stated certification is current (you can confirm credential status through the certifying organization’s tools, or request written proof).

Get 2–3 itemized, written quotes—not a single lump sum. The best bids break down labor and materials, show what’s included for base prep, specify asphalt thickness or concrete thickness, and clearly state whether they will remove and haul the old driveway. Read the exclusions: ask whether disposal, saw-cutting, permit coordination for the driveway approach, and any drainage/grading work are included. If an insurance claim is involved, ask what documentation they provide and how they handle repairs that require spec compliance.

Warranty should be in writing. Ask for workmanship warranty length (how long they stand behind the paving install) and material warranty terms. Payment schedule matters too: in general, avoid paying more than 10–15% upfront and hold back payment until the job is complete and cleanup is verified. Finally, insist on a written timeline with an arrival estimate and a completion window.

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in Milford?
