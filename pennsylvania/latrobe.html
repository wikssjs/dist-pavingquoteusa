Asphalt or concrete for a Latrobe driveway?

With Latrobe’s hard winters and regular freeze-thaw cycles, asphalt is by far the most common choice for driveways. Asphalt has a flexible structure that can move slightly as the clay and shale soils shift when they freeze and thaw. This means fewer cracks and longer service life, even as the ground heaves beneath. For most Latrobe homeowners, asphalt’s up-front cost and ability to withstand winter weather make it the practical choice.

Concrete offers the longest lifespan—30 to 50 years—but only if you avoid using de-icing salt. In Westmoreland County, salt is essential for winter safety, but it reacts with concrete to cause spalling and scaling. Walk any older neighborhood and you’ll see examples: concrete that looked sharp for a few winters, then started to pit and crumble. That trade-off matters more than the numbers suggest. Most Latrobe homeowners do eventually use salt, whether intentionally or from windblown road applications, so expect more maintenance with concrete than advertising promises.

Pavers can be a good fit for decorative circles, small driveways, or properties with significant slopes, as individual units are replaceable. Gravel is best in rural settings and long driveways, where drainage is critical and budgets are tighter.

Permits and approvals for paving in Latrobe

If you plan on repaving your existing driveway in Latrobe, as long as you aren’t changing the width, footprint, or altering drainage, you typically do not need a building permit—especially in most of Westmoreland County’s municipalities. But if you are replacing or expanding a driveway in the City of Greensburg and your project alters the curb, sidewalk, or impacts drainage patterns, expect to need a zoning or building permit from the city.

The spot most homeowners miss is the approach where your driveway meets a state or township road. That area is regulated and, if altering or expanding, you’ll need a separate approach permit from PennDOT District 12 via the Westmoreland County maintenance office. Do not rely on your contractor’s word alone—a missed permit here can cause problems at closing or resale.

Whether or not permits are required, you’re still responsible for handling runoff properly. Expanding the impervious area can trigger local stormwater management requirements. If drainage pushes water onto a neighbor’s property, you may face private liability even if your project met city code.

Before any work above $500, Pennsylvania law requires a written contract. Make sure your contractor is registered as a Home Improvement Contractor through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General (hic.attorneygeneral.gov) and has workers’ compensation insurance (wcais.pa.gov) if they employ anyone else on the job.

In Westmoreland County, price alone won’t tell you if a paving quote is solid. Start your search with the basics: Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor registration for driveway work over $500, which you can check at the Attorney General’s registry (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). For any company with employees, you can confirm workers' compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. But paperwork is just the beginning.

The best contractors in Latrobe and the Greensburg area operate with a fixed business address, not just a cell number that could move states by next week. They own and maintain their paving equipment, which matters for quality and reliability. Over half the difference in how long a driveway lasts here is in the ground work, so ask for references from three or more years ago—if a company can’t show you driveways that have held up through a few freeze-thaw cycles, move on. One thing unique to our area: local aggregate supply matters. Contractors should be able to tell you what plant or quarry they pull material from. If not, they’re likely outsourcing or brokering.

How often should a Latrobe driveway be sealed?

Sealcoating is widely pushed in Westmoreland County, but it’s not magic—just a surface treatment that slows down the rate at which sunlight and chemicals break down asphalt. It repels minor oil leaks, prevents the top layer of binder from drying out, and can bring back a deep black appearance. What it cannot do is glue a crumbling driveway back together, make thin asphalt thicker, or rescue a failing base. If you have alligator cracking or areas sinking below grade, sealcoating is a band-aid, not a cure.

Too many contractors recommend sealing every year—don’t. Applying sealcoat before new asphalt has cured (usually at least 6 to 12 months in our region), or reapplying every spring, causes peeling and cracking as layers build up. Every 3 to 4 years is plenty for a healthy surface in Latrobe, and only after a thorough cleaning and crack filling. A thorough crack fill with hot-pour rubber is much more important than a thin coat of the cheapest sealer. If your driveway is structurally sound, a quality sealcoating can cost $175–$650, but watch out for cut-rate jobs that are little more than black water sprayed on by a crew that vanishes after the check clears.

The price of sealcoating should reflect the area to be covered and prep work done. Anything much lower than $175 for a typical driveway almost always means corners are being cut, either in material or in workmanship.

Latrobe's ground and what it means for paving

Latrobe sits on tough ground for paving. The local subgrade is mostly clay and shale, especially around Greensburg and the suburbs. Clay holds water and is prone to heaving as it freezes in winter, while shale can fracture and create uneven settling. That means every driveway here—concrete or asphalt—needs a compacted stone base thicker than what you’d see in neighboring counties with sandier soils. Cut corners on the base, and within a few winters you’ll see ruts or even full sections shifting out of line.

The frost line reaches 36 inches here, so a shallow or poorly graded base traps moisture that will freeze-thaw dozens of times each winter. Paired with heavy freeze-thaw cycles, the wrong design leads directly to surface cracks, edge crumble, and pothole formation. Compounding this is the era of construction: in the post-1960 subdivisions of Hempfield and North Huntingdon, quick builds often meant minimal base prep. That’s why so many driveways in these developments now need resurfacing or complete reconstruction—original construction simply wasn’t deep enough to withstand Latrobe’s winters.

Why you can't pave in February

In Latrobe, paving is not a year-round operation. Hot-mix asphalt must be laid when both the air and ground temperatures are high enough for proper compaction—ideally 50°F and rising. Our winter design temperature here is 8°F, which means February is out of the question: the asphalt would cool and stiffen before it can be properly compacted, leaving a rough, porous surface that won’t last long through freeze-thaw cycles. Local asphalt plants in Hempfield Township and Greensburg-area quarries simply shut down for the season because of these cold conditions, so most contractors literally can’t get fresh mix delivered from November to late March or early April.

Concrete is just as constrained. Below-freezing days interfere with the curing process, leaving weak spots that will quickly show damage come spring. Any company offering to pave your driveway outside of May through early November either doesn’t know the climate or doesn’t care about quality. Busy season for crews is June through August. Shoulder seasons—late spring or early fall—are best if you want more flexible scheduling and sometimes sharper pricing. If a crew offers to pour or pave on a 35°F gray November day in Latrobe, you’re buying future potholes.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Latrobe

Does road salt damage driveways in Latrobe? Yes, and it matters more than most people think. In Latrobe, road salt is essential for winter safety, but it speeds up the wear on both asphalt and concrete driveways. Concrete here, especially, is vulnerable—salt causes spalling, where the top layer pits and flakes off, turning a smooth slab into a patchwork of rough spots in just a few winters. Asphalt is less sensitive but can still show premature wear if salt washes into cracks, accelerating freeze-thaw damage. If avoiding salt isn’t realistic (and for most of Westmoreland County, it isn’t), asphalt’s ability to flex and resist deep cracks is why you see it used on 90% of single-family lots. Major repairs to salt-damaged slabs can run the same as installing a brand new asphalt driveway—$3,200–$8,300 isn’t unusual for full replacement.
What slope does a driveway need for drainage? Proper drainage is crucial for any Latrobe driveway because our clay-heavy soils and freeze-thaw cycles make ponding water a recipe for rapid failure. You want a minimum slope of 2%—that’s about a quarter inch per foot—so water sheds away from the house and doesn’t pond on the driveway. For a 20-foot stretch, that means at least a 5-inch drop from the garage down to the apron. Without this grade, water sits in depressions, seeps into the base, freezes, and pushes up or cracks the surface. Many driveways in older Greensburg and Unity subdivisions lack this pitch and need regrading before resurfacing. Regrading alone, depending on the extent, can run $1,400–$4,200 if paired with a full asphalt overlay.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? In Latrobe, timing matters, especially in hot summer months when asphalt stays soft longer. After a fresh installation, you should wait at least three full days in warm weather before driving on it. In cooler or cloudy weather, two days may be enough, but don’t rush it—parking too soon will rut the surface, leaving tire dents that never come out. Even after you resume driving, avoid parking in the same spot and turning the steering wheel while stationary for the first month. The surface continues to harden over several weeks, and full curing can take up to a year. Sealcoating shouldn’t even be considered until six months later. If you crack it too soon, you may be looking at repairs costing $200–$800 just for patching.
Are there stormwater rules on paving in Latrobe? Yes—both municipal and practical. In Latrobe and the rest of Westmoreland County, adding driveway surface means you’re creating more runoff. If you expand or replace your driveway and it alters the way water flows—especially if it now drains toward a neighbor or public sidewalk—you could trigger zoning or drainage review, particularly in the City of Greensburg. Even without permit requirements, state nuisance law still applies: you’re liable if your new driveway sends water onto someone else’s property. Good paving contractors grade carefully to shed water correctly, but if you need new drains or regrading, budget an additional $1,400–$4,200 on larger projects.
Asphalt or concrete for a Latrobe driveway? For most Latrobe properties, asphalt is the better balance of cost, durability, and winter survival. Concrete can last twice as long on paper—up to 50 years—but only if you can avoid using de-icing salt, which few homeowners realistically do here. Salt leads to pitting and breaks down the concrete long before age alone would. Asphalt isn't hurt as much by salt and stands up better to the heavy freeze-thaw cycles in Westmoreland County. Plus, when asphalt does need repairs, they're easier and less expensive. Expect to spend $3,200–$8,300 for quality asphalt replacement versus $4,600–$11,000 for durable concrete.
Why does my driveway have depressions that hold water? Depressions—“birdbaths”—are common in Latrobe because our local clay and shale subgrade can move with each spring thaw. If the original base was thin, or the ground wasn’t graded and compacted properly, freeze-thaw cycles push sections up or let them sink under car tires. Over time, these spots catch water, which freezes again and deepens the dips each winter. Usually, fixing this means cutting out the soft sections, rebuilding the stone base to proper depth, and patching. For small areas, expect to pay in the $200–$800 range for repairs; widespread issues often mean it's more cost-effective to resurface or replace.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in Latrobe? A new concrete driveway in Latrobe typically costs $4,600–$11,000, depending mainly on size, access, and preparation needed for the clay and shale subgrade. Expect costs at the higher end if your property is on a slope or needs extra base to fight frost heave, which is common in older sections of Greensburg and Unity Township. Concrete's appeal is low ongoing maintenance, but remember—using de-icing salt shortens its lifespan. If you want a surface to last 30–50 years and are diligent about winter care, it can be worth the spend.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating get lumped together, but they solve different problems. Crack filling is about durability—it uses hot rubberized material to seal gaps wider than a quarter inch, keeping water out of the base (which is especially important for Latrobe’s freeze-thaw cycles). Sealcoating, by contrast, is a cosmetic and protective surface treatment; it slows UV and chemical damage but can’t fill structural cracks. Done alone, sealcoating won’t rescue a driveway with cracks—those will show right through, and water will keep getting into the base. In Latrobe, a good maintenance job involves crack filling every 2–3 years and sealcoating every 3–4 years, with prices around $175–$650 for most driveways.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in Latrobe usually means excavation, base prep, and tying new material to the old—especially challenging on clay subgrade. For asphalt, expect to pay $3,200–$8,300 if you’re redoing the entire surface, or $1,400–$4,200 for a partial overlay with good existing base. If you’re only widening a short section or adding parking, the price will be at the lower end, but drainage and curb changes may trigger extra costs and permits, especially within city limits. Always budget for edge reinforcement; skimping there is why widened sections often crack away from the main drive.
