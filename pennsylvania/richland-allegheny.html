Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors generally must be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before you hire anyone, verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. After storms, be especially cautious of “storm chasers” who solicit driveway work door-to-door without verifiable HIC registration.

Permits are situational. In many cases, residential driveway paving does not require a building permit, but altering the driveway approach—where it meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit from your local municipality and/or PennDOT. Check before excavation, especially if you’re changing the slope, adding a new apron, widening, or connecting to sidewalks or curb lines.

Runoff matters here. Pennsylvania’s stormwater requirements (Chapter 102) require that paving projects not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public right-of-way. That’s why contractors may include regrading, proper crown, and drainage solutions as part of the scope.

Also verify workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov. If a crew isn’t properly covered, you can be exposed to liability if something goes wrong.

Step-by-step for Richland homeowners: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request a certificate of insurance for general liability and auto (if hauling is involved), (3) provide proof of workers’ comp, (4) ask what permit is required for your specific driveway approach, and (5) ensure the scope shows how water will be managed after the work.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Richland driveway?

For Richland homeowners, Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor when comparing asphalt and concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better than rigid materials; however, it performs best with consistent maintenance—most driveway owners plan for sealcoating every 2–3 years to protect the surface from oil spotting, oxidation, and winter wear.

Concrete can be a great long-term surface, but in Allegheny County it’s more likely to show spalling or cracking when road salt, water infiltration, and repeated freeze–thaw cycling hit joints and edges. Since about 48.5% of Richland’s housing stock was built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), many driveways have reached or passed the point where the base and drainage are aging too—not just the top surface. When that happens, resurfacing choices can narrow quickly.

Here’s a realistic dollar example: if your driveway is in good shape and the base is solid, an asphalt overlay in the $2,500–$6,500 range can extend service life for about 8–15 years. But if you’re seeing settlement, pumping at joints/edges, or widespread alligator cracking, trying to “save money” with an overlay often leads to early failure, making full replacement in the $4,500–$11,000 band the more cost-effective long-term approach. In contrast, concrete’s higher upfront cost (typically $7,000–$16,000) can be justified when you want a low-maintenance surface and you can address drainage and sub-base correctly from day one.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Richland?

The best way to tell is to look for symptoms that the pavement is being stressed from underneath—not just surface aging. Common signs in Richland include alligator cracking that spreads quickly, areas that “pond” or stay wet after rain, and rutting or depressions that return even after patching. You may also notice edges that crumble, separation from aprons, or cracking that repeats in the same pattern year after year. These issues often tie back to drainage and base compaction problems that worsen during Pennsylvania’s freeze–thaw cycle: water infiltrates, freezes, expands, and the pavement structure loses support. Homes built before 1980 make this more common across the area (48.5% pre-1980, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), so an older driveway may look like it only needs sealing but actually needs base repair or full replacement. If you see widespread fatigue cracking plus movement, sealing alone usually won’t solve the problem; you’re more likely looking at overlay limitations or moving into full replacement pricing rather than staying in the smaller $2,500–$6,500 overlay band.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Richland's climate?
