Sealcoating: what it does and what it doesn't

Sealcoating gets advertised as miracle insurance for driveways in South Abington, but it’s best looked at as a limited maintenance step. What sealcoating does: it protects the surface by keeping the asphalt binder from oxidizing and drying out, repels some oil and gas damage, and gives tired gray pavement a black, uniform look. That satisfies many, especially when you want curb appeal fast.

But there is no structural benefit from sealcoating—none. If you already have cracks, base movement, or alligatoring, sealcoat won’t fix them. Done too often, sealcoat actually causes problems by trapping moisture or building up enough film to peel and flake. Never seal new asphalt until it’s fully cured, which in our climate can mean a full season. Annual sealing is usually a waste, and every real contractor in Lackawanna County will tell you every 2–4 years is plenty in our weather.

The maintenance that extends driveway life most isn’t a coat of black: it’s timely crack filling. Water getting to the subgrade is what destroys driveways here. Crack filling, done with heated rubber and properly routed cracks, keeps moisture out much more effectively than a quick roll-on sealer. A cheap sealcoat—what you get with an unlicensed crew and a price at the bottom rung ($200–$750)—means watered-down product sprayed on thin, barely lasting one season and potentially flaking in colder months.

If you’re choosing between the two, prioritize crack filling before sealing. And always wait for the right curing time before the first coat. Fast jobs, especially for suspiciously low prices, are rarely a good investment in South Abington.

Why South Abington driveways fail from the bottom up

If there’s one truth about driveways in South Abington, it’s this: failure starts where you can’t see it—the bottom. Every proper driveway is built in layers: the natural soil (subgrade), then a compacted aggregate base (typically crushed stone), then the asphalt or concrete surface. Each layer is there to distribute the load above it to a wider area below. Doesn’t matter how thick your asphalt is—if the base is thin, weak, or poorly drained, you’ll get cracks, depressions, and heaves within a few cycles of winter.

Our local soil, a stiff glacial till, is tricky. It naturally holds pockets of moisture, so when deep cold hits (frost depth is 36 inches in northeastern PA), water freezes and expands, lifting the driveway in spots—a process called frost heave. As that happens, the surface flexes and cracks, then settles out unevenly as the ice melts. That’s why real base prep matters more here than in warmer states: six to eight inches of 2A modified aggregate stone, compacted in lifts, not just dumped and spread. Drainage is non-negotiable; water must move away from the drive and not linger below the pavement.

This is where the low bids cut. A crew that’s in and out in a day is skipping something: shallow excavation, laying new material over a failed base (“overlay”) when a rebuild is needed, using less or cheaper aggregate, skipping compaction. You can’t see it when they leave, but by year three, the driveway tells the tale—cracks, waves, edge collapse. Before you sign, demand base depth (in writing—at least 6–8 inches), aggregate type (“2A modified”), and proof compaction was done in layers, not just on top.

Why driveways behave differently in South Abington

The base under your driveway in South Abington is mostly glacial till—angular stones, sand, silts, maybe some clay, left by retreating glaciers. This glacial till doesn’t drain like gravel, but it isn’t as sticky as clay soil either. In South Abington, its presence means water can collect in parts of the base unless drainage is properly managed. And during our classic Pennsylvania freeze-thaw cycles and harsh winters (36-inch frost depth), trapped water expands and contracts, stressing the driveway from beneath.

Unlike limestone regions to the west or pure clay zones south of here, our glacial till requires extra attention to both the base depth and the design of any under-drains. If you skimp, frost heave works quickly, and that’s why many mid-20th-century homes in the township—built before driveway codes were standard—now need edge repair or outright widening. An overlay might buy a few years, but without addressing the base on glacial till, cracks return fast.

So, a standard recipe from a contractor who doesn’t know this soil is a risk. Local knowledge, and building for both drainage and frost movement, is the only way paved surfaces in South Abington last as long as they should.

The paving season in northeastern Pennsylvania

In South Abington, the paving season is non-negotiable, and it’s defined by our climate, not contractor preference. Asphalt can only be installed when ground and air temperatures are reliably above 50°F. Below that, hot-mix asphalt cools too quickly to properly compact—if a crew paves in cold weather, you’ll end up with a brittle, porous surface that falls apart in a few years.

Winter design temperature for this region is just 4°F, and the local asphalt plants in Lackawanna County close for the winter because there’s simply no demand and no way to guarantee quality. As for concrete, it should never be poured below freezing or without cold-weather protection—the chemical reaction that cures concrete simply won’t happen right, and early surface failures are almost guaranteed.

The upshot: roughly May through early October is reliable paving weather. Shoulder seasons—late spring and early fall—are when you’ll get the best value and more availability from local crews; summer peak is booked out months. If someone offers to pave your driveway in late November or early April, they are either desperate for work or cutting corners—don’t reward it. Northeast Pennsylvania’s paving calendar is set by temperature, plant operations, and the reality that shortcuts in seasonality show up as failed driveways a few years later.

Comparing paving estimates in South Abington

The only way to compare paving quotes in South Abington—or anywhere in Lackawanna County—is to break them apart line by line. Demand that a quote spells out the measured square footage, base excavation and disposal, aggregate type and depth, how compaction will be performed, the number of asphalt courses and each course’s thickness after compaction, edge treatment, and specific drainage work. Warranties should spell out what is and is not covered; most exclude cracking, which catches a lot of people out when they learn driveway cracks are considered “expected aging.” If the quote is just a single line—“asphalt driveway, $6,800”—walk away. That’s the one thing almost all early failures have in common: a single number and no accountability for the work beneath the surface.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

For most South Abington homeowners, repaving or resurfacing an existing driveway in the same footprint does not require a building permit—saving you some paperwork. However, if you are putting in a new driveway, widening, or making changes where your driveway meets the public road (the “approach”), local municipalities control that process. For driveways accessing a state route, approach permits run through PennDOT District 4-0; for township or city roads, your local municipality has authority. In Scranton, specific permits are required for new driveways, curb cuts, or anything affecting access or the public right-of-way.

If you increase the impervious surface—widening or adding pavement—be aware that local runoff rules may trigger. You are responsible for making sure water from your driveway does not discharge onto a neighbor’s property or cause any downstream problems, permit or not. Stormwater management is enforced locally in Lackawanna County, so check before work starts if your project is more than a resurfacing.

For any work over $500, contractors must have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Verify this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation coverage is required for all employers; you can check this via the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) for your protection.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in South Abington

What is the difference between binder and surface course? Both binder and surface course are layers of asphalt, but they serve different roles in a South Abington driveway. The binder course is the thicker, coarser layer placed directly on the compacted stone base. It’s made with larger aggregate and creates the strong, load-bearing foundation for your driveway—essential for Northeast PA’s freeze-thaw cycles and subgrade movement. The surface course (sometimes called “top” or “wearing” course) is the final, smoother layer with smaller stones and more asphalt binder. It provides the finished look, smoother ride, and the first line of defense against weather, but it won’t carry structural loads alone. Both are typical in a properly rebuilt driveway at the higher end of the price range, such as a full replacement ($3,600–$9,400). Cheap or thin driveways sometimes skip the binder, which leads to early cracking and rutting.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in South Abington? A new concrete driveway in South Abington typically runs from $5,200–$12,500, with the “typical” job landing around $8,300. This reflects extra site prep in our region—they have to excavate the local glacial till, add a deep stone base for frost protection, and often form thicker slabs compared to southern states. Concrete is more expensive up front than asphalt, but can last 30–50 years with little maintenance. Most people here choose it if they want a low-maintenance, long-term solution and don’t mind the initial outlay. Just remember: concrete also needs a good base, especially with our 36-inch frost depth, or it will crack like anything else. The price includes site excavation, base install, rebar/grid, forming, pouring, and finishing—not including decorative features.
Can I pave over my existing driveway? In some cases, yes—you can overlay your existing asphalt driveway in South Abington, but there are important cautions. The base beneath must still be sound and stable. An overlay, costing $1,550–$4,700 for most homes here, adds a new layer of asphalt over the old, sealing small surface cracks and bumping up the appearance. But if you have deep cracks (over a quarter inch wide), rutting, or sinking spots, just paving over them is a temporary fix at best. All the underlying problems will reflect back through the new surface within a few freeze-thaw cycles—the famous “reflective cracking.” If the base is compromised, you’ll waste money on an overlay and end up replacing it sooner. Always ask for a core sample or have a contractor confirm base integrity before skipping a full replacement.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes, changes to the driveway “approach”—where your drive meets any public road in South Abington—generally require a permit, and not always from who you’d expect. If your property fronts a state road, PennDOT District 4-0 handles approach permits. For township and city roads, it’s your local municipality. In Scranton, a permit is explicitly required for a new approach, curb cut, or driveway expansion in the right-of-way. The process is separate from any building permit and ensures safe access between your property and the public street. The permit fee is usually modest compared to the main paving cost (often less than $1,000), but skipping it can lead to fines or costly rework if the work isn’t up to local spec. Always check before work begins.
What causes driveway cracks in South Abington? Driveway cracks in South Abington are usually the result of our climate and local soils. The main culprit is frost heave, when water in the glacial till beneath your drive freezes, expands, and lifts the pavement. As temperatures fluctuate rapidly, that cycle causes the surface to flex and, eventually, crack. Poor drainage, thin or uncompacted base material, and even heavy vehicles parked in the same place all make it worse. Over time, small surface cracks widen and let water seep down, speeding up further freeze-thaw damage. If your driveway is 30–40 years old—as many are in the township—it may not have been built with today’s minimums, making it more prone to these issues. Preventing cracks long-term means investing in proper base prep, drainage, and timely maintenance such as crack filling ($200–$800 per round).
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in South Abington isn’t just about putting down more asphalt—the real costs are in excavation, building up the correct base, and tying new pavement into the existing drive. For many homeowners here, a typical widening costs about $3,600–$9,400 if it’s part of a full replacement, or $1,550–$4,700 for a resurfacing overlay of existing pavement. If you just need to add some gravel to the side runs, it can be less—think in the range of $900–$3,500. Remember, any curb cuts or changes to your approach will likely require municipal permitting, and drainage may need updating to prevent water from pooling or running off onto a neighbor. Avoid adding width without a proper stone base or you’ll see edge cracking and settlement quickly.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? In the climate of South Abington, you usually have to wait a minimum of 24–48 hours before driving on a new asphalt driveway. However, this can stretch longer in the spring or fall, when cooler temperatures slow the asphalt’s curing. Those first days are critical—heavy vehicles, sharp turns of the wheels, or parking in the same spot can cause gouges, scarring, or permanent dips before the surface sets hard. You can safely walk on it after a few hours, but it’s best to keep cars off as long as your contractor recommends. For overlays and thicker full-depth jobs in the $3,600–$9,400 range, give the full curing window its due. And don’t sealcoat or wash it for at least a season; the material needs time to finish emitting volatiles and “lock up” before you add any further maintenance layers.
What kind of soil is under South Abington driveways? Driveways in South Abington are primarily built on glacial till—a mixed matrix of stones, sand, silt, and pockets of clay deposited by glaciers ages ago. This type of subgrade isn’t as free-draining as you might expect and definitely holds onto water more than coarse gravel. That creates the specific risk here of frost heave, when trapped water freezes, expands, and pushes up on your driveway (and then settles as it thaws). Good contractors know to excavate deeply (often a foot or more for a full rebuild) and install a thick base of crushed stone (“2A modified”) to create drainage and buffer frost movement. Building on the native soil without that preparation is the fastest route to cracks and settling in South Abington.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating each play a role in driveway maintenance but they aren’t interchangeable. Crack filling targets specific damage: using a hot rubber or asphalt product, it fills cracks (usually up to a quarter inch wide) to keep water from entering and undermining the base. This is especially crucial in South Abington, where freeze-thaw cycles turn small cracks into big problems fast. Sealcoating, on the other hand, is a thin layered material applied to the surface of the whole driveway. It gives a fresh look and some protection from weathering and oils, but it won’t stop water entering existing cracks. For true longevity, always fill cracks before sealcoating—never the other way around. Crack filling is a focused repair ($200–$800), while sealcoating ($200–$750) is a broader maintenance step.
