Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors are often required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before you sign anything, verify HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Also be cautious of driveway sales after storms. “Storm chasers” who solicit door-to-door work without a verifiable PA HIC registration are a significant red flag.

Permits: for many residential projects, driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most PA municipalities. However, the driveway approach—where your driveway meets the public road—may require a driveway access permit through PennDOT or your local municipality. If you’re working near an apron, sidewalk ramps, curbs, or the street right-of-way, check requirements before excavation to avoid work stoppages.

Runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (commonly referenced as Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. That means proper grading matters. A quality contractor will plan drainage so water flows to the appropriate area rather than pooling at low spots where freeze–thaw damage accelerates.

Liability: confirm workers’ compensation coverage by checking wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can leave you exposed to liability if someone is injured on your property.

How to verify step-by-step: (1) check HIC registration online; (2) request a certificate of insurance showing general liability and (when applicable) auto coverage; (3) confirm workers’ compensation coverage via WCIS; and (4) ensure your written contract matches the scope and includes documentation you can keep.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Bridgeport driveway?

In Bridgeport, the Pennsylvania freeze–thaw cycle is the deciding factor in asphalt versus concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they tend to fail differently. Asphalt is flexible and typically handles freeze–thaw cracking better, especially when the driveway is sealed and kept watertight. Concrete, while long-lasting, can spall or crack under heavy salt exposure and repeated winter cycles—particularly if the base is poorly prepared or drainage is weak. In Montgomery County, where older housing stock is common (86.9% of homes built before 1980 in the local profile), it’s common to find driveways at or beyond their expected service life. That reality often pushes homeowners toward full replacement rather than resurfacing, because the base and drainage issues can be the true root cause.

Concrete is often a good choice when you want a long-term, low-maintenance surface and you’re comfortable investing upfront. If your driveway is already performing poorly, going “halfway” with an overlay usually doesn’t fix settlement. A practical example: say you’re comparing asphalt replacement versus concrete installation on a driveway that needs base rebuild. Asphalt might land in the $4,500–$16,000 range, while concrete often sits in the $6,000–$18,000 range. The price difference can be justified if you want to minimize recurring surface maintenance and you’re addressing drainage properly for the long run. But if your asphalt base is only slightly worn and still tight, asphalt resurfacing or sealcoating may stretch the driveway cost-effectively—provided the structural integrity is there.

Bottom line: in Bridgeport, pick based on what the driveway needs underneath. If the base is failing, repair and rebuild decisions matter more than the top layer.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Bridgeport?

Failing base is usually the real reason a driveway keeps “coming back” even after spot repairs. In Bridgeport, signs include cracking that spreads quickly (especially alligator cracking), areas that feel sunken or hollow when walked on, and recurring potholes in the same spots. Ponding is another big clue: if water sits after rain or melts, freeze–thaw cycles can pump moisture into the base and weaken it over time. Edge failure—where asphalt crumbles or lifts near the driveway borders—often indicates the foundation near the perimeter isn’t stable.

Look for separation at joints or around transitions to patios/sidewalks, and watch for areas where patchwork keeps returning within 1–3 seasons. If cracks are wider than about 1/4 inch, or if you see heaving during temperature swings, it’s often beyond “crack filling and patching only.” In many Montgomery County driveways built before 1980, the base may be undersized or contaminated, and an overlay can’t correct that structural problem.

When base failure is present, a full replacement plan (often $4,500–$16,000) with rebuilt base and proper drainage is usually the more realistic path than resurfacing or sealcoating alone.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Bridgeport's climate?

A properly installed asphalt driveway in Bridgeport can last about 20–30 years, but that assumes the base and drainage are built correctly for Pennsylvania winters. The asphalt itself is flexible, which helps it handle freeze–thaw better than some rigid surfaces; however, water infiltration and weak base conditions will still shorten lifespan.

To protect the pavement, sealcoating is usually recommended when the asphalt is still structurally sound—typically every 2–3 years. That preventive maintenance helps keep the surface from oxidizing and helps slow moisture damage. Should I repair or replace my driveway in Bridgeport?

In Bridgeport, the repair-vs-replace decision usually comes down to whether the base is stable. If you have minor cracks (commonly under 1/4 inch) and the asphalt remains tight without settlement or ponding, crack filling and patching can be a reasonable short-term approach—often around $200–$800 per application depending on area and condition. For surface-only wear, asphalt resurfacing may extend service life, typically in the $2,400–$9,000 range.

Replacement is usually the better choice when you see widespread alligator cracking, heaving, repeated potholes, or areas that settle differently than the rest of the driveway. That’s because freeze–thaw cycles in Montgomery County can keep pumping moisture into weak layers, and simply patching the surface doesn’t fix the underlying failure mechanism. With older driveways (many built before 1980), it’s common to find undersized or compromised bases that require excavation and rebuilding.

If your contractor recommends full replacement, ask specifically what they plan to excavate, how much base they’ll rebuild, and what asphalt thickness they’ll install in lifts. A clear plan helps you decide whether replacement is truly necessary versus an avoidable upsell.
