Why driveways behave differently in Lansdowne

Under Lansdowne’s streets and driveways, you find a mix of glacial till and compacted urban fill. Glacial till is notorious for poor drainage and uneven settlement, holding pockets of water that freeze, expand, and heave even well-built surfaces. This underlying reality is crucial in southeastern Pennsylvania, where the frost depth runs to 30 inches and freeze-thaw cycles are routine through winter and early spring. The result is that any driveway, especially an older one, is always at risk for movement and cracking unless the base was designed deep and solid enough to handle these changes.

Most Lansdowne driveways were originally installed between 1920 and 1960, a time when paving standards varied considerably—much of what was laid back then was little more than a thin layer of stone with minimal compaction or drainage consideration. The combination of old construction and the region’s soil means today’s common failures—settling, edge crumbling, and widespread surface cracks—often trace back to inadequate base depth or composition. If you’re resurfacing or replacing, it’s not just about the top layer; the base must be thick enough to resist vertical movement in glacial till, and new work should always include granular sub-base and sound drainage to last through Lansdowne’s winters.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In Lansdowne and much of Delaware County, repaving an existing residential driveway without changing its size or footprint typically does not require a building permit, freeing you from an extra bureaucratic step if you’re just resurfacing or repairing. However, if you plan to enlarge your driveway or install a new one (increasing impervious surface), be prepared for permit requirements—especially to address stormwater runoff.

It’s also important to know that the part of your driveway where it meets the road—the approach—falls under a separate set of rules. For homes on local streets, your township handles these permits; if the approach touches a state route, you’ll need to get a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 6. This split surprises many homeowners and can add steps to a seemingly simple job.

Changing your driveway’s dimensions, particularly if it directs more runoff into the street or onto someone else’s property, can trigger stormwater management requirements. Even without permit triggers, Pennsylvania law holds you responsible for water leaving your lot—runoff directed onto a neighbor can come back as a claim. When hiring a contractor, verify their Home Improvement Contractor registration at the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s HIC portal (hic.attorneygeneral.gov), and check their workers’ compensation coverage with the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Both are required by law for your protection.

How to tell if the base is gone

The hidden condition under the surface—your driveway’s base—determines whether a fix will last. You can do a lot of diagnosis yourself before calling anyone. Large “alligator” cracks that form a network pattern mean the base has failed beneath the surface; laying new asphalt on top will only disguise the issue briefly. A few linear cracks, especially if they’re narrow, point to surface aging that’s easily fixed. Depressions or “birdbaths” are where the ground underneath has settled or moved. Crumbling at the edges signals lack of support and usually shows the base was never built out wide enough. Potholes are a sure sign water has reached and weakened the base.

If a contractor gives you a resurfacing quote without physically probing or coring the base, find another—blind overlays over bad base are money burned, not saved.

Grading and water management on a Lansdowne driveway

Water is the number-one enemy of any driveway in Lansdowne. Every long-lasting driveway—especially on glacial till or urban fill—starts with grading that moves water off the surface and away from the base. For most jobs in this area, that means setting a minimum slope of at least 2% (about a quarter inch per foot) so water runs away instead of pooling. Narrow driveways, common from the mid-century building boom here, often lack proper cross-slope or “crown,” letting water collect along one side or right in the center.

If your drive slopes back toward the house or garage, water will always win unless you address it. Sometimes, regrading is not practical, and this is where trench or channel drains get installed to capture water and send it to daylight or a dry well. The heavy glacial till and compacted fill in Lansdowne slow water from soaking away, so holding water against the drive after rain is a recipe for base damage—especially during freeze-thaw cycles. As water seeps down, freezes, and expands, even small surface flaws become expensive repair items.

Redirecting runoff onto a neighbor’s property is not just a bad neighbor move—it can bring an actual claim, regardless of what your permit says. Good contractors will always keep water on your property and moving toward a lawful outlet.

Why you can't pave in February

Lansdowne’s paving season isn’t just a matter of scheduling convenience—there’s a hard stop dictated by southeastern Pennsylvania’s winter weather and material availability. Hot-mix asphalt needs to be placed and compacted while still hot, typically above 50°F, or it cools too quickly and won’t bond or compact as intended. The local asphalt plants in Eddystone and South Philadelphia shut down from around Thanksgiving through early March, so material simply isn’t available. Concrete fares a little better, but it cannot cure properly unless overnight temperatures are above freezing for days on end.

Our winter design temperature drops to 14°F, and freeze-thaw cycles are regular. Even the best crews can’t work against physics here—attempting to pave in winter means a weak, porous surface likely to break up within a season or two. If a crew shows up offering to pave your drive in late November or February, that’s not a favor; it’s a red flag. Early spring and late fall are good times to schedule work because crews have more availability, while summer books up fast and may come with premium prices.

Remember, with the short material hauls in our area, transport isn’t the holdup—temperature is.

Maintenance that actually works

For Lansdowne driveways, the simplest maintenance—crack filling done early—delivers the most value. Stopping water from getting into the base prevents almost all major problems that ruin driveways in our freeze-thaw climate. It’s inexpensive and can usually be done by a pro for under $700. Keeping edges supported with good backfill or simple curb blocks stops crumbling, especially where driveways are narrow and meet lawns or walks without any structure.

Watch where water runs. Clean debris from drains and keep the surface sloped so there’s nowhere for water to sit. Don’t park heavy vehicles or set jack stands or dumpsters on the same spot for weeks—point loads will crush asphalt, especially in summer heat or during spring thaws. New asphalt needs about 2–4 weeks of curing before any vehicles drive on it, and longer before tight turns or trailers.

When it comes to de-icing, avoid rock salt on concrete drives—salt eats concrete and shortens its life. On asphalt, salt is safer but frequent melt-refreeze cycles can still damage the surface. Sand works as a traction aid when it’s icy and is much gentler on any material, but it requires cleanup in spring.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Lansdowne

What are the signs of a failing driveway base? The classic warning signs of base failure on a Lansdowne driveway are widespread “alligator” cracking (a web of interconnected cracks), depressions that hold water after rain, edge crumbling, and potholes that keep coming back. These issues are common here because many driveways were originally installed in the 1920–1960 build-out on top of glacial till or urban fill, not a properly compacted gravel base. Alligator cracking over large areas almost always means the support layer underneath the asphalt is broken down. Depressions or “birdbaths” mark where the ground below has shifted or wasn’t prepped right. Edge crumble shows the base doesn’t extend far enough, while potholes indicate water's been sitting in or under the base long enough to undermine it. Small, narrow cracks alone aren’t a sign of deep trouble—they can often be fixed with crack filling for about $200–$700.
Why are paving quotes in Lansdowne so different from each other? Pricing for driveway work in Lansdowne can swing widely because you’re rarely comparing apples to apples. The biggest part of the job isn’t what you see—it’s the work that happens underneath: removing old material, installing a thick, compacted aggregate base, and handling drainage. Delaware County is saturated with contractors, so competition keeps prices from spiking, but some crews will write you a cheap bid by skipping or skimping on base prep, using a thinner pour, or skipping proper compaction. Since nearly all of Lansdowne’s driveways rest on glacial till or urban fill, the only right way is to rebuild with sufficient base depth, not just pave over what’s there. Lowest price can quickly become the most expensive way if the job fails in a few years. A full asphalt replacement runs $3,400–$8,700 because it includes ground-up rebuild—not just surface work.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating do entirely different jobs for your Lansdowne driveway. Crack filling targets individual openings in the pavement, stopping water from leaking into the base through openings under a quarter inch wide. It’s a focused, cost-effective repair—do this first and often. Sealcoating, by contrast, is a top-coat applied over the entire surface to slow oxidation from sun and weather, giving the pavement a fresh look and a mild barrier to water. Sealcoating alone will not stop water entering existing cracks, so its value comes only after cracks are filled well. For most local homeowners, crack filling every couple years is both more important and cheaper, with combined costs coming out to about $200–$700 per service.
What does compaction in lifts mean? Compaction “in lifts” means that new asphalt or gravel is installed in layers, with each layer compacted thoroughly before adding the next—all vital for Lansdowne’s climate and soil. On glacial till or urban fill, common subgrades in Delaware County, thick single pours or deep dumps will not compact uniformly; that leads to soft spots that settle and crack after just a few freeze-thaw cycles. A typical driveway sees two or three layers (“lifts”) of asphalt, and proper machinery is needed to compact each before moving to the next. This process ensures every part of your surface and base is as solid as possible, preventing premature failure. While the word “lift” may sound technical, what matters most is that it translates to a driveway lifespan of 20–30 years when done right, as with a full replacement in the $3,400–$8,700 range.
What does sealcoating cost in Lansdowne? For a standard Lansdowne driveway, sealcoating generally runs between $200–$700, with most jobs coming out close to $400. The cost depends on the square footage and how much prep (cleaning, weed removal, minor crack filling) is needed. The high end covers larger double-wide drives or deeper crack treatment ahead of application. Compared to replacement, sealcoating is inexpensive preventive maintenance, especially valuable in southeastern Pennsylvania’s humid summers and freeze-thaw winters. It won’t reverse structural damage but will slow down weathering and give the asphalt a fresh, uniform appearance for another 2–4 years. Since Lansdowne’s climate is on the humid side and the housing stock is mostly from before 1980, a regular sealcoat can stretch driveway lifespan and help detect small issues before they require bigger repairs.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? In Lansdowne’s typical summer and shoulder season conditions, you should wait at least 2–4 days before driving a car onto newly placed asphalt, and a full two weeks before parking something heavier like a van or turning wheels sharply in place. Asphalt needs this time to cool, set, and gain enough strength to resist rutting or scarring. Since southeastern Pennsylvania draws humid air and sees temperature swings, the cure time can vary, so it pays to ask your specific contractor. Summer heat and high humidity (common here) both slow down curing. With a typical driveway install in the $3,400–$8,700 range, giving it the full cure window is cheap insurance against early wear and ruts that never come out. For concrete, plan on a longer wait—typically 7 days minimum, longer in cool or damp weather.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? In Pennsylvania, all contractors taking on home improvement work over $500 are required by law to have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, which you can verify at the Attorney General’s online portal (hic.attorneygeneral.gov). Lansdowne homeowners should also check that a contractor carries valid workers’ compensation coverage, which is mandatory for all employers—this can be confirmed online through WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Before you sign, get a written contract as required by the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. Never work with anyone who says you can “waive” your insurance deductible or asks for full payment up front. This paperwork protects you, not just the contractor, especially with bigger repairs that run from $3,400–$8,700 for asphalt replacement. Checking credentials takes five minutes and prevents major headaches later on.
Can I pave over my existing driveway? In Lansdowne, overlaying new asphalt over an existing driveway is possible if the base is still stable and free of major cracks or sunken areas. Surface cracks, slight raveling, or faded but solid asphalt can often be resurfaced for less—usually $1,450–$4,400—than a full tear-out and rebuild. But if you have widespread “alligator” cracking, ponding, or edge failures, putting new asphalt on top is wasting money; the overlay will mirror those flaws within a year or two. Most driveways here date from before 1980, and the underlying glacial till or fill means the base may be suspect. A reputable contractor will check the base for movement, probe for soft spots, and reject overlays where the base is failing. Quick and cheap fixes don’t last in this soil and climate.
What kind of soil is under Lansdowne driveways? Lansdowne sits mostly on glacial till and compacted urban fill—both types of soil pose stability challenges for driveways. Glacial till is a mixed bag of stones and fine particles deposited by ancient glaciers, notorious for slow drainage and irregular settlement. Urban fill can include brick, ash, and construction debris, depending on whether your street was built up or cut in place. Neither is great at draining water, and both will hold moisture against the base, especially after a heavy rain or snowmelt. This is why local driveways need a thicker, well-compacted base compared to some other areas—otherwise, freeze-thaw cycles common to southeastern Pennsylvania will heave and crack anything that isn’t built strong. Whatever repair you consider, respecting the actual soil under your driveway is as important as choosing between asphalt ($3,400–$8,700) and concrete ($4,800–$11,500).
