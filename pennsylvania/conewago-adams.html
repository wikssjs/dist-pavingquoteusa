Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors are required to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office if the work exceeds $500. Before you sign anything in Conewago, verify the contractor’s HIC status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. After storms, homeowners sometimes get contacted by “storm chasers.” If the contractor can’t provide a verifiable PA HIC registration (and usually a clear business address), that’s a major red flag.

Permits: residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most Pennsylvania municipalities, but driveway approach work—where your driveway meets the public road—often requires a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. The approval process can vary, especially for driveways near roadways under PennDOT jurisdiction. Check before excavation so you don’t end up reworking an apron or violating street apron specifications.

Stormwater/runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater requirements (Chapter 102) require that paving projects not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. A reputable contractor will address grading and may include drainage solutions so water flows properly.

Workers’ compensation: confirm coverage through wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can put you at risk of liability.

Step-by-step for Conewago homeowners: (1) confirm HIC online, (2) request a certificate of insurance (general liability) and a WC verification number, (3) compare the job address to the contractor’s legal business name, (4) get your permit/approach requirements in writing (if applicable), and (5) keep copies of all paperwork in case workmanship disputes arise.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Conewago driveway?

The freeze-thaw cycle is the defining factor for Conewago homeowners choosing between asphalt and concrete. Both materials expand and contract with temperature swings, but they tend to fail differently under Pennsylvania winter stress. Asphalt is flexible, so it typically handles freeze-thaw cracking better in driveways where the base is properly built and water is managed. That said, asphalt relies on routine top-layer protection—sealcoating every 2–3 years helps slow oxidation and keeps the surface from ravaging faster with road salt exposure.

Concrete is rigid, which can be a great long-term solution, but it can still crack or spall when it’s exposed to repeated freeze-thaw cycling and de-icing salt. In Adams County, winters often bring multiple freeze-thaw events per season, and once moisture gets behind a thin or improperly prepared section, concrete can deteriorate. That matters in Conewago because many driveways start from older housing stock—about 35.4% of homes were built before 1980—so base conditions may already be unstable and require full tear-out and rebuild to get the performance you expect.

Here’s where the dollars usually make sense: if your asphalt is structurally failing, resurfacing may be a short detour. A full replacement in the $4,500–$14,000 range can be justified because it corrects the base, not just the surface. If your current asphalt is still structurally sound, overlay/resurfacing in the $2,500–$9,000 band can be the smarter move—especially when you plan for sealcoating on schedule. Concrete often wins when you want low ongoing maintenance and your site prep supports a long service life; in exchange, you’re paying the higher upfront cost and accepting that proper jointing and drainage are non-negotiable.

How to choose a driveway paving contractor in Conewago

Choosing the right contractor matters in Conewago because the quality difference is usually in the prep work: base depth, compaction, drainage correction, and the thickness plan. For pavement-related credentials, ask if the company maintains IICRC-style certifications for construction/repair work—then confirm the certification details through their listed credentials or the relevant registry/credential source they reference.

Get 2–3 itemized written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labor, base stone, asphalt/concrete material, saw-cutting/removal, hauling/disposal, and any drainage/grading work. Avoid single-line lump sums without quantities—on Pennsylvania driveways, those “small line items” often explain why one bid comes in lower. Read the scope carefully: what’s excluded (like apron work at the road, regrading, or utility locating)? Warranty matters: confirm workmanship warranty length and what materials are covered (sealcoating if included, as well as asphalt/concrete thickness and compaction commitments). For payments, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back the remainder until the job is complete and the punch list is addressed. When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in Conewago?

What causes driveway cracks in Conewago?

Driveway cracking in Conewago is usually caused by a combination of moisture, base movement, and Pennsylvania’s repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Water that gets into the asphalt and edges can expand in winter, pushing the surface up and creating transverse and longitudinal cracks. If the base is undersized or not properly compacted, traffic and drainage movement can produce settlement—leading to alligator cracking patterns that “map” across weak areas. De-icing salts can also contribute to surface breakdown over time, especially when they’re repeatedly applied and wash into cracks. Home conditions can make this worse: driveways on older properties (about 35.4% of homes built before 1980) often have base layers that have already been stressed by years of moisture cycling. (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024) Another common cause is improper slope or drainage—when water ponds on the driveway, the freeze-thaw damage accelerates and cracks return faster. If cracks are under about 1/4 inch and the driveway is recently paved or otherwise stable, crack filling and patching can be a temporary maintenance tool (often around $200–$800 per application). But if cracking is deep, widespread, or accompanied by settlement, the root issue is usually base/drainage and calls for resurfacing or full replacement.

Does homeowner's insurance cover driveway damage in Conewago?

Insurance coverage depends on the cause of the damage, the policy language, and whether the problem is considered sudden and accidental versus long-term wear. Most homeowner policies do not cover routine deterioration from normal freeze-thaw and aging—cracking, surface oxidation, and eventual base failure are typically considered maintenance issues. However, damage from an insured event (for example, a sudden vehicle impact, a fallen tree during a storm, or certain sudden water events) may be covered if your policy includes those perils. In Conewago, winter conditions and stormwater issues can play a role, but insurers often look for proof of a sudden event rather than gradual degradation. If you believe your driveway damage is tied to a specific incident, document it right away with photos and dates, and ask the paving contractor to provide job-site documentation that supports the cause (e.g., photos of base condition, evidence of washout, or proof of localized failure). A contractor should also carry proper insurance so claims handling doesn’t put you at risk. When quotes are being prepared, confirm whether the contractor can include the necessary documentation for your claim process. Regardless of coverage, base-related failure usually requires correction, which is why costs can fall in the repair range or move up toward overlay/full replacement depending on what caused the failure.

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

In Pennsylvania residential driveway work, the “right thickness” depends on whether you’re overlaying or building new and on the driveway’s intended use (light passenger cars vs heavier vehicles). The most reliable way to answer for Conewago is to match thickness to base condition and drainage—especially given the freeze-thaw cycle in Adams County. For a full-depth replacement, contractors typically build a compacted stone base and then install finished asphalt in two lifts to reach an adequate total asphalt thickness for the application. As a practical homeowner reference point, full-depth jobs in the $4,500–$14,000 band generally reflect the extra base prep and thicker asphalt needed to keep driveways stable through winter. For overlays/resurfacing, the asphalt thickness is often less because you’re reusing a structurally sound base; that’s why resurfacing estimates commonly land around $2,500–$9,000.
