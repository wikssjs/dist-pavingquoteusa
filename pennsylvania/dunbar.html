Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

Every driveway in Dunbar—especially those on the region’s hilly clay and shale soils—is only as good as what you can’t see. The paved surface is just the top layer: real longevity comes from proper base preparation. Under the asphalt sits a thick, compacted aggregate base, which itself lays over a subgrade that, if poorly drained or left soft, spells early trouble in Fayette County’s freeze-thaw climate.

Clay and shale soils in this area retain water. When that water freezes during our hard winters, it expands. This leads to frost heave: the freezing water lifts the slab unevenly, cracks it from below, and when the thaw comes, those cracks let in even more water. That’s why proper excavation, drainage, and thick, well-compacted aggregate matter here more than anywhere mild.

The #1 thing that separates a durable job from a short-lived one? Hidden shortcuts. A low bid often comes from skipping excavation, laying over failed base, skimping on base stone, or just running the roller once instead of compacting in lifts. Asphalt can look and roll tight for a season or two, but if the base is thin, loose, or not properly built up—especially over Dunbar’s clay and shale—you’ll see cracks, depressions, and edge failures by year three.

Before you sign, require specifics in writing:

  • Excavation: For full replacement, demand at least 8–12 inches below finished grade.
  • Aggregate base: 6–8 inches of 2A or AASHTO #57 limestone, compacted in lifts, not all at once.
  • Compaction: Proof-rolled between lifts; a vibratory roller is best over our clay/shale—not just a skid loader’s tires.
  • Drainage: Grading so water runs off, not toward your house or neighbor’s line.

If these aren’t spelled out, you’re paying for good looks only—not for a driveway that will last through our winters.

Diagnosing a failing Dunbar driveway

When your Dunbar driveway starts to go, figuring out what’s really happening below the surface is key—because the right fix depends entirely on what’s underneath. You don’t need to be a contractor to spot most of the tells.

If you see alligator cracking—those interlaced cracks like reptile skin—over a large area, that’s base failure. No amount of patching or overlay will fix it. The base has given up, usually from water or inadequate thickness, and an overlay will just follow the cracks within a year or two. If the cracks run long and straight, or they’re under a quarter inch wide and not in a cluster, that’s mostly surface wear; these can often be sealed and watched.

Depressions or “birdbaths” that hold water after a rain signal subgrade movement below the base—especially common over clay or shale. Edges that crumble usually mean the base wasn’t extended wide enough to support the asphalt. Potholes mean water has already made it to the base layer, washing out supporting stone.

If someone recommends an overlay without probing the base or pulling a few core samples, they haven’t truly assessed your driveway, and you’d be paying twice—once now, again when the overlay fails.

Widening, extending or adding a parking pad

Widening for a second vehicle, extending to reach a detached garage, adding a turnaround, or paving a gravel run are common upgrades.

All these jobs require proper base preparation under any new footprint—the soil hasn’t been compacted or built up for vehicle loads before. The seam between old and new work will always be the first weak point, so expect some visible difference over the years. If you’re extending your driveway, more area means more impervious surface, and in Dunbar regulations may cap how much runoff you can create. Setback rules may also restrict how close to your property line paving is allowed.

If your project touches or creates a new entrance onto a public road, be aware that in Fayette County, driveway approaches onto state routes are permitted by PennDOT Engineering District 12-0, not your local borough office. Stormwater management—such as grading, adding a swale, or directing runoff—should always be considered. Ignoring drainage will cost more in the long run than taking care of it up front.

Numbers, not adjectives

In Dunbar, price shoppers lose and spec shoppers come out ahead. Ask two contractors for a “three-inch driveway” and you could get wildly different build quality. A quote should spell out numbers, not adjectives—because “three inches” as placed might turn into less than two inches after compaction, and that matters in a region with heavy freeze-thaw and clay or shale soils. Don’t settle for “thick” or “heavy-duty”: ask to see the specs in writing.

Over Fayette County’s soils and with our winter design temps, proper depth beats any surface claim. That means excavating deeper, laying the right kind of stone, compacting in layers, placing binder and surface asphalt separately and compacting them both—and treating edges so they don’t fail come next spring.

  • Excavation depth below finished grade (inches)
  • Aggregate base thickness and type (6–8" of 2A or AASHTO #57 limestone)
  • Base compaction method and proof-rolling between 3–4" lifts
  • Binder course asphalt depth as compacted (2–2.5") and surface course (1.5–2")
  • Mix designations (ask for state route blends when possible)
  • Edge treatment—tapered and supported with stone, or concrete curb if possible
  • Drainage grading and finished slope, away from buildings
  • Joint treatment at seams (asphalt or fabric)
  • Clean-up, topsoil regrade and seeding around edges
  • Final compaction equipment—vibratory roller preferred

A contractor who won’t detail these specs is asking you to trust, not verify.

What you need before paving in Dunbar

Most residential repaving projects in Dunbar—if they stay inside your existing driveway footprint—do not require a building permit. That saves you a phone call, but there’s more to check: if you’re replacing, moving, or adding an entrance where your driveway meets a state route, a driveway approach permit is needed from PennDOT Engineering District 12-0. This permit is separate from any township paperwork and takes precedence on those public rights of way.

Expanding your paved area can trigger stormwater requirements or runoff control. Even if your project seems minor, know that directing runoff onto a neighbor’s land can open you up to legal liability. Grading and drainage need attention regardless of permits. Some properties may have impervious surface limits set by municipal code, so check before adding parking pads or extra pavement.

Pennsylvania law requires any home improvement contract over $500, paving included, to be in writing. Your contractor must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor with the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. Always verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For any employer, even small local outfits, workers’ compensation coverage is mandatory and can be confirmed at wcais.pa.gov. Run both checks—scammers won’t show up on either.

How paving fraud actually operates

Dunbar and all of Fayette County see more than their fair share of transient paving operations. Paving is uniquely vulnerable—once laid, you can’t see if corners were cut on depth, base or compaction, and the damage doesn’t show until the trucks are long gone. Thinly populated counties like Fayette are soft targets: it’s easy for a contractor to work out of an unmarked truck with out-of-area plates, demand cash, and move on before defects emerge.

Here are the classic fraud pitches in our area: the “leftover asphalt” trick (offering you a deal with supposed extra from another job), today-only pricing to pressure a quick decision, refusing to put a quote in writing or detail the scope, and using oil-based spray as a “sealcoat” that vanishes with the first rain. Promises of deep discounts or “crew in the area” should be red flags, especially when the contractor won’t offer a Pennsylvania HIC registration or written contract.

Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract for any driveway job over $500, and entitles you to three business days to cancel with no penalty, no exceptions. No legitimate contractor can require you to waive this right. Waiving or paying your insurance deductible for you is illegal, too.

To protect yourself, take five minutes: check the HIC number at hic.attorneygeneral.gov, verify workers’ comp at wcais.pa.gov, refuse any verbal-only quote, and insist everything is in writing—including base, thickness, and materials. Every scam in this business dies in those five minutes.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Dunbar

What does an asphalt overlay cost in Dunbar? For a typical Dunbar home, an asphalt overlay—that is, resurfacing your existing driveway where the base is still sound—costs between $1,400–$4,200 in 2026. The wide range is due to total square footage and whether any patching or edge repairs are needed first. Overlay is only appropriate if your base is solid; if you have large areas of alligator cracking or lots of depressions, that’s a sign you’re due for full replacement instead. Sometimes, people are tempted by a cheap overlay, but in Fayette County, with our freeze-thaw cycles and clay subgrade, it’s money wasted if there’s movement or water in the base. Always have the base probed before signing up for resurfacing.
What does sealcoating cost in Dunbar? Sealcoating a driveway in Dunbar in 2026 typically runs from $175–$650 per application. Actual cost depends mostly on the driveway’s total square footage, length, and necessary prep (such as cleaning and crack filling). Fayette County’s freeze-thaw cycles mean a fresh, well-bonded sealcoat can help extend the life of your asphalt by keeping water out of microcracks. Just remember, sealcoating is a maintenance product on a sound surface, not a cure-all. If your asphalt is already soft, crumbling, or alligatored, sealcoating won’t help and may even hide worsening problems. Get a trustworthy contractor who uses a proper asphalt emulsion, not oil-based spray, and confirms the square footage before quoting.
What is the difference between binder and surface course? In driveway paving across Dunbar and the rest of southwestern Pennsylvania, you’ll hear contractors talk about “binder” and “surface course.” The binder course is the lower, thicker asphalt layer laid directly over your aggregate base; its main job is structural strength and load-spreading. The surface course is the top, finer material designed to shed water, resist weather, and give a smooth finish. On a quality Dunbar install, you might see 2–2.5 inches of compacted binder under 1.5–2 inches of surface asphalt—these are measured after compaction, not before. Cheap jobs sometimes skip binder altogether, but that means you’re missing the strength your driveway needs to survive freeze-thaw and heavy vehicles. A proper split costs more up front (think $3,300–$8,400 for a full re-do) but pays for itself in longevity.
When can I seal a brand new asphalt driveway? In Dunbar, you should wait at least 90 days—sometimes up to six months—before having your new asphalt driveway sealcoated. This pause allows the asphalt binders to cure, gases to escape, and the surface to fully harden; premature sealing traps those volatiles and can lead to soft, scuffed, or patchy areas. The region’s typical summer installs mean you’re usually safe to seal the following spring, especially given our freeze-thaw climate. Expect to pay $175–$650 for your first professionally applied sealcoat. If anyone offers to seal your brand-new pavement immediately, that’s a red flag—they’re looking to take your money, not protect your investment.
Can I cancel a paving contract I signed at my door? Yes—you absolutely can. Under Pennsylvania law, including in Dunbar and all of Fayette County, a homeowner who signs a contract for paving work at their residence has a legal right to cancel within three business days. This right cannot be waived, no matter what a contractor says. The written contract, which is required for jobs over $500, must include a notice explaining this right. If you change your mind in that time, notify the company in writing (email or letter works) and keep a copy for your records. This law is designed precisely because driveway paving is so often sold door-to-door and under pressure. Never let anyone rush you out of your right to reconsider.
Can I pave over my existing driveway? Sometimes, yes—an asphalt overlay is possible in Dunbar if your existing base is solid, the old asphalt isn’t crumbling, and there are no soft spots or depressions. Minor, isolated cracks or faded areas can usually be resurfaced. But if you have widespread alligator cracking or potholes (common over our clay subgrade after a tough winter), overlaying is wasteful; those failures will reappear quickly. An overlay costs $1,400–$4,200, compared to $3,300–$8,400 for a full tear-out with new base. Don’t trust a contractor who offers an overlay without thoroughly inspecting what’s underneath—sometimes a full replacement is the only smart choice.
What kind of soil is under Dunbar driveways? Most Dunbar driveways are built over a mix of clay and shale, freshly exposed on hillsides above the Monongahela River. This isn’t just academic—clay and shale hold water, which makes our driveways extra vulnerable to frost heave and subgrade movement. That’s one reason Fayette County recommendations call for thicker aggregate bases (6–8 inches compacted) than you’d see in flatter or sandier areas. Extra care with excavation, drainage, and compaction pays off long-term, especially given paving rates here: a full replacement runs $3,300–$8,400 for a typical driveway.
What causes concrete to spall or flake in Dunbar? In Dunbar, concrete driveways sometimes develop surface flaking (spalling) due to freeze-thaw cycles combined with road salts and improper finishing. The region’s clay beneath your driveway holds water, and if the concrete wasn’t air-entrained or finished in the wrong weather, trapped moisture expands and pops off the top layer in winter. Using deicers that contain ammonium nitrates or sulfates speeds up the breakdown. Quality concrete jobs with proper air entrainment and curing, like those costing $4,700–$11,000, fare better but still require careful maintenance over decades of southwestern Pennsylvania winters.
How long does a driveway installation take? A typical asphalt driveway replacement in Dunbar, including excavation, new base, grading, and paving, usually takes two to five days, depending on weather and the size of the job. Concrete driveways, which need time for curing, may stretch to a week or more. Extra time is needed if you’re widening, correcting drainage, or waiting out rain—Fayette County’s hilly terrain and stormy spring weather mean flexible scheduling works best. Don’t rush the process: base compaction and proper curing are the biggest factors in how long your driveway lasts. For the average home, expect a total job cost of $3,300–$8,400 for asphalt, or $4,700–$11,000 for concrete, with bigger projects taking longer but paying off in longevity.
