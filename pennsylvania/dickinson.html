Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving contractors who perform home improvement work typically need to be registered as Home Improvement Contractors (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office when the project exceeds $500. Before signing anything, verify the contractor’s HIC registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious with “storm chaser” behavior—especially door-to-door solicitation after heavy weather—because a contractor without verifiable PA HIC registration should be treated as a major red flag.

Permits: for residential driveway paving, a building permit often isn’t required in many PA municipalities. However, the driveway approach where it ties into the public road may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. Because Dickinson-area projects commonly involve curb/sidewalk tie-ins and street approach areas, it’s smart to ask your contractor to confirm permit needs before excavation.

Runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater regulations (Chapter 102) require that paving does not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should plan grading and may need drainage solutions so water doesn’t pond and accelerate freeze-thaw damage.

Workers’ compensation: confirm coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Then, step-by-step, verify the contractor’s HIC online, review a current certificate of insurance for liability, ask for proof of workers’ compensation, and keep copies with your contract so you’re protected if anything goes wrong.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Dickinson driveway?

For Dickinson homeowners, the freeze-thaw cycle is the deciding factor. Asphalt and concrete both expand and contract with temperature swings, but they fail differently when water gets into the system. Asphalt is more flexible, so it typically handles freeze-thaw movement better—provided the base is stable and properly compacted. The tradeoff is maintenance: asphalt should be sealed on a practical cycle of every 2–3 years, assuming it’s structurally sound.

Concrete is rigid and can last decades, but in Cumberland County winters (including repeated freeze-thaw periods and frequent road salt), concrete can spall or develop cracks that allow water intrusion. Repairable hairline cracking can be manageable, but if your driveway subgrade has settlement or drainage issues, concrete may still show problems—so we still look closely at base prep regardless of the finish material.

With 35.2% of Dickinson homes built before 1980, many driveways are at or beyond the typical lifespan of older asphalt layers, making replacement more common than “just paving over it.” A concrete option is often justified when you want low ongoing maintenance and you’re willing to address drainage and subgrade correctly. A realistic cost comparison: an asphalt full replacement in the $4,500–$12,000 band may be enough for one homeowner’s timeline and budget, while concrete installation in the $7,000–$16,000 band can make sense when you’re planning to stay for the long haul and want a tough, clean-finish driveway.

Bottom line: if your base is failing, the “best” option is often the one that rebuilds the structure first—then you choose asphalt or concrete to match your maintenance preference and winter tolerance.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Dickinson

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Dickinson?

Failing base usually shows up as pavement symptoms that “look structural,” not just cosmetic. In Dickinson and across Cumberland County, the most common signs include alligator cracking that returns quickly, areas where the asphalt is sunken or rippled, and edge failure where the driveway crumbles along the sides—often caused by drainage and base loss. Ponding or standing water after rain is a big indicator, because water gets into weak layers and accelerates freeze-thaw heaving during Pennsylvania winters. You may also see separation at the driveway apron or near garage doors where loads concentrate. If crack filling was done before and the cracks reappear in the same pattern within a year or two, that’s another clue the base is still moving. A proper contractor should be able to explain whether the issue is surface cracking that can be resurfaced or whether excavation is needed. If you’re currently in the range of a resurfacing plan (often $2,500–$7,500) but the base appears unstable, the safest path may be full replacement in the $4,500–$12,000 band with a properly compacted stone base.

How much does driveway paving cost in Dickinson, PA?

Driveway paving costs in Dickinson typically depend on square footage, existing conditions, how much removal is required, and whether your project is a resurfacing/overlay or a full rebuild. In 2026 estimates for this Dickinson tier, asphalt full replacement with new base generally falls in the $4,500–$12,000 band, while resurfacing/overlay (when the base is sound and you’re mainly dealing with surface cracking) is often in the $2,500–$7,500 range. Sealcoating is a preventive option when the pavement is structurally good, typically $300–$1,200 per application for a standard residential driveway. If you’re switching to concrete, new concrete driveway installation commonly runs $7,000–$16,000. Gravel is usually the most budget-friendly approach at $800–$3,000, but it requires more frequent top-dressing. To get an accurate number, request an itemized quote that states excavation depth, stone base thickness, and asphalt lift thickness—those specifics are where Cumberland County projects can swing significantly.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Dickinson's climate?

In Dickinson’s Cumberland County climate, a new asphalt driveway can often last about 20–30 years when it’s built on a properly compacted stone base and paved with adequate asphalt thickness. The key is that freeze-thaw cycles in Pennsylvania stress weak layers and water pathways, so construction quality matters as much as the product. Asphalt also needs preventive maintenance to reach its full potential: sealcoating every 2–3 years (commonly $300–$1,200 per application for a typical residential driveway) helps protect against oxidation and helps slow surface deterioration. If your driveway skips sealing and sits with widespread surface cracks, the lifespan can drop. If you had heaving or ponding before, the right fix is not just surface work—drainage and base rebuild may be required. For homeowners who already have a structurally sound driveway and only need surface renewal, an overlay can extend life roughly 8–15 years (often $2,500–$7,500)—but it’s dependent on the base holding steady.

Should I repair or replace my driveway in Dickinson?

Repair vs. replacement depends on whether the problem is surface-only or structural. In Dickinson, many driveways from older neighborhoods and pre-1980 housing stock show base stress that makes patching a short-term solution. If you have minor cracks under about 1/4 inch and the surface is generally firm and drainage is working, crack filling and patching only may be appropriate for a short window—often $200–$800 per application—with results that can last roughly 1–3 years. If cracks are widespread, edges are breaking up, or the driveway has a pattern of settlement, that points to base and subgrade issues. In those cases, resurfacing/overlay may not hold, and full replacement becomes more cost-effective long-term, especially when you’re in the $4,500–$12,000 range for a rebuild with new base. Concrete can be more expensive upfront (often $7,000–$16,000) but may suit homeowners seeking a long-term solution if drainage and subgrade are addressed. A reputable Dickinson contractor should be able to explain the “why” after inspecting cracking patterns, evidence of ponding, and edge conditions—not just recommend the most immediate option.

When should I sealcoat my asphalt driveway in Dickinson?

Sealcoating is most effective when your asphalt is still structurally sound—meaning the base isn’t failing and the pavement isn’t showing major heaving or deep structural cracking. For Dickinson-area driveways, a typical preventive cycle is every 2–3 years, which lines up with the idea that sealcoat protects against oxidation and surface wear from winter conditions. If you’ve recently had new asphalt installed, wait until it cures fully per the contractor’s guidance before applying sealcoat. When to delay or re-evaluate: if you’re seeing widespread alligator cracking, loose or scaling asphalt, or areas that sink/rock, sealcoating over a failing base will usually hide the symptoms temporarily and you may need resurfacing or full replacement instead. The sealcoating price for a standard residential driveway often lands in the $300–$1,200 range per application, so it’s worth doing at the right time. A good rule is to have a contractor inspect after winter and after freeze-thaw events—if only minor cracks are present and drainage looks correct, sealing can extend service life; if cracks are structural, plan a higher-level repair approach.
