Reading a driveway proposal

When you get a paving estimate in Lewistown, the true measure is not just the total—it’s what is spelled out underneath it. A solid proposal lists the measured square footage—no guessing—plus the depth of excavation and whether old material removal is included. The aggregate base should specify the type (usually Pennsylvania 2A limestone around Lewistown), along with depth, and whether it will be compacted in layers. Every asphalt job should detail the binder and wearing courses, each with depth provided as “compacted inches”—since two inches loose becomes less than two once rolled. Kerbing or edge support adds labor and prevents future crumbling, and the slope or drainage pattern should be clear in writing, safeguarding you against water issues. Restoration of the yard or sidewalk area is not automatic; if you expect it, it needs to be written. Payment terms should involve only a modest deposit upfront with final payment upon completion, and a warranty should state what it covers (usually only defects and labor, not cracks from movement or rutting). If you get a one-line "pave 2,000 sqft — $5,000," you cannot compare that to anything and risk being on the hook for a thin overlay on top of the old, crumbling base. Most homeowners who overpay do so after signing for a non-specific, lump-sum quote—don’t be one of them.

What you need before paving in Lewistown

If you are repaving or resurfacing your existing driveway in the same footprint within Lewistown Borough, you typically will not need a building permit—this is a point that saves many owners confusion, as patching or overlaying doesn’t trigger code. However, the location where your driveway meets the borough street—the approach—almost always requires a local right-of-way permit, especially if any public curb, apron, or sidewalk will be altered. On a state highway, this moves to PennDOT District 2-0 for a Highway Occupancy Permit. In rural sections like Granville Township, if you are simply resurfacing an existing driveway that doesn’t touch a township or state road, a permit is not normally required.

Stormwater deserves respect here. Adding width or paving new areas turns more yard into runoff, and Lewistown’s climate means heavy rain, snowmelt, and freeze cycles push water quickly. Permits aside, you are liable if you send runoff onto a neighbor’s property or overwhelm an existing drainage system, even when no permit is pulled. It’s not worth short-cutting grading or drainage planning.

Contractors working on jobs over $500 must carry Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General—verify your contractor at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing. Employers must carry workers’ compensation insurance, verifiable through the state WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). Ask for proof. This protects you from accidents on your property.

The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is so widely promoted in Lewistown that it can seem like an annual obligation, but the reality is more measured. Sealcoating creates a thin barrier on the top of your asphalt, shielding it from sun oxidation and the oils, salt, and gasoline that speed up surface wear. The deep black finish does make old driveways look sharp again, but a fresh coat is not structural—no matter how thick it’s applied, it cannot rebuild what’s broken beneath. If your base or subgrade is failing, no amount of sealing will fix it.

Done wrong or too often, sealcoating can backfire. Piling coats on every year leads to peeling, flaking, and cracks in the built-up layers. In Lewistown’s region, a well-maintained driveway needs a sealcoat about every 2–4 years, not yearly. On fresh asphalt, sealing too early—before the oils have had a season or two to dissipate—can trap volatiles, softening the surface and shortening the life of the drive.

Crack filling is more important than sealing; those quarter-inch cracks collect water, and here, with deep frost, that water does real damage after every freeze. A good application forces rubberized sealant deep into the cleaned crack, not brushed on the surface for appearance only.

If you’re quoted under $175–$600 for sealcoating, understand what you’re getting: often a thin, driveway-brush or spray coat that won’t last a winter here. For a sound driveway, sealcoating on a measured interval maintains looks and slows oxidation. For a surface with cracks and base failure, you’re better off patching or planning for overlay than sealing over trouble.

Where the water goes

Grading is the most important part of a driveway job in Lewistown. If done wrong, poor drainage leads directly to frost heave, rutting, and early failure. Asphalt and concrete are both hard, so they need to move water—ideally at least a 2% slope, or a quarter inch per foot, so water runs off instead of ponding. Where driveways are wide, a crown in the center or a cross-slope helps, but in the narrow layouts common in 1900–1940 Lewistown homes, space constraints sometimes mean full width cross-slope toward the street is the only option.

When driveways slope back toward the house or garage—as often happens with older homes—the only reliable fix is to re-grade, or, where that’s impossible, to cut in a trench drain or channel drain across the approach. These must outlet the water somewhere safe: “daylighting” it out to a yard or swale, or a dry well if there’s nowhere else to go.

Our freeze-thaw winters compound all this—the shale and limestone subgrade in Mifflin County doesn’t absorb water quickly, so ponding leads to infiltration, freezing, and heaving. It’s also not legal or safe to route your runoff across a property line—even with proper permits, sending driveway water onto a neighbor’s lot turns into a liability and frequent disputes.

Why driveways behave differently in Lewistown

Lewistown sits on a mix of shale and limestone subgrade, which gives driveways here a set of headaches you won’t find in the next county over. Shale fractures easily and can settle or shift, while limestone drains poorly and holds water against the base. In practical terms, this means driveways here need a thicker stone base to bridge “soft” or shifting ground, or you’ll see rutting and heaving, especially after winter thaws.

The frost depth in Mifflin County is a full 36 inches, and long heating seasons mean the freeze-thaw cycle puts a real beating on the pavement. If you’re dealing with one of Lewistown’s many older homes—most built between 1900 and 1940—your driveway probably started as compacted earth or gravel, with minimal or no real aggregate base. That’s why so many older driveways settle unevenly or crack from edge to center. To stay ahead of the region’s winters, modern paving here demands deeper excavation and more base work than just surface patching, and many shared or narrow drives need creative layout to fit today’s wider vehicles. If a paving contractor doesn’t mention these Lewistown-specific concerns, they’re quoting to the wrong conditions.

What to put in a Lewistown paving contract

If you want your Lewistown driveway to last, don’t shop on price alone—shop on what is written into the contract. Every quote should state, line by line, what is being built. Excavation depth should be listed in inches, not just “as needed,” with the kind and thickness of aggregate spelled out—locally, 2A limestone is typical, but how much and how well it’s compacted matters just as much. Asphalt jobs should always list binder and surface course as compacted depths, not just “three inches” (which means nothing; asphalt compresses by 20–25% as it’s rolled in). You want to see compaction in lifts stated, not a single dump and roll. The mix used (“ID-2” or similar) tells you if the right material is chosen for the loads and climate here. Edge treatment—tapered roll, concrete, or supported edge—needs to be on paper.

Lewistown's deep frost depth, and the shale and limestone subgrade, require thicker bases and careful grading to avoid heave and rutting. These specs are not one-size-fits-all: southern standards will not survive five years of winter here. If a quote just throws out a rounded thickness without qualifying as "compacted," or skips base details, your investment is exposed to early failure.

  • Excavation depth (in inches, stated numerically)
  • Aggregate base type (e.g. 2A limestone) and total compacted depth
  • Number of compaction passes and method (roller type, vibratory/plate)
  • Asphalt binder course, compacted thickness (in inches)
  • Asphalt surface (wearing) course, compacted thickness (in inches)
  • Asphalt mix type (designation or job-mix formula)
  • Edge treatment (supported, rolled, or concrete kerb)
  • Drainage plan: slope percentage or “falls to street/yard”
  • Disposal method for excavated material
  • Restoration of lawn, sidewalk, aprons, and disturbed areas

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Lewistown

Why are my driveway edges crumbling? Crumbled edges are a common sight on older driveways in Lewistown, especially where narrow or shared drives were pushed out wider as vehicles became larger. The problem often comes from the underlying shale or limestone subgrade that doesn't give strong lateral support, and from not building up enough stone at the edge or compacting the base. Our 36-inch frost depth makes it worse because the outside edges freeze and thaw faster, forcing cracks and accelerated breakdown. Without a supported edge—especially if the original construction just ended the asphalt at dirt—the pavement chips and unravels faster than the center. If you're seeing this, the minimum fix is patching, but long-term durability comes from replacement and edge support, which, in Lewistown, costs $3,100–$7,900 for a full rebuild with new base.
How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in Lewistown? The right thickness for a Lewistown driveway is higher than you'd need downstate. For our region, with a 36-inch frost line and shale/limestone base, you want at least 4 inches of compacted 2A limestone covered by 2 inches of compacted binder course and 1.5 to 2 inches of compacted surface course. Less than this, especially on top of poor or settling subgrade, leads to rutting and frost heave. Always make sure those numbers are stated as compacted thickness, not loose, as asphalt compresses when rolled. Skimping here to save a little will show immediate results each spring. Local contractors quoting less may not be considering what Mifflin County winters demand or may be pricing for resurfacing only, which comes in at $1,300–$3,900.
How often should I sealcoat my driveway in Lewistown? In Lewistown, you do not need to sealcoat your driveway every year. The climate here—with deep frost, snow, and spring salt—does benefit from a fresh seal every 2–4 years, but sealing too often causes peeling or soft spots, especially if the last layer hasn't fully weathered. New asphalt should be left to cure a full season before any sealing, as premature applications can actually damage the drive. If your driveway is in fair condition, sticking to this schedule will help maintain the surface and slow oxidation, but cracks should always be filled first. A quality single application typically runs $175–$600 depending on size. If you are being pressured for annual sealing, it’s more about sales than longevity in Mifflin County conditions.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in Lewistown? A new concrete driveway installation in Lewistown generally costs between $4,400–$10,500 for a typical residential layout, with most homes landing near $7,000. Local prices are shaped by the extra base preparation needed on Mifflin County’s mixed limestone and shale soils, as well as higher trucking and labor costs due to our rural setting—most concrete and raw materials are sourced from just outside the county. Concrete is more expensive up front than asphalt, but for many owners, especially those with older or shaded properties whose driveways see a lot of water, its long service life of 30–50 years and lower maintenance appeal. If you are replacing an old, crumbling lane, concrete is a good fit if you expect to stay in your home for decades or want minimal upkeep.
How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania? Homeowners in Lewistown and throughout Pennsylvania should always verify their paving contractor before signing a contract. For any job over $500, your contractor must hold Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, issued by the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General. You can check a contractor’s status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. For workers’ compensation insurance—required for any company that employs workers—verify coverage at wcais.pa.gov. These checks protect you from fly-by-night operators and liability if someone gets hurt on your project. Legitimate contractors will never balk at furnishing proof, and any company suggesting a cash discount or waiving your homeowner’s insurance deductible is breaking Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. For a typical driveway, entry-level prices are in the $1,300–$3,900 range for overlays, so be wary of ultra-cheap offers.
Asphalt or concrete for a Lewistown driveway? Both asphalt and concrete perform in Lewistown if installed correctly, but the decision should be shaped by local realities. Concrete costs more upfront ($4,400–$10,500), takes longer to cure, and resists rutting and frost heave better if the base is solid—making it the go-to for those wanting the lowest long-term maintenance. Asphalt is still the most common choice, as it handles the expansion, contraction, and deep frost of Mifflin County winters well if properly built over a deep base, and repairs are less expensive. Sealcoating and crack filling may be needed on asphalt more frequently. For most of Lewistown’s older, narrow lots, asphalt’s flexibility is handy, but concrete can be worth the investment in shared driveways or high-traffic approaches.
How much does driveway paving cost in Lewistown? For a standard single-family home in Lewistown, expect driveway paving costs for full asphalt replacement (new base) to fall between $3,100–$7,900, typically around $4,800. A simpler asphalt overlay (on a good base) ranges from $1,300–$3,900, while concrete runs higher at $4,400–$10,500. These prices reflect the special challenges of Mifflin County’s shale and limestone soils, which require more work than other regions to prevent rutting and frost damage. Square footage, site access, and whether you need new base preparation will be the biggest price drivers—don’t be fooled by surface-only quotes that seem low but skip essential subgrade work.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? After paving a new asphalt driveway in Lewistown, you should wait at least 2–3 days before driving light vehicles on it, and ideally one full week in hot weather. Larger trucks, trailers, or equipment should stay off for longer. Lewistown’s summer temps mean fresh blacktop stays soft for days, and heavy traffic too early can rut or scuff the surface, especially before the first full cooling cycle. Even after the initial wait, avoid parking in the same spot or using jacks or kickstands for a month if possible. Proper curing is one part patience, one part weather: in cool, cloudy weeks, wait longer. If you paid in the $3,100–$7,900 band for a new job, protecting your investment at the start goes farther than any later repair.
Does Lewistown have local asphalt plants? Lewistown and the rest of Mifflin County rely on local limestone quarries for driveway base stone, but the nearest asphalt plants are outside the county. This means most jobs incur travel or delivery surcharges, especially for smaller projects in rural settings—the plant haul can add notable cost in peak paving season. While this does not affect crack filling or small patching jobs ($200–$800), it’s a built-in factor for full overlays and replacements. For best prices, schedule work together with neighbors, or ask contractors about batching with jobs they’re already doing nearby.
