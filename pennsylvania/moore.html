Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, paving is regulated both from a contractor-credential standpoint and from a job-site impact standpoint. If the job exceeds $500, your paving contractor must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the Pennsylvania Attorney General’s Office. You can verify HIC status at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be cautious if a contractor is soliciting driveway work door-to-door after storms—“storm chasers”—because a valid HIC registration should be verifiable.

Permits: in many PA municipalities, residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit. However, the driveway approach where your driveway meets the public road (or any change to curb cuts, sidewalks, or work near rights-of-way) may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. Because Moore-area projects often involve grading near the road or drainage tie-ins, it’s smart to confirm permit needs before excavation begins.

Water runoff: Pennsylvania stormwater rules (commonly referenced under Chapter 102) require that paving projects do not increase runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should address grading and may include drainage solutions if the current slope traps water.

Workers’ compensation: confirm workers’ comp coverage at wcais.pa.gov. Uninsured crews can expose homeowners to liability if someone gets hurt.

Step-by-step homeowner checks: (1) confirm HIC registration online, (2) request a current certificate of insurance (general liability and auto if needed), (3) ask for proof of workers’ comp coverage, and (4) verify whether a driveway access/approach permit is required for Moore based on your exact location and any curb/sidewalk modifications.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Moore driveway?

In Moore, Pennsylvania, the freeze–thaw cycle is the defining factor in asphalt versus concrete decisions. Both materials expand and contract, but they fail differently. Asphalt is flexible, which helps it resist freeze–thaw cracking in many Pennsylvania driveways; however, it needs ongoing surface protection. Regular sealcoating—typically every 2–3 years—helps slow oxidation and keeps the pavement from getting porous enough for water to infiltrate.

Concrete is rigid. That rigidity can be a strength when it’s built correctly and properly reinforced, but it’s also more prone to spalling, surface deterioration, or cracking under repeated freeze–thaw and heavy salt exposure—especially if there’s poor drainage under the slab. Northampton County winters can bring multiple freeze–thaw cycles per season, and many Moore driveways were built decades ago; with 53.0% of homes built before 1980, it’s common to see drives already beyond their expected service life, which is one reason full replacement comes up frequently in homeowner conversations.

When is the price difference justified? Take a typical scenario: a homeowner might consider resurfacing in the $2,500–$7,000 band if the base is sound and cracking is only at the surface. But if the base is soft or the driveway has widespread structural cracking, paying for resurfacing on top of failure is usually wasted money—making the $4,500–$12,000 full asphalt replacement (or a concrete rebuild) the smarter long-term choice. A homeowner who wants “set it and maintain it minimally” sometimes selects concrete, but only after verifying drainage and base preparation are addressed, not ignored.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Moore?

Signs of base failure are common in older Moore drives, especially where you see repeating cracking patterns and edge breakdown. Look for alligator-style cracking, areas that flex underfoot, or bumps and depressions that return after patching. If you notice standing water after rain, that’s another big indicator—ponding lets water infiltrate and then freeze–thaw expands the damage from underneath. Alligator cracking combined with pumping or “soft” spots after snowmelt is a strong signal that the base needs excavation and recompaction, not just resurfacing.

Other red flags include raised sections, heaved edges, and cracks that widen or reappear quickly. If the driveway has widespread cracking rather than a few isolated surface lines, it’s often beyond the benefit range of sealcoating or simple crack filling. In those cases, homeowners typically end up in the full replacement band—often $4,500–$12,000 depending on size and base depth—because fixing the base is the only way to stop recurrence.

How long does a new asphalt driveway last in Moore's climate?

A new asphalt driveway in Moore generally lasts about 20–30 years when it’s built with proper base preparation, correct asphalt thickness, and good drainage grading. Sealcoating is usually recommended every 2–3 years to help protect against oxidation and keep moisture out of the pavement system.
