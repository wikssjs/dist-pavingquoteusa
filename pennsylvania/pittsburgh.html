Diagnosing a failing Pittsburgh driveway

In Pittsburgh, a driveway starts to fail from the bottom up, due to freeze-thaw cycles working on our clay and shale soils. When considering whether to repair or outright replace, what matters most is what lies beneath the surface, not just how it looks at the top. If you see alligator cracking—a patchwork of tight, interconnected cracks spanning a wide area—it signals that the base has failed. No overlay can save it, and any money spent patching over is wasted. You need a full-depth reconstruction.

On the other hand, if you spot mostly isolated, straight cracks under 1/4 inch wide, it’s surface aging rather than structural; here, a good hot crack filler and a sealcoat might keep things together for years. Depressions, birdbaths, or areas that hold water point to subgrade settlement—a section that needs to be dug out and rebuilt with better compaction. Crumbling edges mean the driveway was put in without edge support, common in neighborhoods with modest postwar driveways. And if potholes form, water has already made its way to the base, demanding more than just a patch.

Be wary of a contractor who quotes an overlay without probing or coring your base. They haven’t checked the underlying trouble, and you will be paying twice. Always ask them what they found under your driveway before accepting a bid.

Beyond replacement: layout changes

Many Pittsburgh-area homeowners are looking to widen a driveway for another car, add a turnaround for safety on our hilly streets, or build out a pad for extra parking. Others want to swap out an old gravel run with a proper asphalt surface, or extend to a detached garage added after the house was built. Here’s what changes with these projects: every new square foot means new base over undisturbed subgrade, and that transition—the seam between old and new—remains a permanent weak spot, more so across a clay hillside or where freeze-thaw puts extra stress.

When expanding your driveway, be aware that setback and impervious-surface rules may limit how close you can pave to your property lines or how much area you can cover. In Pittsburgh, a permit from the Department of Mobility and Infrastructure is mandatory if curb, sidewalk, or apron work in the right-of-way is involved. For additions that connect to a state highway, PennDOT District 11-0 requires a Highway Occupancy Permit. Tying new work into old asphalt or concrete should be done with a full-depth saw cut rather than a butt joint, but expect that seam to be the first place where cracks show up in the years ahead.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In Allegheny County, simply repaving your existing driveway on your own property rarely requires a building permit—there’s no local rule stating otherwise for places like Ross Township. That saves you time and paperwork if you aren’t changing layout or touching the public right-of-way.

But almost everywhere in the Pittsburgh area, a separate permit is needed for the approach—where your driveway meets the public street. This is managed by authorities that may not be obvious at first: in the city, it’s the Department of Mobility and Infrastructure; for driveways connecting to state routes, PennDOT District 11-0 handles permitting. If you’re modifying the apron, curb, or sidewalk, expect to file for that permit.

Expanding your driveway’s footprint may also trigger stormwater rules since additional pavement changes runoff patterns. Even if no permit is needed, you cannot lawfully direct stormwater onto your neighbor’s land; liability follows the water, regardless of paperwork.

If you are hiring the job out, verify your contractor’s Home Improvement Contractor registration number through the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Every legitimate contractor must carry workers’ compensation, which you can check through the state’s WCAIS portal at wcais.pa.gov.

Maintenance that actually works

In Pittsburgh, where freeze-thaw can be relentless, what counts is keeping water out of your base. Start with crack filling—any crack a quarter inch wide or more should be cleaned and filled before water works its way below. Seal the edges, since unsupported pavement at the sides fails first. If your driveway borders a steep yard, consider adding a stone shoulder or curb to reinforce the edge and keep turf from collapsing.

Good drainage is key; clear leaves and debris so water never stands. Don’t let dumpsters or jacks stay in one spot; they’ll leave dents. On brand new asphalt, wait several days, especially in cooler months, before parking—turning the wheels while stationary can scar soft blacktop in its first few weeks. Concrete can take several weeks of cure before handling freeze-thaw and heavier use.

De-icing matters: salt is tough on concrete driveways, eating away at the surface—magnesium chloride and calcium magnesium acetate are safer, but cost more per bag. On asphalt, salt isn’t as destructive, but cycles of melting and refreezing drive water deep into the base and speed up damage. It’s worth using sand for traction or treating early when possible, and always shove the slush off before it goes solid again.

What to put in a Pittsburgh paving contract

If you want a driveway that lasts in Pittsburgh, specs beat price. Always get key details in writing; otherwise, you’ll never know what’s under the surface. The biggest mistake is accepting a verbal promise like, “we’ll put down three inches.” You need to know if that’s three inches loose or three inches compacted, because after rolling, that’s often closer to two. Our heavy freeze-thaw cycles and the clay and shale subgrade common in Allegheny County demand a thicker base and tighter compaction than milder regions—cutting corners here is how you end up paving twice.

A proper paving proposal should spell out: how deep the old material will be excavated, what kind and depth of aggregate base will be installed, whether it will be compacted in lifts with proper equipment, and exactly how much asphalt (or concrete) will be installed, measured after compaction. The mix used should be named, edge restraint described, and drainage plan included. If these aren’t written, you're relying only on a handshake.

  • Excavation depth (minimum 8–12 inches in local clay/shale soils)
  • Aggregate base type and thickness (typically 6–8 inches crushed stone, compacted in lifts)
  • Compaction method for each layer (machine roller, not hand tamping)
  • Asphalt binder course thickness (e.g., 2 inches compacted)
  • Asphalt surface/wearing course thickness (e.g., 1.5–2 inches compacted)
  • Specified asphalt mix design (e.g., PennDOT 9.5 mm wearing course)
  • Edge treatment (stone shoulder, curb, or reinforced edge)
  • Drainage management plan (slope, swales, or drain inlets)
  • Joint detail at tie-ins (full-depth saw cut, not butt joint)
  • Start and completion dates

Why driveways behave differently in Pittsburgh

Pittsburgh sits on hillsides of clay and shale—these aren’t just geological trivia but the main reason so many older driveways here have heaved, settled, or cracked wide open. Clay holds water, then swells and shrinks with every freeze and thaw. Shale can break apart or slip under heavy loads, especially where the subgrade wasn't properly compacted decades ago. In postwar subdivisions across Penn Hills and McCandless, most driveways were poured or paved over basic, often minimal stone—little more than enough to get through a builder’s punch list. Over time, water finds its way down and the repeated expansion and contraction opens edge cracks and potholes far faster than in, say, sandy soils one county over.

With a frost depth of 36 inches and a ground snow load of 30 psf in Allegheny County, winter puts constant pressure on driveways—especially those without enough base or true edge support. The right answer for a long-lasting job here is a deeper aggregate base and enough crown to move water off quickly. Skip these, and even a thick asphalt or concrete slab will fail. In Pittsburgh, the rules of thumb from flat, dry regions don’t apply; every inch of base matters more than anything you’ll see at the surface.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Pittsburgh

How long should an asphalt driveway last in Pittsburgh? In Pittsburgh, a well-built asphalt driveway—one with the right base and decent drainage—typically lasts 20 to 30 years. Our local freeze-thaw pattern and clay/shale soils mean driveways here often show edge cracks and depressions sooner if the subgrade was never properly compacted. Full replacement (with a new base) can deliver the top end of that lifespan—more than 25 years is possible in level areas with regular crack filling and prompt sealcoating every few years. Expect to pay $3,300–$8,500 for a true full-depth job. Thin overlays or quick patches might look fresh for a few years, but underlying issues will almost always return, cutting effective life to the lower end.
When can I seal a brand new asphalt driveway? In the Pittsburgh area, you should wait at least three to six months after new asphalt is laid before scheduling your first sealcoat. The fresh blacktop needs this time to finish curing and release built-up oils. Sealing too soon can trap solvents below the surface, leading to a dull finish and even premature cracking in our cold winters. A good rule here is to wait until the next spring or fall after installation—never during the heat of summer or the dead of winter when cure rates slow dramatically. When you do get it sealed, expect to pay around $200–$650 for a standard single application.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? Yes, if you are altering or repaving the apron, curb, or sidewalk at the entrance to your driveway, you likely need a permit—and not always from the city’s building office. In Pittsburgh, approach permits are issued by the Department of Mobility and Infrastructure. If your driveway ties into a state road, you need a Highway Occupancy Permit from PennDOT District 11-0. Even if you are only repaving your private driveway within your property in Ross Township, this rarely requires a right-of-way permit unless you touch the public street, which saves some paperwork and potential delays.
How long before I can drive on a new asphalt driveway? After a new asphalt driveway is installed in southwestern Pennsylvania, it’s best to keep vehicles off the surface for at least 2–3 days in warm weather and longer if laydown happens late in the fall. Asphalt needs time to cool and firm up, especially here where humidity fluctuates and overnight lows can get chilly quickly. You’ll usually be safe walking on it within 24 hours, but let it reach full firmness before parking anything heavy—or turning tires when stationary, which can scar soft new pavement. Waiting a bit longer is better, especially for big SUVs or work trucks.
Are pavers worth the cost in southwestern Pennsylvania? Paver driveways have a certain curb appeal and can last decades if installed on a deep, well-drained base. But the upfront cost is often two to three times that of asphalt, and our heavy freeze-thaw climate is tough on joints—paver movement and weed growth are facts of life here. If your priority is value and longevity, asphalt full replacement at $3,300–$8,500 (base included) is often the more practical option in Pittsburgh. Pavers do best when professionally installed with drainage planned from the start, and require ongoing maintenance to stay level here—in mild climates, they need much less fuss.
How much does driveway paving cost in Pittsburgh? For a typical single-family home in Pittsburgh, a full-depth asphalt driveway replacement with a new compacted base runs $3,300–$8,500 in 2026, with the most common job falling near $5,200. If your base is solid and you’re just resurfacing, an overlay runs $1,400–$4,200. Basic crack fill and patching starts at $200–$800. Material delivery costs remain reasonable here, thanks to short hauls from local plants, but total price depends most on how much prep work is needed under the surface.
Does Pittsburgh have local asphalt plants? Yes. The Pittsburgh metro market is well served by multiple local asphalt plants and aggregate quarries, especially in Allegheny County. This generous supply keeps hauling times short and means most residential driveway work can be scheduled quickly without waiting on long material deliveries. Shorter hauls translate to slightly lower material costs for homeowners, so more of your paving dollar should go to prep work and quality labor, not trucking fees. That’s part of how the regional driveway price band—$3,300–$8,500—remains stable for a full replacement, rather than swinging wildly with transportation spikes.
How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in Pittsburgh? In this climate, residential driveways are typically built with two layers: a binder course (about 2 inches compacted) and a surface course (another 1.5–2 inches compacted), placed over at least 6–8 inches of compacted crushed stone if you’re starting from scratch. Don’t accept any contract that measures thickness “as laid,” because proper compaction reduces that figure by as much as a third. With our clay and shale soils and deep frost, you want true compacted depths—surface thickness alone is not enough to survive Pittsburgh’s freeze-thaw cycles.
How much does a concrete driveway cost in Pittsburgh? For Pittsburgh area homes, concrete driveways cost more up front but can be a good value for those planning to stay put long-term. Expect a new poured concrete driveway to run $4,700–$11,500 depending on slope, size, and required base prep. Most homeowners spend about $7,500. Concrete offers the longest service life if installed with proper base and expansion joints, but needs at least a month to fully cure before winter salt or heavy traffic. If you want low maintenance and are not on a severe slope, concrete is a solid choice for many Allegheny County neighborhoods.
