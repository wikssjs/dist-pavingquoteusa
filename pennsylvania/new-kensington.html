The honest case for and against sealcoating

Sealcoating is widely promoted in New Kensington, but it’s not a cure-all. Its main real benefits are protection of the asphalt’s binder against oxidation from sun and air, limited shielding from oil or gasoline spills, and refreshing a faded or patchy appearance. A proper sealcoat helps slow aging of an intact driveway, but if your base is failing, if you have major cracks or potholes, or if the surface is already alligatored, sealcoating cannot fix the underlying problems.

The drawback nobody selling sealcoating will mention: using it too often, or too soon after new asphalt, does more harm than good. New asphalt needs about six months to cure, or longer in cool weather, before taking a sealcoat. Over-application leads to peeling and flaking as layers break away from each other. For best results in New Kensington, seal every three to four years — not annually — and only once the driveway has shown signs of light oxidation, not while it’s new-black.

Crack filling, especially on cracks under a quarter inch wide, matters more than general sealing. Proper hot-rubber crack fill provides waterproofing where the freeze-thaw cycle exploits weaknesses worst; a decent sealcoat just sits on top. Quality of application counts: a brush-applied or thick squeegee coat lasts, while a cheap spray-on job at the low end of $175–$650 looks good for one season, if that.

Bottom line: Sealcoating is good maintenance if your surface is in decent shape, you don’t overdo it, and you get the right prep — but it won’t revive sunken, cracked or broken pavement and shouldn’t be seen as a structural repair.

Widening, extending or adding a parking pad

Many New Kensington homeowners outgrow their original driveway. Widening for a second car, extending to a backyard garage, or building an extra parking pad are common upgrades, especially with more adult drivers at home. These are bigger projects than just repaving, because any new footprint — anywhere you pave over lawn, gravel, or undisturbed soil — demands full-depth excavation, compacted stone base, and careful grading. Unlike a simple overlay, you’re dealing with “fresh” subgrade, which in our area means breaking up stubborn clay or shale and ensuring proper drainage below.

One catch: the joint between your old and new pavement will always be a weak spot. Cracks and slight settling at the seam are almost impossible to avoid, so expect it down the line, especially with our heavy freeze-thaw cycles.

Expanding driveways or adding parking pads can also bump into zoning or stormwater rules. Adding impervious area may trigger runoff requirements or even a permit — in Greensburg, you’ll need a permit if you alter the curb, sidewalk, or drainage. If your new layout affects how water leaves your property, you’re also responsible for not dumping runoff onto a neighbor’s yard. And on state roads, PennDOT District 12 handles approach permits, so always check before cutting a new curb or apron.

Permits and approvals for paving in New Kensington

The good news for New Kensington homeowners: simply repaving your driveway in the same dimensions, without changing the curb, sidewalk, or drainage, generally doesn’t require a building permit. The exception is in Greensburg, where replacing or expanding in a way that affects curb or drainage usually triggers a permit. But where many get tripped up is at the street — the approach, or where your driveway meets a public road. On state routes in Westmoreland County, it’s PennDOT District 12 that issues the necessary driveway approach permit, not your local city or borough.

If your project increases impervious surface, stormwater rules may apply: extra pavement changes how quickly water runs off your property and can impact neighbors. Even if the township doesn’t ask for a formal plan, you’re on the hook for managing runoff so it doesn’t end up across the property line or pushing water toward the street in a problematic way.

Before signing any contract over $500, Pennsylvania requires a written agreement (Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act). Your chosen paving contractor must have a valid Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration — verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov — and workers’ compensation coverage if they have employees, which you can check via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). Skipping these steps isn’t just risky — it could mean you’re liable for injuries or unresolved disputes.

Asphalt or concrete for a New Kensington driveway?

Choosing between asphalt and concrete in New Kensington is about more than just price. Our region’s 36-inch frost depth, hammering freeze-thaw cycles, and heavy de-icing salt use tip the scales in ways most homeowners haven’t considered. Asphalt is flexible: when the ground heaves or settles, asphalt absorbs some movement without cracking. This is crucial on our local clay and shale soils, which expand and contract seasonally.

Concrete, in theory, lasts much longer — often 30 to 50 years — but it’s less forgiving. It tends to spall (flake off at the surface) under the heavy use of salt-based de-icers that are standard for Pennsylvania winters. Spalling and surface scaling, not cracking, are what actually cause most local concrete driveway failures. Owners who avoid salt, or keep a broom finish and seal joints, get decades of service, but this takes discipline.

Asphalt costs less upfront ($3,100–$8,100 for full replacement), requires maintenance (sealcoating every 3–4 years, crack filling as needed), but stands up better to ground movement. Concrete commands a premium ($4,500–$11,000), but beware: one winter with over-application of rock salt can shorten its lifespan dramatically.

Paver driveways are uncommon here but legitimate for decorative or historic homes, offering individual unit replacement if something goes wrong. Gravel is best for long rural runs or tight budgets; it doesn’t mind a freeze-thaw cycle, provided you’re willing to top-dress every couple of years.

Grading and water management on a New Kensington driveway

Proper grading and water control are the difference between a driveway that lasts and one that fails in only a few years. In New Kensington and throughout Westmoreland County, water that sits or flows the wrong way will destroy both asphalt and concrete. Every driveway should slope away from structures at a minimum pitch of 1/4 inch per foot. This directs rain and snowmelt toward the street or a proper collection area, not your garage or basement. For wide driveways, a slight crown at the center sheds water to the edges; on narrow or sloped drives, a cross-slope may be necessary.

If the ground slopes toward your house, or you’re boxed in by terrain, trench or channel drains may be needed to reroute surface water. Where the driveway can’t daylight (discharge) directly downhill, dry wells, or properly routed piping, are a must — especially with our region’s heavy freeze-thaw cycles. Trapped water in the stone base can freeze, expand, and heave the pavement, leading to premature cracking and potholes.

The clay and shale subgrade typical of Greensburg and surrounding areas retain moisture, meaning poor drainage under the driveway can linger for weeks after a storm — accentuating the effects of winter freeze cycles. If you pave over wet or uncorrected clay, expect heaving and base failure within a few years.

Finally, Pennsylvania law is clear: you’re responsible if your drainage alters runoff in a way that damages neighboring properties, permit or not. Setting the correct grade and handling water right isn’t just about your own driveway — it’s about avoiding conflicts and surprise repair bills down the line.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in New Kensington

Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? In New Kensington and across Westmoreland County, you usually need a specific approach permit for any work where your driveway connects to a public road, especially for changes or new installations. If your property fronts on a state road, it’s PennDOT District 12, not the city, that issues this permit. Don’t assume your paving contractor will “take care of it” — always ask for documentation. Unauthorized work can result in fines or being required to tear out new pavement. This is true whether you’re doing a simple repair or a full replacement (running from $3,100–$8,100 for asphalt). Approach permits protect both you and municipal infrastructure, so plan ahead and budget time for approvals.
What does compaction in lifts mean? Compaction in lifts is a crucial method used for long-lasting driveways in New Kensington. It means that asphalt or stone is installed in layers, or “lifts,” typically 2–4 inches thick, and each layer is compacted fully before the next is placed. Our area’s freeze-thaw cycles and clay subsoil make this especially important — thick, un-compacted placement will settle unevenly, leading to dips and cracks. You may not see the work, but it’s what keeps a $4,900 driveway from falling apart in two winters. When a contractor explains their compaction routine for overlays or new installations (ranging $1,350–$8,100), listen carefully; cutting corners here is the number one cause for premature driveway failure in southwestern Pennsylvania.
When is the best time of year to pave in New Kensington? In New Kensington, the paving season usually runs from late April to early November. The best window is when daytime highs are reliably above 50°F, and you can count on the asphalt or concrete to set up properly — too cold, and it won’t bond or cure right. Avoid the first few weeks of warm weather, when subgrades are still saturated from winter thaw, particularly with our common clay soil. Late spring and early fall are often ideal for asphalt overlays ($1,350–$4,000) because conditions are mild and plants aren’t overloaded. Rushed paving outside these months, or during freeze-thaw swings, means a higher risk of failures that usually don’t show up right away.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in New Kensington, whether for an extra car or a parking pad, means building out new base over unpaved soil and matching to the old surface. This is more work than resurfacing — you’re excavating, putting in new stone, and tying in the materials. For asphalt, costs typically run $3,100–$8,100 unless you’re only adding a small strip. A gravel extension is less: $900–$3,500. The final figure depends on how much area you’re adding, whether stormwater management or approach permits are triggered, and the condition of your existing driveway’s edge. Always budget for some seam cracking down the line, no matter who does the work.
What causes concrete to spall or flake in New Kensington? Spalling — or flaking off — of concrete driveways in New Kensington is almost always due to freeze-thaw cycles and the use of de-icing salts over the winter. Our region’s heavy clay soil holds water against the slab, and repeated freezing forces can push the top layer to break and shed. Surface finishing techniques, mixes, and install temperature also play a role, but salt is the main culprit here. Concrete driveways cost $4,500–$11,000 and should last decades, but a single harsh winter with too much salt can start the spalling process that spreads each year. Most owners simply do not avoid salt, so if you need low-maintenance, asphalt may be the smarter option in this county.
What does an asphalt overlay cost in New Kensington? An asphalt overlay, also called resurfacing, is when a new layer of asphalt is placed over a structurally sound base and worn top surface. In New Kensington, overlays usually fall in the $1,350–$4,000 range for a typical single-family home’s driveway. Saving money with an overlay only makes sense if the sub-base is still solid — if not, you may be paving over a problem that will just reappear. Be wary of quick-turn “overlay” offers at lower prices if you have deep cracks or settled spots; these signal base trouble that an overlay will not fix.
Can I pave over my existing driveway? Paving over an existing driveway — with a new asphalt overlay — is a common option in New Kensington if the current base is stable, with only surface cracking or minor wear. If you see deep cracks, heaving, or sunken areas, an overlay won’t last; it’ll just mirror the existing failures within a year or two. Overlay projects range $1,350–$4,000 and can be a smart money-saver if the base was well built and has aged evenly. Have your contractor check for “soft spots” by probing or coring; skipping this step is what leads many overlays to fail prematurely in our region.
Is sealcoating a scam? Sealcoating isn’t a scam in New Kensington — but it’s not a miracle, either. On a healthy, solid driveway, sealcoating ($175–$650) is real preventive maintenance: it keeps sun, air, and minor oil leaks from aging the asphalt and gives the surface a nice uniform look. The “scam” comes from fly-by-night crews who spray a thin, watered-down coat and promise miracles for next to nothing. Done right — with decent prep and not too often — sealcoating helps your pavement last longer. But if a contractor claims it will fix structural cracks, potholes or base failure, that’s just false. Always ask what prep and method are used, and steer clear of cheap “drive-by” deals.
Does road salt damage driveways in New Kensington? Yes, road salt is tough on driveways in New Kensington, especially concrete. Salt speeds up the freeze-thaw cycle and draws more moisture into the slab or asphalt, leading to spalling (surface flaking) on concrete and accelerated aging on asphalt. Asphalt handles salt better, but even it will dry out and gray faster with repeated heavy salting. Owners who lose track of how much de-icer they’re using quickly see their concrete driveways ($4,500–$11,000) break down and pit. If you must use de-icer, choose calcium or magnesium chloride over rock salt, use it sparingly, and consider physical removal first — it’ll stretch the life of any paved surface here.
