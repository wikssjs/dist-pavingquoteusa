Permits and approvals for paving in New Hanover

If you’re repaving an existing New Hanover driveway in its current shape and size, a building permit is usually not required. Most townships in Montgomery County, per local code enforcement patterns (see FACTS), do not treat basic resurfacing or maintenance as permit-triggering projects — this saves homeowners time and permit costs. That changes if you alter the curb, sidewalk, or especially the driveway approach (the first few feet connecting to the public road). In Montgomery Township, for example, the approach is under the Public Works Department’s jurisdiction, and doing work there requires a specific road occupancy or approach permit. Homeowners often miss this, thinking the township or their local code office handles everything — but the approach may be a different process altogether.

Any increase in paved area can trigger stormwater requirements. Creating new impervious surface — such as widening or adding a parking pad — affects local runoff. Discharging water onto your neighbor’s property opens you to liability, regardless of whether you secured a permit. Always discuss stormwater with your contractor and check municipal rules before changing your driveway’s footprint.

For all work over $500, Pennsylvania law requires your contractor to have Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, which you can verify at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Workers’ compensation coverage is also mandatory for anyone with employees; verify current coverage status via WCAIS (wcais.pa.gov). These are your two basic consumer protections — never skip them, even if the contractor was “used by a neighbor.”

Changing the footprint of a New Hanover driveway

Adding a second parking space, putting in a turnaround, extending to a detached garage, or finally swapping that gravel strip for real asphalt — these are projects that change more than just the surface. Any new section means building a base from scratch over undisturbed subgrade, which is slower and costlier than just resurfacing. The seam where new paving meets old always remains a vulnerable point; over time, it may show cracking or differential settling.

Widening or extending your driveway also brings up local impervious-surface and setback rules. There are township restrictions on how close you can pave to a property line, and adding square footage could push the total impervious surface on your site past thresholds that trigger runoff management requirements. The driveway approach, where your drive meets the township road, is separately permitted — in Montgomery Township and other parts of the county, that’s a distinct permit handled by the local Public Works Department, not the building or zoning office.

If your contractor skips excavation or installs a thin base under new sections, it’s a shortcut that often fails in New Hanover’s freeze-thaw climate and soft subgrade. Get detailed plans showing base depth and drainage provisions before work begins.

Sealcoating: what it does and what it doesn't

Sealcoating makes an asphalt driveway look fresh and protects the surface from oxidation, sunlight, and minor spills like fuel or oil. Applied properly, it forms a thin barrier that slows the binder’s aging and helps water roll off, buying a few extra seasons of good appearance. For a sound driveway, it’s routine maintenance — but it’s not a miracle product.

Sealcoating does not add structural strength, does not fill potholes or large cracks, and, most importantly, will not save a driveway whose base or foundation has failed. Once the subgrade moves or the aggregate base loses strength through freeze-thaw or water infiltration, no amount of sealer can slow structural damage. In Montgomery County, many original driveways from the mid-century housing boom are at or past the point where sealing alone is effective.

The catch: sealing too often (every year, or even every other year) is actually worse over the long run. Each new layer builds up until it starts to peel, crack, or flake. Sealers need sound, cured asphalt to adhere. If you seal too soon after an asphalt install — before the new surface has fully cured — you’re just trapping out-gassing oils, reducing life. Once every three to five years is the reasonable interval for most New Hanover homes. The first seal should only go down once the surface is darkened and chalky, usually 12–18 months after paving.

Crack filling — routing and backfilling isolated cracks with proper material — does more to slow water penetration and prevent base failure than sealcoating. A good sealcoat means a thick, hand-squeegeed layer, not a fast spray-and-run. If you’re seeing quotes under $225–$800 for what claims to be a full seal, expect a watery blackwash that won’t last through a single winter.

Why New Hanover driveways fail from the bottom up

Every driveway in New Hanover, whether asphalt or concrete, depends for its life on what’s underneath — not just the pavement you see, but the assembly of subgrade, compacted aggregate base, and paving above. Here, eastern Montgomery County’s silty clay subgrade is prone to holding water. Combine that with southeastern Pennsylvania’s moderate freeze-thaw cycles, and you have a recipe for ground movement.

A proper driveway install goes like this: First, the existing soil (subgrade) is excavated, graded, and compacted. On top of this goes several inches of compacted crushed stone — the aggregate base. This layer distributes vehicle loads and helps drain water away. Only after this is compacted in lifts (thin layers, each compressed before the next), does the asphalt or concrete go down. A thick pavement over a thin, poorly compacted base is a trap: it may look indestructible for the first year, but as soon as freeze-thaw hits, water in the subgrade expands, lifting parts of the pavement (frost heave). As it thaws, the pavement settles unevenly. Over time, this causes cracking, rutting, and (in concrete) slab heave and edge breaks. That’s why good base depth and drainage are non-negotiable in this climate.

Where cheap quotes come from: skimping on excavation (sometimes paving over grass and soft soils), using thinner or ungraded stone, skipping compaction or doing it all at once instead of in lifts, or paving over a failed base without proper repair. None of this is visible when the crew leaves. Insist, in writing, on at least 6–8 inches of 2A modified crushed stone base for asphalt in this soil, compacted in 3-inch lifts, with confirmation of base thickness before paving starts. If that language is not in the contract, you’re rolling the dice on a short-lived driveway.

Which surface survives New Hanover winters

Here in New Hanover, winter is the test: freeze-thaw is routine, and humid summers don’t help drainage. Asphalt and concrete each have strengths, but the real story is in how they handle ground movement and winter salt. Asphalt’s flexibility lets it flex with each freeze-thaw cycle — in practice, it absorbs the slight heave of wet, freezing native soils common throughout Montgomery County. Cracking tends to be surface-level, repairable, and rarely moves down to the base unless water is allowed in year after year.

Concrete is more rigid and in principle longer-lasting, but it does not forgive errors in base prep or drainage. In counties like ours where winter road salt is a fact of life, concrete’s most common failure is scaling and spalling — surface chipping where de-icing salts break down the matrix. It’s not cracking that ends most concrete driveways here, but this steady surface loss, especially near the road apron where plow trucks drop more salt. Few owners are truly willing to keep all salt off their driveways, so this is a real concern.

Concrete costs more ($5,700–$14,000), lasts longer if you avoid salt, but isn’t maintenance-free. Pavers handle freeze-thaw well if installed over a deep base, but cost more; gravel works for long rural drives and best on well-drained lots.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in New Hanover

Why are paving quotes in New Hanover so different from each other? Contractors in New Hanover and the wider Montgomery County area often provide quotes that differ by thousands of dollars for what sounds like the same job. The root of this isn’t usually the visible work; it’s what happens to the base and subgrade. Most of the cost in a proper driveway replacement — whether asphalt or concrete — is beneath the surface, in excavation, base material, and compaction. Some crews skip steps or cut corners, paving too thin over a weak base. It can look fine the first year, but our area’s freeze-thaw cycles and clay soils make these shortcuts show quickly. The supply chain for asphalt and stone here is consistent, with aggregate brought locally along the I‑276 and US 202 corridors, so legitimate material costs are steady. When you see a substantially lower price than other bids (for example, a full asphalt replacement quoted at $4,000–$10,500), ask in writing for details on excavation depth, stone base thickness, and compaction method. Cheap quotes almost always hide invisible, expensive risks for the homeowner.
Do I need an approach permit where my driveway meets the road? In much of Montgomery County, including townships around New Hanover, the work you do on the main stretch of your driveway may not need a permit if you’re resurfacing or making minor repairs. However, once you touch the approach — that’s the section where the driveway meets the township or state road — you almost always need a specific permit. In Montgomery Township, for example, the Public Works Department, not the regular code office, issues road occupancy or approach permits. If your project includes widening, replacing, or even regrading the apron, you’ll need to get their sign-off first. The process isn’t just bureaucratic: the township wants to keep stormwater and gravel off public roads, and make sure sight lines and drainage are protected. Failing to get this permit can result in a stop-work order or fines, even if the rest of the paving is done to code. The permit cost is low compared to a redo; for context, a typical resurfacing (excluding approach work) is $1,700–$5,200.
Asphalt or concrete for a New Hanover driveway? The decision between asphalt and concrete in places like New Hanover usually comes down to how the materials handle our climate and your maintenance expectations. Asphalt flexes with the ground during freeze-thaw, resisting cracking, and absorbs minor ground movement — it’s the standard choice for long driveways and those near the road where salt is used. It needs periodic sealcoating (at $225–$800 every 3–4 years), but repairs are simple. Concrete lasts longer and doesn’t need sealing, but is more expensive up front (full install at $5,700–$14,000), and is prone to damage from salt, which is common in this region. If your HOA or preference leans toward concrete, be prepared to avoid salt-based deicers and patch any early scaling quickly. For most New Hanover homes, asphalt remains the practical long-term choice unless you absolutely need a white, crisp look and are willing to manage salt exposure closely.
How long does a driveway installation take? In New Hanover, a typical asphalt driveway replacement (full depth with base work) takes 2–4 days, including excavation, base installation, and paving — but curing and setting time before driving on it stretches that closer to a week. Concrete installs require more time for curing: plan on 4–7 days before it’s ready for vehicles. If you’re only resurfacing with an asphalt overlay and the weather cooperates, the work itself might be done in a single day, with another day for curing. Keep in mind that complications — like wet silty subgrade, which can delay compaction in Montgomery County soils, or surprise base failures uncovered during removal — can add days to the project. Always ask for a written schedule. The labor cost is included in quoted prices, for example, a typical full-depth asphalt job runs $4,000–$10,500.
How much does it cost to widen a driveway? Widening a driveway in New Hanover involves more than just adding asphalt to the side — you’re looking at excavation, compacted stone base installation, and then new pavement. Because you’re working over undisturbed soil, the new section needs a base just as thick as the main drive. Joining old and new is always a weak point for future cracks, so detail matters in prep. For a typical two-car-width addition in Montgomery County, expect costs on the high end of the asphalt spectrum — usually around $4,000–$10,500 for full-depth work, depending on how much width/length you add and whether any drainage or approach permit is required. Narrow strips or minimal expansions can be less, but watch for shortcut pricing that skips stone or compaction. Always insist on written details for new base thickness and transition treatment.
What does compaction in lifts mean? “Compaction in lifts” refers to how the stone base under your driveway is installed in New Hanover and southeastern Pennsylvania. Rather than dumping all the stone at once and compacting just the top, proper installation lays the stone in layers — or “lifts” — typically 3–4 inches thick. Each lift is compacted separately by a roller or plate compactor before the next layer is added. This process removes air pockets, prevents settling, and ensures the base is dense enough to carry vehicles. In our local silty clay soils prone to holding water, layering and compacting is crucial: it resists the freeze-thaw cycles that crack or shift pavement over time. If a contractor skips lifts and only rolls the surface, you’re likely to see rutting or alligator cracking within a few winters. No matter the surface, whether it’s an asphalt install at $4,000–$10,500 or concrete, getting this right is what makes driveways last.
What is the difference between crack filling and sealcoating? Crack filling and sealcoating are related but distinct maintenance tasks for driveways in New Hanover. Crack filling targets individual openings, typically under a quarter inch wide, with hot rubberized or asphalt-based material. It’s a preventive step that blocks water from reaching and weakening the base, which is especially important in our region’s freeze-thaw climate. Sealcoating, by contrast, is a thin liquid layer applied over the whole asphalt surface to slow oxidation and improve appearance; it does not fill or repair cracks, only coats what’s there. Many homeowners mistake the black color of a fresh sealcoat for real restoration, but it won’t stop water infiltration or structural problems. Ideal maintenance is to fill cracks every fall and sealcoat every few years, spending $200–$800 on proper crack repair and $225–$800 on resealing as needed.
Does New Hanover have local asphalt plants? Yes, New Hanover and the wider Montgomery County rely on a network of asphalt and aggregate suppliers located along the I‑276 and US 202 corridors. Haul distances here are generally short, which keeps delivery charges modest and turnaround times quick. This is one reason why quotes for new asphalt or overlays ($1,700–$10,500, depending on scope) are fairly consistent in raw material pricing across local pavers. Any contractor bidding work in New Hanover should be able to tell you which plant will supply your asphalt, often by name. If a bidder can’t answer this or gives vague answers, it’s a red flag that they might be brokering jobs rather than doing the work themselves. Local sourcing is normal and helps ensure timely, hot-mix installs that survive freeze-thaw cycles better than material trucked in from much farther away.
How long should an asphalt driveway last in New Hanover? On a properly prepared base, an asphalt driveway in New Hanover should last 20 to 30 years. This assumes installation over a full-depth, well-compacted crushed stone foundation — crucial given the moderate freeze-thaw cycles and clay subgrade common in Montgomery County. Surface care does matter: sealcoating every 3–4 years for a price of $225–$800 helps slow binder aging, and filling cracks every fall prevents water infiltration that leads to base failure. If you notice rutting, edge crumbling, or puddles after storms, those are signs of base movement, which must be addressed by more than surface work. A budget install that skimps on base or compaction will show signs of failure in as little as 5–8 years, especially if exposed to heavy vehicle loads and our local winter cycles. Invest up front in prep, and your drive will long outlive the average.
