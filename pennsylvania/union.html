Permits & regulations in Pennsylvania

In Pennsylvania, driveway paving isn’t just a materials choice—it’s also a compliance checklist. If the work exceeds $500, the contractor you hire generally must be registered as a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) through the PA Attorney General’s Office. Verify registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Be especially cautious if someone canvasses door-to-door after storms (“storm chasers”); without verifiable PA HIC registration, that’s a major red flag.

Permits depend on the work and the driveway approach. Residential driveway paving typically does not require a building permit in most PA municipalities, but the driveway apron/approach where your driveway meets the public road may require a driveway access permit from PennDOT or your local municipality. Before excavation, ask your contractor to confirm whether you need a permit and which authority oversees the street connection.

Stormwater matters too. Pennsylvania’s stormwater requirements (Chapter 102) aim to prevent paving from increasing runoff onto adjacent properties or public rights-of-way. Contractors should verify grading and may propose drainage solutions (like proper slope changes) before paving.

Finally, confirm insurance and workers’ compensation. Ask for a current certificate of insurance, and verify workers’ compensation through wcais.pa.gov. To verify step-by-step in Union: (1) check the HIC listing online, (2) request and review a COI showing the correct address as the job site, (3) verify WC status at wcais.pa.gov, and (4) confirm whether any driveway access permit is needed before base work begins.

Asphalt vs. concrete — what's right for your Union driveway?

For Union, Pennsylvania homeowners, the deciding factor between asphalt and concrete is how Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle treats each material over time. Asphalt is flexible, which helps it tolerate repeated temperature swings more gracefully, especially when installed over a properly drained and compacted base. The tradeoff is maintenance: asphalt typically needs sealcoating about every 2–3 years to slow oxidation and protect the surface from water intrusion. Concrete is rigid and can last longer when installed correctly, but it can spall and crack under freeze-thaw cycling—particularly when road salt exposure and water get to the joints or edges. In Washington County, that risk is real because driveways often sit on older, pre-1980 subgrades where drainage may be inconsistent.

Since about 67.4% of Union area housing was built before 1980, many driveways are already near or beyond their expected service life. That’s why full asphalt replacement often comes up more than resurfacing: if the base has been softened by water and settlement, an overlay won’t hold. A quick price example: if resurfacing lands near $1,500–$4,500 but tests or visual signals show failed base, stepping up to a full replacement closer to $3,000–$8,000 is usually the justified choice to prevent a repeat failure cycle.

Bottom line: if your base is sound and you’re catching surface deterioration early, asphalt can be the best value. If you want maximum longevity and can address drainage and joint/edge detailing carefully, concrete can make sense—just don’t let the age of the underlying base become the weak link.

If a contractor can’t produce documentation quickly, move on.

Next, get 2–3 itemized written quotes so you can compare apples to apples. Good quotes list labor and materials separately (base material, asphalt mix, tack coat, grind/teardown, excavation, haul-off) and specify thickness and compaction steps. If an insurance claim is involved, confirm whether they document moisture issues and the existing pavement condition.

Ask about warranty terms: workmanship coverage (often for a defined period) and material or asphalt performance coverage, plus whether repairs include matching the existing lift thickness and sealcoat/tack coat details. Use a sensible payment schedule—generally never pay more than 10–15% upfront, and hold back until the job is complete and cleaned up. Finally, request a written arrival estimate and a completion window for Union’s typical 4–8 week peak-season lead times.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Union

What causes driveway cracks in Union?

Driveway cracks in Union most often trace back to what’s happening below the asphalt, not just the top surface. In Washington County, many older driveways sit on stone bases that were never designed for today’s vehicle loads. When the base is shallow or poorly drained, water collects during rain and spring thaw, then freezes during cold snaps. Pennsylvania’s freeze-thaw cycle expands the trapped moisture, leading to heaving, edge breakup, and later “alligator” cracking patterns. You’ll also see cracking worsen where the driveway transitions to the street apron—especially if the slope funnels water there. If your driveway was built before 1980 (Union’s older housing stock is significant at 67.4% of homes pre-1980, U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), it’s more likely the underlying structure needs rebuild depth rather than surface-only repairs.

If cracks are minor and you still have stable edges, crack filling and sealing can buy time. But if cracking spreads, worsens after freeze-thaw, or comes with soft spots, that’s usually a base-prep issue and points toward resurfacing or full replacement depending on what’s found.

Does homeowner's insurance cover driveway damage in Union?

Sometimes, but it depends on the cause of the damage and the type of policy you carry. In Pennsylvania, standard homeowner’s insurance commonly covers sudden, accidental events—such as a tree falling on the driveway or damage from a vehicle impact. What’s typically not covered is gradual deterioration like oxidation, normal cracking from freeze-thaw cycling, or long-term base failure. In Union, where many driveways reflect the area’s older construction history (homeowners often notice issues years after initial installation), insurers usually treat cracking and settlement as maintenance problems unless there was a clearly documented sudden event.

If you suspect coverage, document the condition with photos from multiple angles and dates, and request contractor documentation that shows the likely cause—especially if you’re dealing with water ponding, failed subgrade, or drainage issues. A contractor who can explain the progression (e.g., edge heaving after freeze-thaw, then surface cracking) helps you communicate the damage nature to your carrier.

As a reference for planning: if it turns out to be maintenance, homeowners often budget repair or maintenance work before it escalates—sealcoating can be around $200–$700 per application, while full asphalt replacement is commonly in the $3,000–$8,000 band depending on scope.

How thick should asphalt be for a PA residential driveway?

In Union, driveway asphalt thickness should be determined by your driveway’s scope—overlay versus full replacement—and the quality of the base underneath. For common Pennsylvania conditions (freeze-thaw and drainage concerns), a proper full-depth asphalt install often uses a total thickness in the range of about 3–4 inches over a well-prepared, well-drained base. If you’re doing an overlay, the typical approach is often around a 2-inch lift, but only if the existing base is structurally sound and won’t continue to heave or pump water. If the base is failing, a thin overlay can crack quickly because the movement originates below the surface.

Thickness isn’t the only requirement—proper compaction and base depth (frequently deeper than homeowners expect in older Washington County driveways) are what prevent repeat damage. In practice, we often find undersized stone bases in older pre-1980 homes, and rebuilding depth 6–12 inches before asphalt goes down can be the difference between a driveway that holds and one that fails again after the next freeze-thaw season.

Ask your contractor to write the thickness in your proposal and explain what they’ll do to fix drainage and grading. If they only talk about topping the surface, that’s a warning sign for Pennsylvania climates.

How long does driveway paving take in Union?

Time depends on whether you’re resurfacing, doing full replacement, or installing concrete. Full replacement usually includes demolition, base rebuild, grading, compaction, paving lifts, and finishing. Resurfacing can be faster because it skips full base reconstruction, provided the existing base is stable.

Sealcoating is short in duration compared to full replacement; for example, it might fall in the $200–$700 range per application. If you’re moving into full replacement territory, plan for a longer onsite window consistent with a scope closer to $3,000–$8,000.

What are the signs of a failing driveway base in Union?

Signs of a failing driveway base in Union usually show up as symptoms that return after patching, or that worsen after freeze-thaw cycles. Common indicators include soft spots or depressions that develop where water collects, frequent cracking that spreads outward from the edges, and edge breakup near the apron where the driveway meets the road. If you see pumping (small amounts of fines or asphalt movement near cracks) or noticeable settlement lines, the problem is often subgrade/base failure rather than surface deterioration. Another strong clue is drainage behavior: standing water after rainfall is a major warning because water plus freezing expands and pushes the pavement upward, causing heaving.

Many Union driveways relate to the area’s older housing stock—about 67.4% of homes were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024)—so the base may be undersized or not properly drained. In those cases, contractors may need to rebuild 6–12 inches (or more) and regrade for slope and runoff control before laying new asphalt.

These are the situations where homeowners should be cautious about choosing only sealcoating or crack filling. Sealcoating is best for preventive maintenance on structurally sound asphalt; it can slow oxidation, but it can’t correct base movement. If the base is failing, the driveway usually needs resurfacing with base evaluation or full replacement depending on what’s found during teardown.
