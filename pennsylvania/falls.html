What you’re really paying for is what happens under the driveway: base preparation, aggregate quality, and drainage.

Do not focus only on the visible surface. A cheap overlay over a failing base won’t last, no matter how neat the finish looks. Most bids come down to how much is being done where you can’t see it. Maintenance that actually works

If you want your driveway to last in Falls, crack filling is the best money you can spend—full stop. Water in the cracks is your worst enemy, especially with our freeze-thaw cycles. Once water gets to the base and freezes, it pries everything apart from below. Early crack sealing—ideally every couple of years for asphalt—shuts that down. Second most important: support at the driveway edges. Unsupported edges break down much faster. Keep the sides built up and don’t let the lawn or flower beds slowly creep away.

Water management is next. Gutters should never dump across your driveway. Standing water or downspout runoff over paved surfaces will start base erosion, then pothole formation. Avoid parking heavy vehicles or placing dumpsters for long stretches. Point loads depress and crack pavement, particularly during spring thaw or after long wet stretches.

After new asphalt, be patient: give it a full 48–72 hours in Bucks County’s typical June-September temps before parking on it, and do not turn your wheels while stationary for the first several months. Letting it cure properly helps it resist surface damage for years.

For de-icing: salt damages concrete (accelerates scaling), but it’s not great for asphalt either, because it drives melt-refreeze cycles. Consider sand, calcium magnesium acetate, or even simple shovel/scraping to clear ice. Alternatives are pricier than salt, but you’ll pay less in repairs and repaving.

Driveway permits, approaches and stormwater rules

In much of Bucks County, including Falls Township, repaving or resurfacing a driveway in its existing layout often does not require a building permit; this is a relief for most homeowners and saves a few days’ paperwork. However, a new driveway install, expansion, or changes to layout may require township review, particularly in places like Bensalem. Always check if your work crosses those thresholds before you sign a contract.

If your driveway connects to a state route, your approach—the entrance at the public road—almost always needs a separate Highway Occupancy permit, issued by PennDOT District 6, not the township. This catches people off guard, but skipping it can mean redoing the work if a state inspector shuts it down.

Adding or expanding a driveway increases impervious surface and can trigger stormwater runoff requirements, such as on-lot infiltration or additional drainage features. No permit allows you to direct new runoff onto a neighbor's property; you can be held liable for resulting damage even if you “followed the rules.” Proper drainage isn’t optional.

Pennsylvania requires Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for any job over $500. Always verify your contractor at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. They must also carry workers’ compensation insurance—check their status in the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov) before signing.

Base preparation: the part you pay for and never see

The life of a Falls driveway comes down to what’s below the surface. The assembly starts with the subgrade—whatever soil is native to your lot. In Lower Bucks, that’s often glacial till or silty clay, which needs particular attention. Over this, a contractor typically installs 4–8 inches of compacted aggregate (3/4-inch crushed stone is standard here), then the asphalt on top (usually 2–3 inches after compaction for residential driveways, though thicker for heavy vehicles).

Every layer matters. The subgrade takes the broadest load but is the most vulnerable to moisture. Water trapped here freezes, expands, and creates frost heave—lifting your driveway unevenly and cracking it from below. If drainage isn’t handled or the base isn’t thick enough for Falls’s 30-inch frost depth, expect early failure, no matter how good the asphalt or concrete on top looks.

This is where cheap contractors cut corners: they skip real excavation, lay thin base, or avoid compacting in proper lifts. It’s all invisible when you cut the check, but in year three the cracks and sunken patches start to show. Ask for the following in writing: 6 inches minimum compacted aggregate base over glacial till or clay, 2–3 inches compacted asphalt for residential traffic, and that all layers are machine compacted in lifts—not “just rolled once.” Demand proof of materials delivered (ticket from the quarry or plant) and basic compaction checks. If a quote is much lower than the others, this is almost always why.

When paving can actually be done in Falls

Seasonality is a technical constraint, not just a preference, in Falls: you can’t pave well here except in the right weather. Hot-mix asphalt must go down when air and ground temps are well above freezing—ideally 50°F and rising. In Bucks County, asphalt plants shut down for winter, generally from mid-November through March, so if someone offers to pave for you in January, run the other way. Concrete needs a few days of consistent above-freezing temps to cure properly; rushing it in the wet, cold shoulder months risks scaling and cracking.

This climate’s winter design temp is 14°F and frost depth is 30 inches, meaning work typically runs late March through early November. Early spring and late fall see less demand and may get you more attention from crews, but weather can be unpredictable—sunny one day, sleet the next. Most driveways are installed May through September, when conditions are reliable and plants are fully stocked with local aggregate from Delaware Valley quarries. During this summer peak, expect lead times to stretch and prices to be on the higher side.

A reputable Falls contractor will never rush a pour or lay asphalt when temperatures are on the edge. If a crew suggests paving outside these windows “for a discount,” there’s usually a quality trade-off you’ll regret. Use the off-peak for price and the peak for certainty—don’t let anyone make you think seasonality is up for debate here.

Asphalt or concrete for a Falls driveway?

In Falls, the freeze-thaw cycles drive the decision more than anything else. Asphalt is flexible: it handles our climate’s movement by flexing instead of cracking, so surface damage can be sealed or patched. Concrete wins on lifespan (30–50 years vs. asphalt’s 20–30), but it is rigid and unforgiving when the silty clay or glacial till underneath shifts—even more so if de-icing salt is used. Salt is a huge issue here, spalling and flaking concrete rapidly if ignored. If you go concrete, you must avoid salt-based ice melts—most people forget, and the driveway pays the price.

Upfront cost is higher for concrete, and while its maintenance is low, repairs are expensive and often obvious. Asphalt requires periodic sealcoating (every 3–4 years) and patching but copes better when the ground inevitably flexes. For most single-family homes in Falls, asphalt is the pragmatic winner unless you absolutely commit to proper concrete care—and even then, remember that older base soils may have never been prepped for a rigid surface.

Pavers are good for those with budget and a taste for aesthetics—they settle but don’t crack, and individual units can be replaced. Gravel stays the lowest cost, especially for long rural runs, and handles drainage exceptionally here.

Falls's ground and what it means for paving

The underlying ground in Falls, especially in the neighborhoods east of Levittown and south toward the river, is mainly glacial till and silty clay. This matters more than most people realize: silty clay holds moisture, so it is slow to drain and highly susceptible to frost heave in our 30-inch frost depth. Glacial till is a mixed bag—stones drain, but fine particles can trap water pockets. Both soil types demand a thicker, well-compacted aggregate base (at least 6 inches), and an absolute focus on drainage—for both asphalt and concrete driveways.

Many homes here, especially those built in the 1950s and 1960s Levittown-era tracts, still have their original driveways or thin overlays on inadequate base. Back then, base preparation was often only nominal, with builders betting on fast completion over long-term performance. If your driveway sits unevenly, cracks every spring, or shows settled patches near downspouts, it’s nearly always a subgrade or drainage issue rooted in these soils. One county west, where the soil is more sandy, the rules would change. In Falls, ignoring the silty clay or glacial till leads to repeating the cycle of replacement.

Frequently asked questions — driveway paving in Falls

How much does it cost to widen a driveway?

Widening a driveway in Falls usually involves excavation, new stone base, and either new asphalt or concrete over the added area. The cost depends on how much you’re adding—many homeowners are looking to widen 2–5 feet along one side for extra parking or easier turns. For asphalt, expect to pay within the $3,800–$9,700 range for a full replacement if the entire drive is being redone. If you’re only widening a section but matching to older pavement, cost per square foot rises because of blending and edge prep, often from $1,600–$4,900 for overlays or add-ons. Remember that adding width often triggers new stormwater requirements with the township, especially since silty clay soils in Falls mean runoff is a bigger concern than in sandy areas. Any reputable Falls paving contractor should spell out the price and permitting process in your written estimate.

Can asphalt be laid in cold weather?

No, not successfully—not in Falls. Asphalt needs to be installed and compacted while it’s hot, otherwise it won’t bond or seal properly. In Bucks County, the winter design temperature is 14°F, and with local plants closing from November through March, crews simply don’t have access to fresh hot-mix. If a paving contractor in Falls is willing to lay asphalt while it’s cold (below 45–50°F), the surface will be porous, never fully cure, and fail in a few short winters. Always plan for work between late March and early November. Pricing for a full-depth asphalt driveway, installed at the right time, runs from $3,800–$9,700. It’s never worth trying to save a buck by pushing the season—wait until the weather and materials are right.

Is gravel a cheaper option in Falls?

Yes, gravel driveways remain the most cost-effective solution in Falls, especially for long runs or rural lots. The price for a fresh gravel driveway typically falls between $900–$3,500, far lower than asphalt or concrete. That said, our local glacial till and silty clay soils mean you still need a properly graded base to prevent ruts and standing water, or you’ll be paying for frequent top-dressing and grading. Gravel also excels with drainage—ideal in an area prone to wet springs and mild flooding near the river—but does demand annual attention. For short residential drives within subdivisions, gravel is rare due to aesthetics and local zoning, but as a budget and drainage alternative, it’s hard to beat for Falls homeowners.

Are door-to-door pavers a problem in Falls?

Yes, and you should be cautious. Door-to-door asphalt or sealcoating contractors are common in Falls, especially in older neighborhoods needing frequent driveway work. Many offer what sounds like a great deal, sometimes citing “leftover material from a nearby job.” The problem is quality—and accountability. Pennsylvania requires HIC registration for all jobs over $500, and you can verify a contractor at hic.attorneygeneral.gov. Many traveling outfits are unregistered and uninsured, and the cost of a quick patch or thin overlay—often between $1,600–$4,900—may seem reasonable but fails quickly. Stick with locally established crews who will still be around for warranty calls. If something feels rushed or too cheap, it almost always is. In the end, rebuilding a failed driveway from a botched job costs far more.

How thick should asphalt be for a residential driveway in Falls?

This is important given Falls’s cold winters and glacial till or clay base. For residential use (cars and light trucks), the standard is 2–3 inches of compacted hot-mix asphalt over a minimum 6-inch, well-compacted crushed stone base. Thicker asphalt—closer to 3 inches—is recommended if you own heavy vehicles or have poor-draining soils. Some contractors offer thinner surfaces at a discount, but these rarely hold up past a few freeze-thaw cycles. The cost for a properly built driveway in Falls runs $3,800–$9,700 in 2026. Always ask your contractor for the specs in writing: total asphalt after compaction and depth of the stone base, matched to your soil type.

How do I verify a paving contractor in Pennsylvania?

In Pennsylvania, any paving contractor working on jobs over $500 must be registered with the Attorney General’s office as a Home Improvement Contractor. You can—and should—verify this at hic.attorneygeneral.gov before signing any contract. You can also check workers’ compensation status through the WCAIS system (wcais.pa.gov). In Falls, because most driveways require substantial investment ($3,800–$9,700 for a full replacement), vetting your contractor is the best way to protect your money and rights under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act. Never accept a business card or handshake as proof; always search the official state registry or ask for HIC documentation and current insurance certificates. A legitimate contractor won’t hesitate to provide this proof up front.

What causes concrete to spall or flake in Falls?

In Falls, concrete driveways often suffer from spalling (surface flaking) and scaling because of our moderate freeze-thaw cycles combined with regular de-icing salt use. Silty clay underneath holds water—when it freezes, the ground pushes moisture up into the slab. Salts, especially from commercial de-icers or road plows, attack the concrete’s surface, making it pit and flake within a few winters. Even a well-poured drive can fail early if salt use isn’t strictly limited. It’s not the concrete’s fault; it’s climate and maintenance. If you’re paying $5,400–$13,000 for a new concrete drive, make a habit of using sand or ice melt alternatives and sealing the surface every few years. Proper drainage and downspout control can help, but salt is the key issue.

Are pavers worth the cost in southeastern Pennsylvania?

Pavers carry a higher upfront cost—typical Falls installations run from $8,600–$13,000—but have a few unique benefits for this county’s climate and soil. Pavers flex and settle gradually with the glacial till and silty clay soils beneath, rather than cracking like concrete. Individual bricks can be lifted and reset if uneven spots appear after a rough winter. Upkeep is moderate: occasional sweeping, joint sand renewal, and sealing to fight weeds or shifting. If you value aesthetics and the ability to repair a small area without patch marks, pavers are worth considering, especially where drainage or tree roots might undermine solid pavement. Be clear about routine maintenance and weed control, though—pavers aren’t set-and-forget, but they are durable for decades if you keep up with care.

When can I seal a brand new asphalt driveway?

After installing new asphalt in Falls, you need to wait before sealing. The fresh asphalt needs to cure and release oils, especially in Bucks County’s humid summers. The standard recommendation is to wait at least 90 days, preferably through one full hot summer if possible, before applying a sealcoat. Sealing too soon can trap oils and cause the surface to remain soft and scuff or tear. With driveways running $3,800–$9,700 for a full build, don’t take shortcuts—use that first summer to let the driveway fully harden. After that, plan to reseal every 3–4 years to protect the surface and get the most value from your investment. Never let any contractor convince you to seal fresh asphalt in the same week as paving.
